"Walk with me," said Lord Alaric, and gave his own horse-boy a look that sent him away with Orryn's bridle.

He did not go in. He went across the yard to the well, where the cobbles were wide and open and nobody could stand near without being seen from the keep, and there he stopped, and put one boot on the stone rim, and waited. Orryn understood that this was how a man chose a place to hear a thing he did not want overheard, and his heart began to beat rather fast.

"Master Fallow," said Lord Alaric. "Begin with him."

So Orryn did. He told of the white step and the glass windows and the fowl, of the six rings and the small bright eyes, and the loud warm voice that had called him young Master Grandison. He told how Dunstan Fallow had asked, kindly, about the east orchard, and what he himself had answered.

"I said it was mended, my lord. And that you were well."

"And Master Fallow wrote it down."

"Yes, my lord. In a little book. He was very gentle about it."

"He is always gentle about it." The hazel eyes did not leave the keep wall. "You said mended. I will tell you why I should rather you had not. It is a word for a thing that is finished, and the east block is not finished, and will not be before spring. A man who hears mended and learns later that it was not will remember who told him. Say in hand. It means as much and promises nothing." He considered. "Though I take your meaning, and I think you meant it well. What else?"

"His wife is Mistress Alis, my lord. She was kind. And her brother runs the mill, Hob Marsh, and he says very little."

"The lower mill. That is mine, and Fallow holds it of me." Lord Alaric said it without any weight at all. "He holds another besides. He mended the Alder Mill for House Haye, and works it on a long lease, and I set my seal to the award that gave it to Haye. A man with his money in two houses likes to know which of them is sound. That is why he asks after my orchards, Orryn. It is not malice. It is bookkeeping."

Orryn thought of Baldric Haye's patched tunic, kept so clean, and said nothing, and Lord Alaric did not seem to expect it.

He told of the road and the causeway, and the grey hound over the gate, and Ser Marlon's hand on Osric's head. He gave the lord Lady Edda's greeting. He told of the long grace, and passed quickly over Merel and the darn, because it was nothing, and his ears went warm. Then he said, "Ser Marlon said at supper that Septa Lenora had written him of trouble in the pear yard."

"She would have." Lord Alaric did not seem surprised. "She writes to him every week. She has done so for years, and I do not suppose she will stop on my account. What did you say?"

"That it was mended." Orryn heard it as he said it. "In hand, I mean, my lord."

"Ser Marlon said something to that."

"He said it was a good word to carry into a house. And to keep hold of it."

There was the very least movement at the corner of the lord's mouth. "Did he. Then you have been told something by a better teacher than I." The boot came down off the rim. "And that is all? The two tables? Osric?"

It was no more than a pause. It was the pause he had made in the lane, with the word half-formed in his mouth. Orryn felt it in the soles of his feet.

"He was courteous, my lord," said Orryn. "He is always that."

It was true. Every word of it was true, and he had said it with his eyes on Lord Alaric's face, and for the space of a breath he thought he would be asked again. The hazel eyes rested on him, level, giving nothing. A pear tree somewhere beyond the wall shed a leaf. Then the lord said, in the same even voice, "You are not obliged to tell me everything you hear, Orryn. A squire who did so would be of no use to anyone. You are obliged never to lie to me, and I do not think you have." He paused. "I should be sorry to find I was wrong."

"Yes, my lord."

"Is there something you wish to ask?"

There was, and it was the thing he had carried all the way from the gate of Dunmere Hall. "When we left, my lord. Ser Marlon said to tell you that Dunmere was yours to command." He heard how it sounded and went on regardless. "He said it as if he had said it before. And he looked at Osric, and said nothing to him. I did not know what it meant."

Lord Alaric was silent for some time.

"It is a courtesy," he said at last. "A form of words, between a lord and a man who holds of him. Marlon Dunmere has said it to me at my father's table and at my own." He looked out across the yard, to where Osric was just disappearing into the squires' door with his back very straight. "But Ser Marlon is a man who says less than he means, as a rule, and not more. In the year of Redgrass he rode for the king with his own men, when not every knight in this country did. He has never once spoken of it to me. He has never once forgotten it, and neither have I. So when he says that his house is mine to command, I have learned to hear the rest of it. A man does not offer what he is not prepared to be asked to repay. He is telling me that he will one day ask me for something, and that I am to remember who spoke first, and who stood where."

"Who stood where, my lord?"

"You will hear that told in the squires' hall, and told differently by every boy whose father stood on the other side of it." It was said quite evenly. "Listen. Do not repeat it. And do not judge by the telling, for you will not hear it fairly from any of them."

"What will Ser Marlon ask, my lord?"

"I do not know." He said it plainly. "I have a notion, and it may be wrong, and I will not put a wrong notion in a boy's head to carry about a keep." The hazel eyes came back to him. "Which is, I think, the whole of what I wished you to learn from your two tables. Go and wash. Eat. And Orryn."

"My lord?"

"When the thing you are not telling me becomes a thing you should tell me, you will know. Come then. Not before, and not after."

He went across the yard towards the keep with his hands behind him, and did not look back. Orryn stood by the well a long moment with the promise still in his chest, and found that it was a good deal heavier than it had been that morning, and in a different place.
