Lord Alaric drank three cups at the buttery hatch with his head thrown back, then put his whole head under the yard pump and came up blowing like a horse. A quarter-hour later he was in the saddle of a rangy bay with a groom's cloth for a saddlecloth, and Orryn was on the grey hack he usually rode, and Alyn Merryweather was on the bench of the cart behind them, with two grooms in the bed among nine empty barrels that knocked together over every rut like a very hollow drum.

"Ser Lucan is a good knight," said Lord Alaric, as if Orryn had argued with him. "I would trust him with a gate. I would not trust him with a pot of broth. He would order it to cool." He dug in his saddlebag and tossed back an apple, which Orryn caught against his chest by luck. "Eat that. Did you eat today?"

"Bread, my lord."

"Then half of what ails you is that. Osric will be in the cold cellar by supper telling the barrels about honour, and I shall have to hear it tomorrow. Eat."

Orryn ate. It was warm from the saddlebag and mealy and the best thing he had ever put in his mouth.

The brook ran south of Grassfield through a fold in the hills, and he had seen it in spring come over the stepping-stones in a brown rush that took a goose with it. Now the stones stood dry on their own, grey as old bones, with a skin of green dried on the bed, and at every bend there was a pool the size of a cart-bed where the cattle stood in a patient row, up to the hocks, and did not even switch their tails. The willows had gone the colour of straw. Lord Alaric looked at none of it. He rode with the reins in one hand and the other on his thigh, humming something tuneless, and it came to Orryn that he was enjoying himself.

The cart caught them on a flat stretch, and Alyn brought it alongside, shouting over the barrels. "Five barrels each and we're heroes. Ten and they'll make a song." He had a wet rag on his head and the sun in his eyes, and his whole face, squinting, was as open as a dog's. Orryn looked at the road. "Don't look like that, Grandview. Fenwick's forgotten it already. He has the memory of a trout. It's Ser Tobin who'll hold it, and he'll hold it till he finds the next thing."

"It was a rotten thing to do," Orryn said, low, so the lord ahead would not hear.

"Aye. That's why it was funny." Alyn grinned, and then, because Orryn did not, he flapped a hand at him. "You didn't say anything. You stood there with your mouth shut like you'd swallowed a spoon." It had not sounded like praise, and Orryn did not take it as any, and neither of them could find anything to say after that, and the barrels went on knocking.

*

The lower mill stood where the brook dropped out of the hills into the flat, a long low house of grey stone with the wheel under one end, and Orryn had been through it once, on the Fallow visit, when it had been dusty with flour and loud. Now the wheel stood still. Above it the pond lay like a sheet of beaten pewter, flat and green at the edges, held back by an earth dam with a timber frame set in the middle of it, and below the dam the brook-bed was bare as a road.

Hob Marsh, the miller, was at the dam end with his cap in both fists. He was a broad man with flour in the creases of his neck, and he looked like someone who had been ready for this visit since morning. His two sons stood behind him: Wat, near sixteen, wide as a door, and Jory, eleven or twelve, all knees and elbows and eyes.

"My lord."

"Hob. I walked up your brook this morning and it is a ditch. Your pond is full."

"It is, my lord. Master Fallow said to hold it." Hob turned the cap a quarter-turn. "The first barley's in. We've three days' grinding bespoke and I can't grind without a head of water."

"You've been grinding on that head since midsummer. Where's the key?"

Orryn saw it then: the stop in the timber frame was chained and the chain went through an iron staple and a padlock as big as a man's fist. "Master Fallow has it," said Wat, who was not a boy to say anything he was not asked, and from down the road came the clop of a cob, and a boy trotting after with a satchel.

Master Dunstan Fallow got down at the cart with his round belly first. He was Piers's father, and he held the lower mill of Lord Alaric, and the long lease on another mill, that belonged to House Haye, and he had six rings on his hands and a calfskin book in his belt. Orryn had heard it said that he wrote down names, and had watched him do it at his own table, asking about Dunmere. "My lord," he said, blowing. "I had your message at noon. I thought you might wish to speak before the lock came off."

"I wish the lock off," said Lord Alaric. "I gave you a mill, Dunstan, and a pond to run it. I did not give you the brook."

"The pond is within the grant, my lord."

"I know what is in the grant. I sealed it." He swung down, and his boots hit the dust and raised it. "There are a hundred and forty head below this dam, and Haye's meadow below that, and my yard well is down two hands. And there's a squire in my cellar this afternoon who stopped sweating, and I do not mean to explain it to Ser Marlon with your barley."

"The mill stops, then," said Dunstan. "And the third of Grassy Vale that sends its corn to me grinds nothing till rain."

"Half, then." Lord Alaric was taking his boots off, one at a time, hopping. "The plank up at dusk and down at dawn, so my yard has its water in the cool. By day you grind what the pond holds. Hob keeps the stop. Not you."

Dunstan's lips moved over the sum. "That halves the toll."

"Then it halves the toll."

Dunstan took the key from his belt, and, after a moment, bowed, an entirely civil bow, and gave it to the miller and not to the lord. Hob unlocked the chain with a small silent look at the water. The chain came away. The stop did not move. The rack-and-pawl was rusted solid, and the oak peg through it had swollen in the damp, and Wat and Hob both got their shoulders to the beam and put their feet in the dust and got nothing but a groan from the timber.

"Mallet," said Lord Alaric, and walked into the pond.

He went in to the thigh, barefoot, in his breeches, with the mud taking him to the shin at every step and a smell coming off it like a stale cellar. Orryn watched him from the bank. He did not tell Orryn to come. Orryn found he had left the grey with a groom and gone anyway, and was in to the knee beside the lord before he had thought about it, and the water was warm as bathwater and green with scum. Lord Alaric knocked the peg once, twice, with the flat of the mallet, then looked at Orryn, hair stuck to his forehead. "Take the beam. Pull on three."

"Aye, my lord."

"One," said Lord Alaric, and hit the peg, and the peg went with a crack like a broken bone, and the beam came up out of its slot all at once, long before three, and Orryn, who had been braced for it, sat down in the pond.

The plank lifted. For a moment nothing happened. Then the brook began to go through the cut under the frame, first a seep, then a brown rope, then a brown shoulder of water that went down the bed with a sound Orryn had no word for, and Jory shrieked and ran along the bank after it with his arms out. Down the slope the cattle raised their heads. Alyn stood in the cart and whooped. Somebody laughed. It was Lord Alaric, thigh-deep, with mud to the hip and a skinned thumb, laughing with his head back, and Orryn, who was sitting in the pond with the green running off his chin, realised that he was laughing too, and could not stop.

It was a good while before he noticed Dunstan Fallow standing at the water's edge, dry and stout in the sun, with the calfskin book open on his belly. The pen was moving. "Squire Orryn," said Dunstan, pleasantly, not looking up. "You were at my table not a month gone, I think. Is my Piers well?"

Orryn was wading out, with his shirt clinging. "He's burnt, Master Fallow. He fought me to a draw at noon. I don't think either of us hit anything."

"And Ser Wyman. Is he kind to the boy?"

He knew what the gossip in the squires' hall said about that, and he did not want to say it in front of Hob Marsh, or at all. "He taught us the ring," Orryn said. "He gave me his old lance. I don't—" He looked for something true. "I think he's better with the horses than with us."

"Dunstan," said Lord Alaric, from the pond, not turning round. He was working the mud off his shins with his hands. "You are paying Wyman to be kind to your son. Ask him if you got your coin's worth. He's fourteen." Behind the dam, Hob Marsh looked hard at the water. Dunstan Fallow only nodded, with perfect composure, and wrote a line, and Orryn could not tell whether he was writing it down or only making it look so.

The barrels were the last thing. Orryn, climbing out, waved at the grooms to fill them from the pond, and Hob said without heat, "Not for drinking, young ser. It's gone green. A horse can have it. A man'll be shitting for a week." So the grooms filled the nine barrels for the horses, and the lord, dripping, put his boots on over wet feet and said the brook would run clear by morning once the mud had gone through. Alyn drove the cart home in the gold light with the wet barrels slopping, singing something with a great many verses and no tune, and his voice carried back along the road.

*

They rode home with the sun a red coin behind the hills and the heat still lying on the road. Lord Alaric had his boots strapped to the saddle after all, and rode barefoot with his shirt drying to a stiff grey, and a blister coming up on his thumb that he looked at and sucked and looked at again.

"Haye will hear by morning that his meadow is wet," he said. "He will say he asked for it. Dunstan will send me an account of the afternoon. It'll be very good, the whole thing set out by the hour, with the names. He writes everything down. It's the least troublesome thing about him." He looked sideways. "Did you like it?"

Orryn considered. "I liked the part when it came through."

"That's the smallest part of it. Most of it is somebody has the water, and somebody wants it, and you go and stand in it." He said it without much feeling. Then, as the first lamp showed in the gatehouse: "I wanted somebody along who wasn't Verrall. Verrall talks."

Orryn did not know what to say to that, and said nothing, and rode on, sticky and sun-struck and not unhappy.

There was a figure waiting under the arch, on the step by the well, with a folded paper in one hand. It was Baldric Haye, Ser Bertram's squire, whose father's meadow lay below the dam. He got up when he saw them, and he did not come forward, and his face in the lamplight was set like a man who has been practising a speech and has lost the beginning of it.

What does Orryn do?

1. Go to Baldric at once. The paper is the lease he spoke of, and he has been waiting for hours.

2. See to the horse and the cart first, then go to Osric in the cellar before anything else.

3. Find Perkin and put right the matter of the boots, whatever it costs with Alyn.

4. Something else of your own.
