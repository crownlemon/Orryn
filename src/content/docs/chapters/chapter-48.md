Bitterbridge, 208 AC

Matthos had been explaining Highgarden to Orryn for most of the morning, although he had never been there.

“Their practice yard is bigger than our whole outer ward,” he said. “Ser Moryn has knights from the Arbor and Oldtown. They train with proper lances, not the things Ser Everard gives us.”

Orryn adjusted the strap on the sumpter's load.

“Ser Everard gives us proper lances.”

“They're old.”

“They're ash.”

“I know what they're made of.”

Matthos had acquired a green riding cloak for the journey. He wore it draped over one shoulder so that the little Meadows eagle worked on the fastening would show. He had also persuaded his father to let him carry a sword, which was rather too long for him and struck the horse's flank when he turned.

“Anyway,” he said, “I'll be riding in Ser Moryn's company, and you'll be back at Grassfield with the others.”

“Perhaps he won't take you.”

Matthos looked offended.

“Father says he will.”

“Lord Alaric says you're to be presented to him.”

Matthos dug his heel into the grey pony's side and rode ahead, the sword beating against his saddle.

Orryn had regretted speaking before the boy was even out of hearing. He would have to spend another two days with him.

They had left Grassfield at sunrise: Lord Alaric, his son, Orryn, Ser Bertram Corliss, two mounted men and a groom leading the sumpter. There had been a short argument at the gate because Matthos wanted his new saddle brought on the spare horse in case the one beneath him grew uncomfortable. Alaric had told the groom to leave it. Matthos had then proposed that they exchange saddles at the first halt, and Alaric told him he was welcome to walk if his present one did not please him.

He had said little about the saddle since.

Orryn rode the chestnut gelding from Grassfield. It had stopped to make water within sight of the gate and twice more before noon. He was beginning to know when to expect it.

Alyn would have found that funny. The thought came when Orryn was tightening the load, and he put it away before Matthos could see any change in his face.

The road was broad enough for two wagons to pass where it ran across the open fields, but narrowed through little belts of trees that gave them a welcome hour of shade. Heat lay over the cut grass. The ditches were low and in places dry, with pale mud cracked in shapes like broken tiles. At one village a woman was drawing water from a well so deep that Orryn could hear the bucket strike the sides twice before it reached the bottom.

Alaric stopped there to let the horses drink. He paid for a second bucket when the first came up brown.

“You've enough water?” he asked Orryn.

“Yes, my lord.”

“Then stop counting how many mouthfuls your cousin takes. He can answer for his own thirst.”

Orryn felt his ears grow warm. He had not realised Alaric was watching.

Matthos looked delighted.

“My father says you'll be good at counting when you're old, Grandison.”

“He's good at it now,” Alaric said. “The trouble is he sometimes counts the wrong things.”

Matthos's delight diminished. He took another drink and spilled some down his new cloak.

By the second afternoon they were passing through a country of larger fields and more traffic. The road joined the Roseroad, and the flow of carts, riders, sheep and dusty walkers increased. At a roadside shrine a barefoot septon had set out a jar to collect offerings for travellers who had no food. Matthos tossed in a copper and made certain Orryn had seen him do it.

That night they slept at an inn where the loft was hot and full of flies. Alaric had a chamber below, but the boys shared their sleeping space with Ser Bertram and the two mounted men. Matthos complained about the straw until Ser Bertram asked whether he would prefer to lie on the floorboards.

He had scarcely stopped complaining before he fell asleep.

Orryn lay awake for a time listening to the horses stamp in the yard. He had ridden away from Grandview almost a year earlier with far more certainty that he knew what it meant to serve a lord. He had imagined he would learn to fight, manage horses and earn his spurs, as any boy of his birth ought to do.

Since then he had learned how easily a girth rubbed a sore into a horse, how much a guest cost in candles, and how a lord might make an enemy by deciding whose water could run through a mill sluice.

He was still no better with a sword than he had hoped to be when he first arrived.

He tried to remember whether he had ever actually told his father he wanted to be a knight.

He supposed he must have. What else would he have said?

*

Bitterbridge announced itself by the noise before Orryn saw the town.

They were still among the outer fields when a long train of mule carts came toward them, the animals burdened with sacks of grain. A boy walking beside the first cart blew a horn to move a herd of pigs from the road. Farther on a pair of cattle drovers were arguing with a man who wanted to collect some fee before allowing them past.

By the time the party reached the town, Orryn could smell tallow, roasting onions and the river. Stalls stood shoulder to shoulder along the road. The awnings were dusty red and green; beneath them lay bolts of cloth, heaps of turnips, wooden cups and iron nails. A woman sold hot bread from a board laid across two barrels, and Matthos asked whether her loaves were as good as those made at Highgarden.

“She wouldn't know, my lord,” Orryn said.

“I wasn't asking you.”

Alaric, riding ahead, turned his head.

“Then ask a question she can answer.”

Matthos sulked until they reached the bridge.

The Mander was broad there, a great brown-green sweep of water moving between banks that were crowded with houses and store sheds. Boats lay moored below the crossing. From the bridge Orryn saw men working at a wharf, passing baskets hand to hand while a clerk beneath a striped shade shouted at them to keep the two piles separate. The current seemed slow until he watched a little branch pass beneath them and disappear against a pier.

He had never seen so much water gathered in one place.

The castle stood on the far side, above the road. It was a small, practical-looking seat behind walls of pale stone, not much grander than Grassfield in size, though the town and bridge made it seem the centre of a much larger world. White banners hung from its gate, each bearing a yellow centaur with a drawn bow.

Ser Bertram pointed at one with a gauntleted hand.

“Caswell. Defender of the Fords.”

“I know,” Matthos said.

“You might try letting someone finish telling you a thing,” Alaric answered.

Orryn watched the centaur snap in the river wind. The creature was aiming its bow at nothing Orryn could see.

A groom in the gatehouse recognised Alaric's colours, and they were admitted without waiting among the carts. Orryn was grateful to dismount. The gelding had rubbed one knee against the saddle, and his own legs felt as though the muscles had been pulled apart and put back badly.

The courtyard held a dozen unfamiliar horses and two carts piled with straw. An old hound slept beneath a bench despite the servants stepping around it. A knight in an elaborate blue cloak was berating a groom over a missing glove, but when he saw the newcomers he broke off, smiled and came toward them.

“Cousin Alaric!”

“Cleyton.”

Ser Cleyton Caswell was fair and slender, with a carefully trimmed beard and a clasp that looked heavy enough to drag his cloak from his shoulders. He embraced Alaric with great warmth, then bowed to Matthos.

“And this must be the young lord of Grassfield.”

“Matthos Meadows,” the boy announced.

“Growing into your father's face already, I see.”

Matthos looked pleased.

Cleyton turned to Orryn.

“And you?”

“Orryn Grandison, ser. Lord Alaric's squire.”

“Of Grandview? Then you're kin as well. Welcome to Bitterbridge.”

He gave Orryn a quick smile and turned back to Alaric, already asking after Lady Jeyne and the journey.

A servant led them through a passage into the small hall. It was crowded with benches, hunting spears and trophies whose horns were too large for the room. A painted centaur hung above the hearth, its forelegs raised as though preparing to leap out of the cloth.

Lord Armond Caswell came from a side chamber before they had been announced.

He was a large man with white moustaches, a red nose and an old scar on his forearm. He moved briskly for his size and grasped Alaric so hard by both shoulders that Orryn thought his lord might stumble.

“Alaric! At last. I told my lady wife you'd be here before supper.”

“Then I hope you've not been keeping the cook idle on my account.”

“Never. We have a cook who'd roast me before she'd let the fire go cold.”

He laughed, then turned to Matthos.

“By the Seven. Jeyne's boy.”

Matthos bowed.

“My lord grandfather.”

“Come here. Let me look at you.”

Armond held the boy at arm's length.

“Too much of your father about the eyes. Never mind. How old now?”

“Thirteen, my lord.”

“Old enough for Highgarden, they say.”

“Yes, my lord. I'm to serve Ser Moryn Tyrell.”

“Are you indeed?”

Matthos took a breath, as though prepared to explain the whole matter again. Armond forestalled him with a clap on the shoulder.

“Good. We'll make a knight of you yet.”

He saw Orryn waiting behind the others.

“And who's this one?”

Orryn bowed.

“Orryn Grandison, my lord.”

“Grandison? A long way from home, lad.”

“My lord.”

“Your grandsire was Gawen of Grandview, wasn't he?”

“Yes.”

“A good man, as I remember. Kept his own counsel too much, but a good man.”

Orryn did not know what to say. Armond spared him the trouble by calling for wine.

A woman entered from the passage behind him.

“The wine is waiting, Armond. It has been waiting since the horses came through the gate.”

Her voice was pleasant, though it made the lord of Bitterbridge stop speaking at once.

She was slight and brisk in a gown of grey-green wool, with sandy hair drawn neatly back. There were a few grey strands near her temples and a composed, rather fixed smile. Orryn recognised the Tyrell rose picked out in gold thread at her sleeve.

“Lady Brenna,” Alaric said, bowing.

“Lord Alaric. You must be tired. I hope the rooms have been made ready to your liking.”

“I've not seen them yet.”

“Then you must tell me when you do.”

She offered her hand to Matthos, who kissed it rather elaborately.

“Your mother's last visit was too brief,” she said. “I trust she is well.”

“She is, my lady.”

“And your sisters?”

“Also well.”

“I am glad.”

Her gaze moved to Orryn. Alaric supplied his name, and she greeted him courteously enough.

Orryn had known for years that Lady Brenna was scarcely older than Lady Jeyne. His father had explained it once, when listing the family connections that would matter during his service in the Reach: Armond's first wife had died, he had married a young daughter of Highgarden, and that lady was the mother of his heir. Orryn could repeat the relationship without difficulty.

Still, he had expected something different. Brenna was Jeyne's stepmother, and whenever Jeyne mentioned her at Grassfield it was in a voice Orryn associated with older women who had earned the right to find fault. Here she stood beside Armond, who might have passed for her father, and looked barely older than Alaric himself. She was not even much older than Jeyne.

Orryn felt foolish for being surprised. He had known the numbers all along. He simply had not known what they looked like together.

Brenna noticed him looking and raised one eyebrow.

“Is there something amiss with your room, Master Grandison? You haven't seen it yet, either.”

“No, my lady.”

“Then perhaps we'd better show you.”

She led them away before Armond could offer another cup.

*

The chambers were up a narrow stair above the gatehouse passage. Alaric and Matthos were to have the larger one, with a curtained bed and a window toward the river. Orryn had a pallet in a smaller room with Ser Bertram and a page belonging to the Caswells. The page had already left his shoes beneath the bed and a wooden flute upon the sill.

Matthos looked at the curtained bed.

“Is this where Father always sleeps?”

“When he's here,” said the woman showing them in.

“I expect we'll have better at Highgarden.”

She looked at the cushions she had just straightened.

“Perhaps you will, my lord.”

Orryn could almost hear the answer she had not given.

He took their riding things down to the yard, where the Caswell grooms were seeing to the horses. The chestnut gelding was already eating from a manger. Orryn lifted its forefoot to check the shoe that had worried him on the second day. A boy on the other side of the stall said, “If you pull it off now, my father will have the smith put it back on and charge your lord for it.”

Orryn put the foot down.

The speaker was thin and pale, perhaps a year older than he was, with sandy hair that fell into his eyes and a long nose reddened by the sun. His doublet was of very fine blue wool, though it had a smear of dust on one sleeve. He carried a practice shield in one hand and a piece of bread in the other.

“Are you a groom?” Orryn asked.

The boy looked down at his clothes.

“Not yet. Mother says there's no telling what I'll become, though.”

Orryn stared.

The boy grinned.

“Joffrey Caswell.”

Orryn bowed hurriedly.

“My lord.”

“Don't do that. You'll drop your horse.”

It was difficult to know what to say to a boy who was heir to the castle and stood in its stables eating bread.

“I'm Orryn Grandison,” he said.

“I know. Cleyton told me. He thinks your horse has a bad foot.”

“That's my other horse. He's at Grassfield.”

“Ah. Then he has two of them confused. That's Cleyton.”

Joffrey took another bite.

“You're to Highgarden, aren't you?”

“With Lord Alaric.”

“And his son?”

“Matthos. He's to serve Ser Moryn, if Ser Moryn will have him.”

“Then I expect he's telling everybody he's already been chosen.”

Orryn looked at him.

“He is.”

“Good. We won't have to discover whether we like him. He'll tell us that as well.”

Joffrey pushed the rest of his bread into his mouth, set down his shield and tried to help Orryn with the girth. He caught the strap through the wrong loop. Orryn showed him the right one.

“Much obliged,” Joffrey said. “I'm meant to know things like that.”

“You're a squire?”

“Cleyton's.”

Orryn thought of the knight in the blue cloak.

“Ser Cleyton is your master?”

“When he remembers. My father says he's teaching me courtesy, and Cleyton says I'm teaching him patience. We both say whatever sounds agreeable.”

A man called from across the yard.

“Joffrey! Where's that shield?”

Joffrey closed his eyes.

“Speaking of patience.”

Ser Cleyton emerged from the shade of the passage, no longer wearing his fine cloak. His training doublet was stitched with thick leather at the shoulders. A blunted sword hung from his hand.

“Come along. Your father wants you to show our visitors the yard.”

“What if our visitors would rather sit down?”

“Then we'll sit down afterward.”

Joffrey looked at Orryn.

“Will you come? You'll make it less awful.”

Orryn had scarcely had time to say yes when Matthos appeared from the stair carrying his own practice sword. He had discovered the yard by leaning out of the bedchamber window and was determined to be the first to use it.

The yard was a strip of hard earth behind the barracks, crowded by a wall on one side and a line of tethering posts on the other. It was smaller than Grassfield's. In one corner stood a quintain with the painted face nearly rubbed away. A pair of men-at-arms were working at one another with wooden poles, their shoes raising little clouds of dust.

Matthos looked disappointed.

“Is this the whole yard?”

“Unless we've misplaced the rest of it,” Joffrey said.

Ser Cleyton put on his gloves.

“We'll have a few passes. The day is too hot for more. Joffrey, your shield.”

Joffrey took it from Orryn with a muttered thanks.

He and Matthos began with light cuts. Matthos fought as he did at Grassfield: quickly, loudly and with the conviction that every blow ought to be noticed. Joffrey was slow to raise his shield, then held it too far forward and allowed Matthos to push his sword aside.

“Again,” Ser Cleyton said.

Matthos struck him on the shoulder.

“Again.”

Matthos struck him on the other shoulder.

Joffrey gave a small, aggrieved sigh.

“Must we have both shoulders?”

“Keep your shield up,” Cleyton said.

“That's what I was trying to do.”

Matthos took a step back and flourished his sword.

“Ser Moryn's squires won't let you stand there like that.”

“Then I won't go near them,” said Joffrey.

Ser Cleyton frowned.

“Matthos, leave him room. It's practice, not a judgment of the Seven.”

Matthos looked as though he would have preferred the judgment.

When Orryn's turn came, he had barely warmed his arms before Matthos caught him across the wrist. Orryn's fingers opened. His sword fell, point first, into the dust.

He was used to the sound it made.

Matthos raised his sword triumphantly.

“You'd better be glad you're not going to Highgarden.”

“Pick it up,” Ser Cleyton said to Orryn.

Orryn did so. He set his feet as Ser Everard had taught him, though the ground sloped toward the wall and his right heel kept slipping on loose earth. Matthos feinted toward his head, and Orryn took the bait. The next blow caught the side of his practice shield hard enough to turn him. He nearly fell against the tethering post.

“That's enough,” said Cleyton.

“I could beat him again,” Matthos said.

“I expect you could. He's had a morning on horseback and two more days before that.”

“So have I.”

“Then you can show me how well you stand against a rested man.”

Matthos's expression changed. Ser Cleyton raised his sword, and the boy hurried to bring up his shield.

Orryn retreated toward the wall, breathing hard. Joffrey had already seated himself in the shade with his back against the stone. He moved his legs to make room.

“Your wrist?” Joffrey asked.

“Only stings.”

“Mine does too. He caught me twice there.”

Orryn examined the red mark. It would be a bruise by morning.

“Do you practise every day?” he asked.

“Every day somebody remembers.”

“You must be better than you showed.”

“No. I'm sometimes worse.”

Orryn looked at him, uncertain whether he was joking.

Joffrey rubbed his shoulder.

“I can ride tolerably when a horse is feeling charitable. I can keep my shield up when nobody's hitting it. As for a lance, I've always thought they were built wrong. Too much wood at one end and not enough at the other.”

“You'll improve.”

“So they say.”

“Don't you want to?”

“I want them to stop making me fall off horses.”

Orryn laughed.

Joffrey turned the practice sword between his hands.

“People talk about my spurs as though they're a treasure somebody has buried and I'll find it if I dig long enough. Father would like me knighted. Mother would like me to be good at the things a knight is good at. Cleyton would like me to stop hitting him by mistake.”

“And you?”

“I'd like to play my lute.”

Orryn looked at him.

“Truly?”

“Truly. I like hunting sometimes, though not when we're expected to catch anything. I like the river. I like being here. I don't dream of standing in the lists while somebody breaks a pole against my chest.”

“Every squire wants to be a knight.”

Joffrey looked at him as though considering whether he had heard correctly.

“Do they?”

Orryn opened his mouth.

He could think of Baldric polishing his sword belt until the leather shone. Of Perkin pretending he had already been in battle. Of Matthos in the centre of the yard, swinging too hard at Ser Cleyton. Of Alyn kneeling before Lord Alaric and rising as a knight, though perhaps Alyn had wanted chiefly to be free.

He looked at the blunted sword in his own hand.

“I thought so.”

“Well, I expect I shall be one someday,” Joffrey said. “I shall also be lord of Bitterbridge, if the Seven don't object. That seems enough occupations for one person.”

He lifted the sword as though weighing it.

“Do you like it?”

Orryn looked toward the yard. Matthos had just been struck on the shoulder, and was insisting that Ser Cleyton had stepped too far to one side.

“I don't mind it.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Orryn thought of the mornings at Grassfield, when Ser Everard's cane tapped his ankle until he got the stance right and his arms ached before breakfast. The days he enjoyed were more often the days he had been sent somewhere, or allowed to put something right.

“Not much,” he said.

Joffrey seemed satisfied.

“Then we've one thing in common.”

He stood and offered Orryn a hand, which was entirely unnecessary but made getting up easier.

“Come. I'll show you the river from the gallery. There's a place where you can hear the boats without having to smell the fish.”

Matthos called after them.

“Where are you going?”

“To recover from your greatness,” Joffrey answered.

Ser Cleyton told Matthos to attend to his feet.

Orryn followed Joffrey into the cool passage. It was pleasant not to be told to try another pass.

*

The gallery overlooked a bend in the Mander. Water moved beneath the walls with a slow authority that made the little rivers near Grassfield seem trifling. From here Orryn could see the market roofs, the bridge crowded with travellers and a line of boats worked upstream by men hauling at ropes from the bank.

Joffrey had brought a lute from his chamber. He sat on the low window bench and began tuning it, turning each peg cautiously before plucking the string.

“Will you play?” Orryn asked.

“When I've made it less dreadful.”

“I don't know how to play anything.”

“Then you'll be an excellent audience.”

The first tune was a quick one Orryn half recognised from a wedding at Grandview. Joffrey played it more slowly than he had heard it before, with a little turn in the melody that gave it a different ending. His face changed while he played. He still had the same careless posture, and his sleeve was slipping down his wrist, but he listened to the sound with a seriousness he had not shown in the yard.

When he finished, he wanted to know whether Orryn knew the song.

“My cousin Alyssa used to sing it.”

“Did she sing it well?”

“She thought so.”

Joffrey laughed.

“Then she's a true singer.”

He began another tune, softer and less familiar. Through the window came the cries of boatmen and the creaking of ropes. Orryn rested his aching wrist against the stone and watched Joffrey's fingers move.

For a while it was possible to forget there was another boy in the castle who would spend the next few days explaining why Highgarden had chosen him.

Joffrey stopped when a woman called from below that supper would be served at the evening bell.

“Mother will want me dressed like an heir,” he said. “I'd better go and be one.”

He put the lute into its case with considerably more care than he had shown his shield.

At the door he looked back.

“If you want to hear the rest tomorrow, come after breakfast.”

Orryn nodded.

“I should like that.”

“Good. It's a long tune, and nobody here stays to hear the end.”

Joffrey went down the stair, leaving Orryn alone at the window.

Below him the river carried another boat toward the bridge. He could hear the men shouting to one another, though he could not make out the words. They were hauling against the current, slowly, and did not seem troubled by how little progress they made from one moment to the next.

He flexed his wrist. It hurt.

He wondered what his father would say if he told him that he did not much like the training yard. He could hear the answer already, though perhaps he had made it up from other answers: liking a duty did not determine whether it had to be done.

His father was a knight. So were Alaric, Ser Lucan and almost every man Orryn had been taught to admire.

Joffrey would inherit a bridge and a castle whether he could lift a lance or not. Orryn had no such certainty awaiting him. His own future had been harder to picture even before this afternoon.

He stayed until the bell sounded, then went to dress for supper.

*

Lord Armond's hall was crowded by the time Orryn came down.

The Caswell lord had invited three visiting knights to supper, and someone had brought in a fat river fish that nearly filled the serving board. Its skin had been scored and rubbed with herbs. A servant was trying to find a knife long enough to divide it without dragging half the flesh away.

Matthos had found a place near the high table. He was talking to Ser Cleyton about the knights of Highgarden and was pointing with his knife whenever he remembered a name.

Joffrey saw Orryn from the lower board and beckoned him over.

“Sit here. Cleyton's going to talk about his new cloak until somebody asks him to take it off.”

Orryn sat. Joffrey had changed into a dark blue doublet and washed the dust from his hair. He had also acquired a cup of wine, which he kept partly hidden behind a dish of onions.

“I thought squires served,” Orryn said.

“Cleyton says I serve by behaving well.”

“Are you?”

“Not especially.”

Across the hall Lord Armond lifted his cup.

“To my goodson, Lord Alaric Meadows, and young Matthos, who means to carry Grassfield's name before the finest knights in the Reach.”

The company drank. Matthos raised his cup before he realised it was empty. Orryn passed him the pitcher, and Matthos took it without thanks.

“You'll be coming back with your lord, then?” Joffrey asked quietly.

“Yes.”

“Matthos will be pleased to remind you of it.”

“He's been reminding me since Grassfield.”

“Then you're properly prepared.”

Joffrey smiled and reached for the fish.

Lady Brenna sat beside her husband, listening to a knight who was explaining that the road beyond the Mander had become dangerous for carts travelling with grain. She asked which stretch of road he meant, and whether the trouble was thieves or the men claiming to collect a toll. When he could not say, she made no answer, but she did not appear much impressed.

Later, when the fish had been replaced with mutton and a bowl of early greens, she turned toward Alaric.

“Have you arranged a private audience with my brother?”

“Not yet.”

“With Moryn, or with Garth?”

“Moryn. I have Matthos to present.”

“Of course. And after that?”

“I expect I'll find Garth when his officers have finished with him.”

“Then don't leave it too late. Garth's time is harder to obtain than Moryn's, though my father would dislike hearing me say so.”

Armond laughed.

“Your brother Garth would put a seal upon the sunrise if the sun would stand still long enough.”

“Perhaps then you would know when to rise,” Brenna replied.

Several people laughed. Armond grinned at his wife and drank.

“Leave the affairs of the Reach to those who can remember a man's face, Brenna. Moryn knows a knight when he sees one.”

“And Garth knows what it costs to maintain him.”

Alaric moved a piece of mutton about his plate.

“Matthos will be serving, not commanding a company.”

“No one thinks otherwise,” Brenna said. “But a boy who joins Moryn's household is seen beside Moryn. Men notice whose sons attend whom.”

Matthos straightened in his seat.

“I shall be riding with his knights.”

“If he accepts you,” Alaric said.

Brenna gave the boy a courteous smile.

“If he does, you must learn to ride well enough that no one remembers who your father is when you fall.”

Matthos flushed. Joffrey put his cup down quickly.

Orryn looked at Brenna. She had said it kindly, or almost kindly, but Matthos had been made ridiculous without being directly insulted.

“Father favours Moryn's company,” she continued to Alaric, “provided Moryn does not turn the whole court into a camp of young men who need horses and new harness every moon.”

“Has he been spending?” Armond asked.

“Everyone has been spending. The question is whose purse pays for it.”

Armond waved this away.

“A great lord must have a great household.”

“Yes,” she said. “And a great household needs somebody to count the candles.”

Orryn thought of Verrall at Grassfield and felt suddenly homesick for the noise of the buttery.

One of the visiting knights asked whether it was true that Lord Rowan was seeking a match for his daughter at Highgarden. The question seemed to please Armond.

“Why shouldn't he? Goldengrove's daughter would make a fine bride for Harlen.”

“Roslin Rowan,” Brenna said. “Though I don't think anything is settled.”

“Lord Leo has always liked Rycherd,” Armond said.

“My father likes a great many men. It doesn't follow that he means to marry his grandson to all their daughters.”

“Garth must know what's best for his own son.”

Brenna took a little wine.

“Garth knows that Roslin brings House Rowan close to his table. Lady Alys knows that Meredyth Blackbar brings her own Redwyne kin closer. Harlen, I suspect, would prefer everyone stopped discussing him before his supper.”

Orryn knew Lord Leo Tyrell's grandson was called Harlen, but he had not known there were two proposed brides. The names ran past him more quickly than the faces could form. He caught Rowan and Redwyne, both great houses, and Blackbar, which he had seen upon Maester Perrin's roll of arms without remembering the colours.

“Blackbar?” Armond said. “I thought the girl was of Hightower blood.”

“Her mother is Lord Jon Hightower's daughter. That does not change her father's name.”

“Then there's no lack of good blood, by the sound of it.”

“Good blood is rarely the difficulty,” Brenna answered.

Alaric was smiling faintly at his plate.

“What does your father want?”

“I think he likes the Rowan match,” Brenna said. “What he will agree to after Garth and Lady Alys have had their say is another matter.”

She did not sound certain. Orryn had expected a daughter of Highgarden to know exactly what her father would do. Perhaps she knew and preferred not to tell them. He could not decide which.

At the lower board Joffrey was picking the green leaves from his mutton.

“Do they always talk about marriages?” Orryn whispered.

“Only when they tire of wars.”

He had scarcely spoken when Armond began talking about the war.

A visitor had mentioned Lord Gormon Peake, who was expected to make an appearance at Highgarden later in the year if his engagements allowed. Armond gave a snort.

“The last time Gormon came through here, he swore he'd never forgive Daeron for taking his castles.”

“Then he should be pleased that Daeron left him one,” Ser Bertram said.

That drew laughter from the knights.

Alaric looked up.

“Peake has a long memory.”

“He does,” Armond said. “So have I. And mine tells me who stood where when Daemon raised his banners.”

Brenna set down her cup.

“Armond.”

“What? I've no quarrel with a pardoned man who keeps his oath.”

He turned toward Alaric with an expression of broad affection.

“Here's a man who knows how narrow the choice can be. I had to come down on him like a hammer, didn't I, Alaric?”

Alaric did not answer.

“You were hardly more than a boy yourself,” Armond went on. “But lord of Grassfield already, with your father's seat under you and half your neighbours waiting to learn where you would lead them.”

Orryn looked at his lord.

Alaric set down his knife.

“I had not forgotten that I was a lord.”

“No, you hadn't. That was half the difficulty.” Armond laughed into his wine. “You thought you could choose for yourself, and I told you plain what choice you had. Daeron, or no help from Bitterbridge. No men, no money, no friendly roof for you or Jeyne if you turned your swords against the king.”

The hall had not fallen silent, but Orryn heard the words distinctly. A man at the far table was still asking for salt.

“I considered the claims on both sides,” Alaric said.

“Considered them? You kept me talking half a day about Daemon's right and Daeron's weakness.”

“Eleven years ago,” Alaric replied.

“Eleven years, yes. I remember it well. You were lord in your own hall, and still I had to tell you what would happen if you took your house to Daemon.”

Orryn had never heard that Alaric had considered the Black Dragon. He looked from his lord to the red-faced man who was chuckling over the tale.

Armond had fought for King Daeron at Redgrass. Orryn knew that. So had Alaric, or at least he had served the king's cause. At Grassfield the men who had chosen Daemon were spoken of as men whose judgment had cost them dearly. Ser Fenwick kept his land by pardon and renewed oath. The Graves had lost a mill and given a boy as surety.

He had not thought to count Lord Alaric among those who had nearly gone the other way.

Lady Brenna's voice cut quietly across the table.

“He was Lord Meadows, Armond. You could not command him.”

“No. So I made it clear what the king's friends would think of him.”

“And Jeyne was his wife,” Brenna said. “She would have borne the cost of his decision with him.”

Alaric looked toward her.

“You needn't instruct me on what my wife would have borne.”

“I didn't mean to.”

But Brenna did not take back the words.

Armond raised his cup once more.

“And in the end he chose rightly. I was proud of him then, as I am now.”

Alaric inclined his head, a little too stiffly for Orryn's liking.

“Your friendship was of great value to me, my lord.”

It sounded like something he had said many times before.

The conversation moved on when Ser Bertram asked Armond about the scar on his forearm. Armond obliged with a long story of a Reach knight whose horse had fallen upon him at Redgrass, and the visiting men leaned forward to hear it.

Orryn was no longer listening.

He watched Alaric's hands. They lay beside his plate, still except when he lifted his cup. At Grassfield, Alaric had taken Orryn to see Ser Fenwick sign a hay tally, and had spoken of the old rebellion as something whose consequences a lord must manage long afterward. He had seemed so sure of the distinction between the men who had remained loyal and the men who had not.

The distinction had been real. Orryn knew that.

Yet Alaric had once stood on the wrong side of it, or near enough for his father-in-law to threaten him.

Joffrey touched his elbow.

“Are you listening?”

“Yes.”

“To what?”

Orryn looked down at his untouched mutton.

“I don't know.”

“Then take some bread before it goes.”

Orryn took a piece and found that he was hungry after all.

*

When supper ended, Armond wanted the visitors to stay for another cup. Brenna reminded him that the young men had ridden three days and must be up early if they meant to put good miles behind them before the worst of the heat. Armond protested, then let the boys go.

In the passage outside the hall, Joffrey asked Orryn whether he would like to hear the rest of the tune.

“Tomorrow?”

“Tonight, if you can stay awake.”

Orryn glanced toward Alaric, who was standing with Ser Bertram near the door.

“I should ask my lord.”

“Then ask.”

Alaric heard enough of the exchange to turn.

“Ask what?”

“May I hear Joffrey play, my lord?”

Alaric looked at the boys, then toward the hall.

“For a little while. Don't keep the household awake.”

“Yes, my lord.”

Joffrey waited until they were beyond the corner before saying, “He sounds like Mother when she thinks I'm going to steal the wine.”

Orryn smiled.

They went up to the gallery above the river. Joffrey set the lute upon his knee and played while the last daylight drained from the water. Orryn sat on the window bench, listening to the long tune and watching a pair of lanterns move beneath the bridge. The notes came slowly at first, then gathered speed until Joffrey had to lean forward to follow his own fingers.

He missed a note, swore softly, and began the passage again.

Orryn was glad he did. It meant there was still something to hear.

When the tune finished, Joffrey looked toward him.

“Well?”

“I liked it.”

“You're easily pleased.”

“I'm not.”

Joffrey grinned.

“Then I'll believe you.”

Orryn thought of the practice yard, of Matthos telling him how great the knights of Highgarden would be, and of Armond's voice in the hall declaring that he had threatened a ruling lord into loyalty.

All day men had spoken as though a boy's path might be decided by the place he served, the man who taught him, the house he would inherit or the marriage somebody arranged for him.

Joffrey rested the lute against the wall and stretched his cramped fingers.

“You're thinking again,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Does it help?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then you're fortunate.”

From the stairs came the tread of a servant carrying candles. Joffrey reached for his instrument, and Orryn stood. They exchanged goodnights at the passage that led to the guest chambers.

In the room above the gate, Matthos was already asleep beneath the curtained bed. Someone had left a candle burning on the table for Alaric.

Orryn laid out his lord's clean shirt, checked that the boots were where they would be wanted, and set the water jug within reach of the bed.

Alaric entered while he was putting away the brush.

“Were they good hosts to you?” he asked.

“Yes, my lord.”

“Good. We leave after breakfast.”

Orryn nodded.

Alaric sat and began pulling off his boots. He struggled with the left, then pushed his heel against the edge of the chair and freed it.

“Go to your bed, Grandison.”

Orryn reached the door before he spoke.

“My lord?”

Alaric looked up.

“Were you already Lord Meadows when Daemon rose?”

The lord did not answer at once. The boot lay across his knee.

“Yes.”

Orryn waited.

“I had inherited before the banners were raised,” Alaric said. “That is why Armond came to speak to me. I was responsible for the men who would ride under mine.”

“And you thought of joining Daemon?”

Alaric looked at the candle.

“I thought of many things. Some more foolishly than others.”

Orryn did not know whether that was an answer.

“Lord Armond said he threatened you.”

“He did. He was my father-in-law. He had the right to tell me what he would do, not the right to command my banners.”

“But you chose Daeron.”

“I did.”

Alaric put down the boot.

“And your grandfather at Grandview chose as he thought best for his house. You may one day learn that being told what is right does not make the choice any easier.”

Orryn remembered his grandfather Gawen and the nine days after Redgrass, and the letters his grandmother had sent that no one could quite explain. He had come to Bitterbridge expecting to hear old war stories. Now he wondered how many men who had fought beneath the same dragon had first considered different banners.

Alaric studied him.

“Don't look at me as though I nearly brought Daemon into this room. I served King Daeron, and I have kept my oath.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Go to bed.”

Orryn bowed and left.

He found the page asleep in the little chamber with his feet hanging out from beneath the blanket. Ser Bertram had not yet come up. Orryn washed the dust from his face, folded his tunic and lay down.

In the dark, the river could still be heard beyond the wall. He thought of the bruise upon his wrist and of Joffrey playing beneath the window, not minding that he had made a mistake and must begin again.

Tomorrow Matthos would ride toward his future place beneath the golden rose.

Orryn would ride beside him, and eventually back to Grassfield.

He was less certain than he had been that the difference between their roads was the one Matthos thought it was.
