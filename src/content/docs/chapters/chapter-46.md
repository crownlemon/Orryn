The Ashfords left before Lady Odessa did. Ser Androw rode out beside his mother, then turned back under the arch because Wylla had come down without her shoes. She stood on the mounting block in her stockings while he bent from the saddle to speak to her. Orryn could see where the stone had darkened under her feet. Whatever Androw said made her reach up and take hold of the rim of his shield. He held still until she let go.

Her mother waited another hour for the heat to come off the leather of her litter, though the heat was going the other way. At the last she took Alyn by the elbow and walked him out of the shade to see what the sun had done to his face. Orryn was holding one of the litter mules. He heard her tell Alyn to eat less salt. Alyn said he had never known anyone so solicitous for his complexion. She turned his head by the chin, looked behind one ear, and rubbed away a streak of black with her glove.

“Your brother did the very same thing,” she said.

“What, got dirt behind his ear?”

“Thought I could only see what I was looking at.”

She gave him her hand to kiss. Then she settled herself behind the litter curtain and asked Wylla whether she had remembered to say goodbye to Lady Leonette.

“Of course I did,” said Wylla. “She lent me a pair of shoes.”

They were the green ones with the embroidered toes. Orryn had seen Leonette come halfway down the gallery to watch Wylla cross the yard in them, and go back before Wylla looked up.

When the last of the Merryweather horses went through, Alyn stood beneath the arch with his thumbs hooked in his belt. The gateward waited behind him with the bar. Orryn had nearly gone back for the mule's nosebag before he realized that Alyn was looking at the empty road.

“He could have stopped,” Orryn said.

“Who?”

“Ser Androw. To say goodbye.”

“He did. I was up before you.”

Alyn bent to pick up the nosebag. A few oats had caught in the seam. He shook them into his hand and ate them, one by one, with an air of close attention to their quality.

“There's a hunt this afternoon,” he said. “If you want the grey, you'll have to ask early. My lord is in a generous humour and will give her to somebody.”

“I have Rook.”

“Your dun walks like my father coming down to breakfast.”

Orryn had been watching Rook walk since they came back from Fenwick's. He had hoped it looked worse to him than it did to other people.

“It's the heel,” he said.

“I know. Ask for the grey.”

Alyn put the nosebag into his arms and went off toward the kitchen. From the gallery above, Leonette called down to ask if he meant to come hunting. He went backwards three steps so he could see her under the eaves.

“If they'll have me.”

“I'll have you. You can carry what I kill.”

“You'll have to kill it first.”

She disappeared from the rail. Alyn stood looking up for a moment longer, and when he turned Orryn was still there with the bag.

“What?” he said.

“Nothing.”

It was a small thing to have said, and he would have liked to say it again in a different voice.

*

Lord Alaric's hunt was six people and two dogs by the time it left the lower gate. He had meant to take the falcons, but Ser Wyman found one of Tansy's feet hot and made him leave her on her perch. Ser Wyman came anyway. He wore an old sleeveless coat and led a white bitch with a swollen teat that she would lick whenever he stopped looking. The other dog was a rangy yellow one from the kitchen, allowed out because it had caught three rats since the visitors came and the cook wished it to catch something belonging to somebody else.

The grey had indeed gone to somebody. Lady Jeyne rode her. Orryn was given a brown pony whose mouth had been spoiled by a boy with strong hands. The beast went sideways whenever he asked it to slow. He had been told to keep the yellow dog near him until they got beyond the orchards, and by the second gate he had let it pass him twice. On the third occasion he came near to running the pony into Lady Jeyne.

“You may let him go now, Master Grandison,” she said.

Orryn let the dog go. It sat down at once.

“An excellent creature,” said Ser Wyman. “He knows when he's free.”

Lady Jeyne bent from the grey and spoke to the dog as if she expected him to come. He got up, shook himself and trotted at her stirrup all the way to the next field.

They rode along the bottom of the orchard to a stretch of stubble where the ground rose toward a low wood. A man and a woman were at work there, lifting stones out of the grass along the ditch. The woman wore a man's hat with a split brim. When the horses came up she put a hand to it, and the stone she had been carrying fell back with a thud that made the white bitch start.

“Digging?” Alaric called.

“Taking the top off, my lord,” said the man. “There'll be a ditch when there's water to run in it.”

“There was a ditch when I came this way last.”

“Aye, and the bank went in after.”

Alaric dismounted and went to look. The dry crust broke under his heel and a strip of earth fell away down the side. He crouched where he was, with his reins in one hand, and asked the man how long the work had been going on. The woman answered him. Four days, with her daughter's eldest to help for two of them. It would have been done by now if the daughter had been able to spare the boy longer. Alaric looked along the bank where the stones lay, some small enough to pick up and some that would have needed all three of them.

“I can spare two men tomorrow,” he said. “They'll tell you how it ought to be done. You needn't listen if they haven't brought a spade.”

“Will they want dinner?” the woman asked.

“Send them up to me for it.”

The yellow dog had found a place to lie in the shadow of the woman's skirt. She moved her feet to make room for him. Orryn watched her do it and wondered what sort of man would tell her how to dig her own ditch. He could imagine his grandfather taking off his gloves to try a stone before he said how many men it needed. He could also imagine how long the man and woman would have had to stand there while he tried it.

Leonette had been waiting on the stubble, twisting in her saddle to look toward the wood. She wore a soft green hood, too hot for the day, which had been drawn back until it hung between her shoulders. Alyn rode beside her. He had taken off one glove and was bending a little twig round his bare forefinger. She kept trying to pull it out without catching the end. Neither of them seemed to notice that the rest had stopped talking.

“What is that?” Lady Jeyne asked.

“A ring,” said Leonette. “He says I can't take it.”

“It's green wood,” said Alyn. “It won't break.”

Leonette got it free on the next try. There was a stripe of bark left under Alyn's nail. He rubbed it away on his sleeve while she fitted the loop over her own finger.

“You'll have to cut it off when the finger swells,” said Ser Wyman.

“Then I shall send for you, ser.”

“I'd send for somebody with a smaller knife.”

Orryn had seen Ser Garlan Fossoway's name on a place set at a table where the man himself had never sat. At the dancing Leonette had spoken of him with her chin up, as if there were something she knew he would say that she was waiting to hear again. Orryn had never seen him dance. He had seen Alyn do it. He wondered whether Ser Garlan had ever sat on a horse in the sun and let a girl try to pull a twig out of his hand.

Alaric climbed back into the saddle and brushed the dirt off his knees. “Shall we go and see if there's a hare left in the Reach?”

“There was one in that hedge yesterday, my lord,” said the woman. “It ate the tops off my beans.”

“Then you'll know it by its fatness when I bring it back.”

The woman laughed after he turned his horse, and Orryn, coming last through the gap, saw that she was still laughing as she put her hands back to the stone.

*

They found the hare on a strip of ploughed ground beyond the wood. Orryn saw only the lift of its ears before it went. Ser Wyman slipped the white bitch, the yellow dog followed on his own account, and in a moment all six horses were moving. The brown pony surprised Orryn by going beautifully as long as it was going where it pleased.

The hare doubled at the edge of a ditch. The white bitch overshot, came round with her tail whipping, and went after it again. The yellow dog went into the ditch and stayed there. Ahead, the ditch met a hedge laid low enough for a horse to take. Alaric went over. Ser Wyman followed him with one hand pressed to his hat. Orryn heard Lady Jeyne call to Leonette, and then Leonette was past him, her green hood flying, with Alyn on her left.

There was a gap where a cart had gone through. Orryn meant to take it. The pony decided otherwise, jumped from too far out and caught a hind foot. Orryn came down against its neck with his teeth together. For three strides he had one stirrup and a view of the ground he would land on. By the time he had both reins again, Leonette's mare was standing in the furrows beyond the hedge, shivering all down one side.

Leonette had lost her hood. She was in the saddle, laughing so hard she could hardly get out the words to tell Alyn she was all right. He had caught her rein below the bit. There was blood on the mare's chest, a spot no bigger than the end of Orryn's thumb where a thorn had taken her. Alyn ran his hand down her foreleg. Leonette rested her palm on the back of his head as he bent, the way a person might rest it on a familiar rail.

Lady Jeyne came through by the gap. She looked at the mare's chest and told Leonette to dismount. Leonette began to explain that it was a scratch. Her mother asked her again, and she got down.

“Walk her a little,” Lady Jeyne said to Orryn. “Let me see her take a step.”

Orryn got off the pony. His tongue tasted of blood where he had caught it. The mare walked soundly enough, though she kept lifting her head at the sound of the others away across the field. When he brought her back, Lady Jeyne was holding Leonette's green hood. Orryn had not seen who picked it up.

“You may go on after my lord,” she told Alyn. “He has only Wyman with him, and Wyman will think of the bitch before he thinks of anybody.”

Alyn looked across the field. Alaric and Ser Wyman had stopped at the far edge where the plough met the grass. There was no sign of either dog from where Orryn stood.

“He has a horn, my lady. He'll blow it if he wants us.”

“Then you'll be there already.”

Alyn took his hand off the mare's bridle. It was done without haste. He mounted, settled the flap of his coat under him, and rode across the furrows. Leonette took a step as if she meant to go after him and stopped when she found she had nowhere to put her foot. She stood in the trampled stubble with her hands loose at her sides.

“The pony will carry you home,” Lady Jeyne said. “Master Grandison can bring the mare round by the lane.”

“You saw she was sound.”

“I said I wanted to see her walk.”

“Then I'll walk her.”

Lady Jeyne shook the earth from the hood. “I should like you to ride with me.”

Leonette held out her hand. Orryn thought for a moment she was asking for the hood, and then he saw she wanted a leg up. He stooped and took her foot. She was lighter than he had expected, or had done more of the work herself. Once she was up she gathered the pony under her with an ease that made his own hour on it seem very long.

“Do you have the ring?” she asked.

Orryn looked down. The loop of twig had gone from her hand.

“I didn't see it fall, my lady.”

“Never mind. I can make one.”

She took the hood from her mother and rode back through the gap. The two of them waited there while Orryn set out on foot with the mare. By the time he turned in at the lane they had gone.

It was a long way round. Under the hedge the thorns had caught little knots of sheep's wool, grey with dust, that had seemed white from the saddle. The mare wanted to crop what grass there was. Orryn let her have some in a patch of shade and put a finger into his mouth to feel where he had bitten himself. He hoped Leonette would ask him to find the twig when she saw him again. He could tell her precisely how foolish it was to send someone back for a thing like that. In his head he spoke quite well. By the time he had brought the mare to the gate he was ashamed of half the things he had thought of saying and still wanted to say the other half.

*

The white bitch caught the hare in the end. Alaric sent it down to the woman at the ditch, and Ser Wyman kept the liver for his dog. The yellow dog came home last, wet to the chest from some place that had water enough to wet him. The cook sent him out again because he had rolled in something dead.

At supper the household took its old places. The empty space where the visitors had sat was filled by Ser Lucan and the septon, who had a sore on the side of his mouth and kept putting his cup down without drinking. Orryn stood behind Alaric with the wine. He could see Leonette past Alaric's shoulder. Her hair had been washed and lay in a damp plait over one sleeve. She spoke to Elyce about the mare's scratch. Elyce wanted to know whether the mare would have a scar. Leonette said she hoped so, for something to show for a day on which nobody had allowed her to finish anything.

Alyn had come in after the bread and gone to the squires' board. There was room at the end nearest the dais, where he often sat when Lord Alaric had finished with him. Marya had put a jug and a plate there for Ser Wyman's man, whose hand had swollen after a dog bite and who could only eat with his left. Alyn found a place beside Perkin instead. Orryn could see him pulling the meat off a wing and laying the small bones in a row on the bread.

“Were you there when the dog got it?” Alaric asked.

Orryn started. He had been looking straight at Alyn's hands.

“No, my lord. I went home by the lane.”

“I thought you came over behind me.”

“He did,” Lady Jeyne said. “On the brown pony.”

“Did you? How did he go?”

“Very well when he had the company, my lord.”

“He was well enough with me,” said Leonette. “He'd have gone anywhere I asked him.”

Alaric took the cup Orryn filled for him. “You can have him, then.”

“For how long?”

“Until you find you don't like him. That's how long I had him.”

Leonette laughed and asked whether he meant it. He said he did, and went back to his meat. Orryn wondered whether her mother would say anything about the hedge. Lady Jeyne was peeling a pear for Elyce in one long strip. She cut away a bruise and put that piece on her own plate.

“Lady Leonette will ride with me to the sept tomorrow,” she said. “The pony may come if he's accustomed to the bells.”

“He stood through them this morning,” said Orryn.

Lady Jeyne looked up at him. He thought he had spoken out of turn. “Good,” she said. “Tell the groom I'd like him at the steps after prayers.”

Leonette put her own pear down whole. “I thought you were going to send Marya.”

“I was. I find I have time.”

The septon touched his lip with a knuckle and said the stones up there were slippery even in a dry year. There was a loose one on the third step that he meant to have set. Alaric asked him why he had not set it already. The septon began to tell him about the man who did the work, who could set anything except a day to come.

The light went while they were still at table. Men brought in the lamps. In the change Orryn saw Leonette look down the hall at Alyn, briefly and quite openly, as if he could still be summoned to a conversation from any distance. Alyn was laughing at something Perkin had said. When he saw her looking, he raised his cup a little. She did the same. Elyce watched her sister set hers down, then reached across to take the unbruised half of the pear from her mother's plate.

*

Orryn went to the stable after supper to see how Rook had done without him. The horse was leaning against the boards with one hind foot turned up. He put it down when Orryn came in, then picked it up again. Piers had left a little pot of dressing on the shelf, with a finger mark in the grease where he had taken some for his own horse. Orryn carried it into the stall. He got Rook's foot between his knees and worked the grease into the edge of the hoof with his thumb. The animal shifted its weight and Orryn put the foot down before it could take it away from him.

Alyn was sitting on the step outside when he came out. There was a bowl beside him with two pears in it, neither quite ripe. He tossed one over. Orryn caught it against his chest and the pot almost went out of his other hand.

“Put that down before you open it,” Alyn said.

“I've had it open.”

“Have you? I could smell it from the stair.”

Orryn put the pot back on the shelf. When he came out again Alyn had drawn his feet up so there was room on the step. The wall still held the day's warmth. Across the yard someone was fastening the shutters in the gatehouse where Lady Ashford had slept. The catch took three tries. Each time the glass gave a little rattle that could be heard from the stable.

“Did you see the hare?” Orryn asked.

“Some of it. Wyman had it away from the bitch by the time I came up. He had to sit on her to get it.”

Orryn could see him doing it, with the old coat bunched round his middle and his hat on the ground. He laughed. He had been waiting all evening for Alyn to say something that would let him be angry.

“Did she fall?” Alyn asked.

“Who?”

“Leonette. When she took the hedge. I saw the mare go down in front and then I had the rein. She might have got back up quicker than I saw.”

“She stayed on.”

“I thought she did.” He bit into the pear and winced at it. “She can ride that one better than I can. Her father ought to let her out on her more.”

Orryn ate some of his pear. It was hard at the core and tasted of the place where the stalk had been broken off. He thought of Leonette's hand on Alyn's head. She had done it in the time it took a horse to lift a foot, as easily as Alyn had moved his feet on the step for Orryn. He could still feel the mare's rein in his own hand, hot where Alyn had held it.

“Your tongue's bleeding,” Alyn said.

“I bit it.”

“On the pony?”

Orryn nodded. Alyn got up and went to the trough. There was a clean dipper hung on the nail above it. He brought it back full and sat down again while Orryn rinsed his mouth. The water tasted of wood. He spat into the yard, and the stain in the dust was so pale he had to look twice to see it.

“Ask for the grey next time,” Alyn said.

“You said that this morning.”

“You can ask before breakfast. I'll go with you.”

Orryn took another mouthful of water. From the dark of the stall Rook blew softly through his nose, and Alyn turned his head at the sound. For a while they sat listening to the horse eat.
