Highgarden, 208 AC

The first thing Orryn saw beyond the arch was Matthos's horse refusing a turn.

It was a tall chestnut gelding, fine-boned and freshly groomed, and it had decided that the line of wooden posts beside the lower riding ground was more interesting than the boy upon its back. Matthos pulled at the left rein. The gelding turned its head and continued walking toward the posts.

"With your legs," a man called. "You cannot drag all of him about by his mouth."

Matthos tried again. This time the horse turned, though not quite where he intended. A few boys seated upon the rail began to laugh.

"Better," the man said. "Again."

Alaric stopped at the edge of the enclosure, and Orryn came up beside him with the bundle of papers tucked beneath his arm. The practice yard was already busy. Two pairs of squires worked with blunted swords in the shade of the wall. Beyond them, men-at-arms were moving through a drill with shields and spears. Horses stood tethered beneath a covered gallery, their coats darkened with sweat.

The instructor was the same hard-faced knight Orryn had glimpsed the previous day. He had removed his surcoat and stood in a plain linen shirt, with a sword belt at his waist and a short stick in his hand.

"Ser Luthor Risley," Alaric said quietly. "The master-at-arms."

Orryn had heard the name from Moryn the previous evening.

Risley saw them and raised his stick in greeting, though he did not interrupt Matthos.

"Eyes ahead, Meadows. That horse has seen the posts well enough without your help."

Matthos flushed and straightened. He rode past the far marker, brought the chestnut round and returned at a steady trot. Orryn could see how deliberately he kept his hands low. There were worse riders at Grassfield, but here even the younger boys seemed to sit their horses as if they had been born in the saddle.

"That's better," Risley called. "Bring him in. And don't let him choose where to stop."

Matthos halted near the rail, breathing a little too hard for so short a ride. He noticed his father and nearly forgot to shorten the reins.

Orryn saw the horse's head lift and looked away before Risley could catch him smiling.

"Lord Meadows." The master-at-arms approached, brushing dust from his hands. "Your son has ridden before."

"Since he could hold on, ser."

"That is not always the same as riding." Risley gave Matthos a glance. "He sits well enough. He needs to learn that a horse is not persuaded by the importance of its rider."

Alaric laughed. "Lord Leo told him much the same thing yesterday."

"His lordship was right."

Matthos dismounted and led the horse toward a groom. Risley stopped him.

"Where are you taking it?"

"To the stables, ser."

"Has it been walked?"

"No, ser."

"Then walk it."

Matthos obeyed, taking the chestnut around the edge of the yard. Orryn had done the same duty often enough at Grassfield to know how little glory there was in it.

Nearby, a young knight in splendid green riding clothes had begun striking at a quintain. He sent its arm spinning with a clean blow and received applause from several onlookers.

The men drilling with spears did not look toward him.

An older lord stood beside Risley, watching those men with his arms folded. He wore a faded riding cloak over a dark doublet, and the sword at his side had the polished hilt and worn scabbard of a weapon often used.

"Lord Luthor Roxton," Risley told Alaric. "Lord Marshal of the Reach."

Alaric bowed. Roxton returned the courtesy, then looked back toward the drill.

"Hold your left," he called. "The front rank has advanced and the rear has not. You'll open a gap wide enough for three horses."

The men stopped. Their captain shouted an order, and the line formed again.

"Is this the ordinary morning drill?" Alaric asked.

"It ought to be," Roxton said. "Half the trouble is that a man can know the use of his spear and still not know where to stand with fifty others."

Orryn watched the spearmen advance together. One moved too quickly and struck the heel of the man before him.

Roxton winced.

"There's your answer."

The knight at the quintain broke another lance. A cheer went up from the gallery.

"He rides prettily," Alaric observed.

"Very prettily," Roxton said. "Set him to hold a gate in rain for six hours and we shall learn something more."

Risley gave a short grunt that might have been laughter.

From the gate behind them came a familiar voice.

"Luthor, must every morning begin with a funeral sermon?"

A handsome knight in a green cloak had entered the yard, followed by two attendants carrying practice shields. He was broader than Moryn and younger than either of his brothers, with fair-brown hair and a confident, easy stride. The boys at the rail turned toward him at once.

"Ser Olyvar," Risley said.

Olyvar Tyrell clapped the master-at-arms upon the shoulder.

"If I were a new squire, I should think you meant to frighten every last boy back into his mother's chamber."

"Some mothers might thank me," Risley answered.

Olyvar laughed and turned to Alaric.

"Lord Meadows. My brother told me you were visiting."

"Ser Olyvar."

"And this must be your boy."

Matthos, who had just returned with the horse, bowed.

"Matthos, ser."

"You were riding the chestnut?"

"Yes, ser."

"An awkward creature. Good shoulders, though. My father would have won half the Reach upon a horse like that, if it could be convinced to turn."

"He turns when properly asked," Risley said.

"Then perhaps I should ask him instead of the boy."

Orryn laughed with the others. Matthos did not seem certain whether he ought to.

Olyvar leaned against the rail and watched the drill. The spearmen were moving forward again, their shields close enough that the edges almost touched.

"Better," he said.

"At last," Roxton replied.

"How many did you have yesterday?"

"Forty. I was promised sixty."

"Where were the others?"

"Some delayed on the road. Some sent word that their horses were not ready. Three arrived without shields they could use."

Olyvar's smile faded.

"From the same lord?"

"Two lords and a landed knight."

Alaric looked toward the men. "Those are household soldiers?"

"Men brought in for instruction," Roxton answered. "If a lord promises twenty spears, I prefer to learn before a campaign whether he means twenty men, twenty heads of rusty iron, or twenty names in a book."

"That sounds like something my brother Garth might say," Olyvar remarked.

"Your brother would count the missing shields," Roxton said. "I would like to know who intends to stand behind them."

He walked away to speak with the captain.

Olyvar watched him go.

"My father has made this the most splendid court in the realm," he said to Alaric. "I would not trade it for another. Every young knight from the Arbor to the Marches hopes to break a lance before him. But we have become rather too pleased with how fine we look while doing it."

Alaric regarded him. "You are a celebrated rider in the lists yourself, ser."

"Then I know the temptation." Olyvar smiled, acknowledging the point. "A tilt is a good thing. It teaches courage, seat and control. It does not teach a hundred men how to march together, nor a captain how to feed them when the roads turn to mud."

"Your father has commanded armies," Alaric said.

"And I have listened to him speak of them. He knows better than most what war requires. Yet a man may know a thing and find a thousand more pleasant duties demanding his day."

Orryn looked toward the knight at the quintain. He had removed his helm and was accepting congratulations, his face flushed with triumph. Beyond him the men with spears had begun the same advance for the fourth time, without any applause at all.

He wondered which exercise he would have chosen if given the choice.

*

Olyvar had brought a pair of riding gloves. He drew them on while speaking with Alaric, though no horse had yet been led for him.

"My good-brother Hewett writes to me about the Shield Islands," he said. "He can tell you how many ships he has fit for sea. He can tell you which walls need repair. He cannot tell you when the next money promised to the island garrisons will arrive, because no one has decided which of six other demands may wait."

"Then Lord Hewett ought to make his case to the Treasurer," Alaric said.

"He has. He makes it every year."

"Every lord would like more money."

"True. Lord Tarly would spend half the Arbor's wine duties upon roads and watchtowers in the Marches, if he could."

"And the Arbor would have something to say to that."

"So it would. That is why a lord needs to decide what he can afford and what he cannot afford to neglect."

Roxton returned to them, dissatisfied with something the spearmen had done.

"Our difficulty is not a want of men," he said. "It is the belief that because the Reach can raise so many, we need not trouble ourselves with making them ready."

Alaric looked across the yard. "I have men for my walls and roads, and tenants who owe service. If I summoned every able fellow for drilling whenever Highgarden wished it, I should have no hands to bring in the harvest."

"Nor would we wish it," Roxton said. "A hungry army is a poor defence for a country stripped of its farmers."

"Then what do you propose?"

"That when a man says he can bring thirty, he should know which thirty. That somebody has inspected their arms before the day they are called. That the captains can read an order, and the roads can bear the wagons carrying their bread."

Orryn thought of the papers he held under his arm. Less than an hour ago, Ser Garth and his officers had spoken about the road from Grassy Vale, asking who kept it repaired and how much the work cost.

Now Roxton was talking about the same roads, but with soldiers and wagons upon them.

"All sensible," Alaric said. "Who pays for it?"

"Now you sound like my eldest brother," Olyvar said.

"I have just spent the morning in his company."

"Then perhaps I should be grateful you haven't demanded a written account of our horses."

Alaric smiled. "I had no need. Ser Risley has already told us what your horse can and cannot do."

"Unkind, Lord Meadows."

Olyvar turned to Risley. "And would you say the young Meadows might profit from such preparation?"

"Every boy here might profit," Risley answered.

"I was speaking of this particular boy."

"He rides well enough to learn, and badly enough to have something to learn."

"That describes most of us," said Olyvar.

"Not all of us remember it."

Roxton's mouth moved slightly, but he did not quite smile.

Matthos had been listening so intently that he had let the gelding move a step toward a patch of grass beyond the rail. Risley saw it.

"Meadows. Tie him where he belongs, then fetch a cloth for that bit."

Matthos hurried away.

Olyvar watched him.

"How old is he?"

"Fourteen," Alaric said.

"At a good age for a new household. Too old to be carried through it, young enough to be corrected."

"He is not always easily corrected."

"Few lords' sons are. I wasn't."

Roxton said, "You are not especially fond of it now."

"Then it is fortunate I have you."

Olyvar looked after Matthos once more. "I understand Moryn is considering him."

"He has offered to consider him," Alaric replied.

"Has he given you his word?"

"Not yet."

Olyvar removed one glove and folded it through his belt.

"Then, my lord, I should be glad to take him myself."

Orryn looked at Alaric.

His master said nothing for a moment.

Matthos returned carrying a cloth. He had plainly heard the last words, for he stopped short of the rail and stared at Olyvar.

The Tyrell knight spoke as though he were offering the boy an ordinary courtesy, but he had ceased smiling.

"I mean it. If you wish your son to learn under my roof, he will be welcome. He will train here, under Risley, and ride with me when I go abroad. He will meet captains who have spent more time keeping men in the field than boasting of what they might do there."

"You honour him," Alaric answered.

"I would ask him to earn it. And I think there may be advantage in knowing one another better. Grassfield stands upon roads which matter to the eastern Reach. I have heard men speak of your crossings and your neighbours across the border."

"You have heard the more flattering accounts, I hope."

"I have heard that you know your country."

"Some of it."

"Then that is more than I can say for certain lords who claim to command it."

Roxton interrupted. "The boy would be welcome in any good household, if his father will see he arrives with sound equipment. The question is whether he will endure the work."

"Which is the question I should ask of any squire," Alaric said.

Matthos finally found his voice.

"Ser, I would be honoured."

Olyvar turned to him.

"Your father must decide before you begin thanking people."

Matthos bowed his head, red-faced.

Orryn thought he looked happier than he had when Moryn first spoke to him the night before. That was hardly surprising. Olyvar was a famous knight, young enough to ride in the lists and surrounded by men whose swords and armour seemed finer than anything at Grassfield.

But he had given Moryn his eager thanks too.

From the far arch came a woman's voice.

"I had been told my husband was in the practice yard. Now I see I should have come to look for him myself."

Lady Melessa Tyrell entered with Ser Moryn beside her. She was dressed for the morning in pale green, with a plain girdle and her hair bound away from her face. Moryn wore no armour, only riding clothes beneath a light cloak.

He greeted Alaric, then his younger brother.

"Olyvar. I thought I should find you here."

"And I hoped to find you elsewhere. It is a cruel disappointment to us both."

Moryn smiled. "You have been entertaining our guests?"

"We have been discussing their son's future."

"Have you?"

"Lord Meadows has brought a promising boy, and I have offered him a place."

There was a pause long enough for Orryn to hear a horse stamping behind the stable wall.

Moryn looked at Matthos.

"That is a considerable honour."

"I believe so," Alaric answered.

Lady Melessa came forward to greet Alaric.

"Ser Moryn has intended to speak with you after the morning's exercises," she said. "I hope we have not kept you waiting."

"Not at all, my lady."

Olyvar glanced from her to his brother.

"I did not know your arrangement had proceeded so far."

"It has proceeded as far as our discussion last night," Moryn replied. "Which is to say, we have spoken of the matter and not yet settled it."

"Then I have offered no offence."

"Of course not."

The brothers smiled at one another.

Orryn thought of the two boys at Grassfield who had once argued over a bow neither of them owned. They had smiled much less, but the argument had seemed easier to understand.

*

Ser Luthor Risley called the squires back to their work. The yard had no intention of pausing merely because two sons of Highgarden had taken an interest in a visitor.

A boy arrived with practice swords, and another led away the chestnut. Matthos was left holding the cloth he had been ordered to fetch.

"Go on," Moryn told him. "Ser Luthor has work for you, I imagine."

Matthos looked toward his father.

"Go," Alaric said.

The boy went, reluctantly.

Moryn turned to Risley.

"What did you make of him?"

"Of the horse?"

"Of the boy."

"A good seat. A little heavy with the hand, a little quick to be offended when corrected. No worse than most."

"Can you teach him?"

"I can teach any boy willing to learn. Keeping him willing is another business."

Olyvar laughed. "You say that of everyone."

"Because it remains true."

Lady Melessa asked whether they might talk somewhere less noisy. Moryn led the party to a shaded walk above the yard, where benches stood beneath an arcade. Orryn followed Alaric carrying the papers, though he wondered why they were still needed.

From the gallery they could see the practice ground through the arches. Men shouted, swords struck shields, and a groom led the chestnut toward the stables with its head low.

Roxton excused himself almost at once.

"That captain has allowed them to leave the centre open again. He'll get his men killed if they do it when somebody is trying to kill them."

Olyvar promised to speak with him later.

"Do not promise," Roxton said. "Come and watch."

He went down the steps.

Alaric watched the old lord depart.

"He seems to take his duties seriously."

"He does," Olyvar said. "He takes mine seriously as well, whenever I give him cause."

"Frequently," Moryn observed.

"More frequently than you deserve to hear about."

Lady Melessa sat beside the low wall, holding a little bunch of lavender she had gathered on the way.

"My lord," she said to Alaric, "my husband and I understand that this is your son's future, not a quarrel between brothers. You must consider what household will suit him."

"That is precisely what I am considering."

Moryn sat opposite him.

"You know how the offer came about. My wife's kin spoke well of Matthos and of Grassfield. I have long thought we should know more of the houses along our eastern borders, particularly those whose kinship reaches both into the Reach and the Stormlands."

"Lord Meadows has useful neighbours," Olyvar said.

"He has troublesome neighbours, too, I should expect."

"Both," Alaric answered.

Moryn nodded. "The boy would live among men and women whose families have dealings throughout the Reach. He would serve me, attend upon my guests, ride when I ride, and be expected to learn their names and his obligations to them. He would receive his instruction in arms here like the other squires."

"And under Ser Risley," Olyvar added. "Whom my brother has just established will teach any boy if he can be persuaded to remain willing."

"I do not dispute your ability to teach him," Moryn said.

"Nor I yours."

They were courteous enough that Orryn could scarcely imagine either of them angry. Yet both watched Alaric while he spoke.

The lord of Grassfield rested one hand upon his knee.

"When I sent word through Lady Ashford, I hoped to obtain a place for Matthos here. I thought he would benefit from service at Highgarden, from the men he would know and the manners he might learn. I have seen enough of my own household to know a boy should sometimes hear another man's commands."

"An excellent reason," said Moryn.

"I had not expected to be offered two places."

"You will have caused great distress among the boys who would like one," Olyvar said.

Alaric smiled, but did not answer.

Orryn thought of his own appointment to serve at Grassfield. He had not stood between two Tyrell brothers while his future was decided. He had known the honour of the place offered him, and the fear of failing once he arrived.

He wondered whether Matthos understood that one could be grateful and disappointed at the same time.

Alaric spoke again.

"Ser Olyvar, I have listened closely to what you and Lord Roxton say about the Reach. I have no wish to see our roads fail beneath an army, or our men march without proper arms. You are right that a lord can promise twenty men more easily than he can find twenty good spears."

"Then we agree upon something important," Olyvar said.

"We do. I would be glad to speak further of it. If the eastern roads matter to the defence of the Reach, I can tell you what trouble we have keeping them sound."

Olyvar inclined his head.

"I should welcome that."

"But I approached your brother, through his wife's family, to ask him to take Matthos. He has received us with courtesy and given the matter consideration. Unless he has changed his mind, I would ask him to keep the place for my son."

Moryn looked toward Alaric.

"I have not changed my mind."

Olyvar said nothing at first.

Orryn could hear a boy calling for his dropped sword below.

Then Olyvar smiled, though there was less laughter in it than before.

"Then Matthos will have an excellent master."

"He will have a demanding one," Moryn said.

"I hope so. We cannot have him learning to attend feasts instead of being trained for war."

Moryn lifted an eyebrow. "You managed both."

"With difficulty."

"And have made a reputation of the effort."

Lady Melessa laughed, and Alaric joined her.

Olyvar offered his hand.

"Lord Meadows, I meant what I said. The place was yours to accept. It was also yours to decline. I hope it will not prevent us from knowing one another."

"I should be sorry if it did, ser."

"Then come and see me before you leave Highgarden. Lord Roxton can explain to you in unpleasant detail why your roads should carry soldiers, and I shall attempt to make the occasion agreeable."

"I have heard several unpleasant explanations about my roads already."

"Then you are well prepared."

The two men shook hands.

Orryn watched Moryn while they did so. He seemed pleased with the outcome, though his face had given little away when his brother made the offer.

It occurred to Orryn that the decision had brought Alaric something besides a place for Matthos. Olyvar had invited him to return.

He wondered whether his lord had expected that.

Moryn rose.

"Well, then. I believe there is a boy somewhere below us who expects to hear his fate."

Lady Melessa stood beside him.

"There are also arrangements to settle. His clothing, his horse, his letters home."

Alaric sighed.

"I knew there would be an account at the end."

"A smaller one than my brother Garth would prepare," Moryn said.

"I met him this morning."

"Then I shall be careful what I promise."

They descended into the yard together.

Matthos was cleaning a bit beside the horse trough. His sleeves were damp, and there was a patch of mud upon his knee. When he saw them approaching, he rose so abruptly that the bit slipped from his hands.

Risley watched it fall.

"Pick that up, Meadows. It has not grown wings."

Matthos retrieved it and bowed.

"Ser Moryn."

"Your father and I have come to an understanding."

Matthos glanced at Alaric.

"Have you, Father?"

"I have asked Ser Moryn to take you into his service."

Moryn nodded.

"And I have agreed."

Matthos stood very still.

For a moment Orryn thought he might not speak at all.

Then the boy bent to one knee.

"I shall serve you faithfully, ser."

Moryn allowed him to remain there for a breath before taking his arm and helping him rise.

"I expect you will try. When you fail, which you will, I expect you to admit it and try again. You will obey the men appointed to instruct you, and you will remember that being Lord Meadows's heir does not entitle you to leave work for other boys."

"Yes, ser."

"You will dine with my household when your duties allow. You will learn its people and its business. I shall write to your father concerning your conduct, and he may write to me if there is anything he wishes me to know."

"Yes, ser."

"And the next time Ser Risley tells you to walk a horse, you will do so before spending half an hour wondering whether I am watching."

Risley looked satisfied.

Matthos reddened.

"I will, ser."

Moryn gave a small nod.

"Then welcome."

Alaric drew his son aside. He said something quietly, with a hand upon the back of Matthos's neck. Orryn could not hear the words. Matthos answered, and Alaric held him for a moment by both shoulders.

Orryn looked away, toward the open stable.

The smell of warm horses carried over the yard. A groom was scraping mud from a hoof, and one of the younger squires had begun a quarrel with a boy over a missing leather strap. There was nothing remarkable about the morning. Yet when Orryn looked back, Matthos stood beside Moryn rather than his father.

Lady Melessa spoke with Alaric about the boy's belongings. Moryn called to a squire in green, an older youth with a narrow face, and told him to show Matthos where the household's armour and tack were kept.

"He's to join us," Moryn said.

The older boy looked Matthos over.

"Have you a saddle of your own?"

"Yes."

"Where is it?"

"With my father's horses."

"Then we'd better fetch it. Bring your harness too."

Matthos glanced at Alaric, waiting for permission.

"Go on," his father said.

The two boys walked toward the stables.

Orryn found himself following without quite deciding to. Alaric had not yet called him back, and he wanted to see the place where Matthos would keep his things.

The older squire introduced himself as Will, and explained which of the tackrooms belonged to Moryn's household. The saddles hung on pegs in a long row. Some were richly worked, others plain and patched, and nearly all bore marks Orryn could not read.

"That one belongs to Ser Moryn," Will said, pointing to a dark saddle. "Don't touch it unless you're told. And if they give you the grey gelding to exercise, mind his left hind foot. He kicks."

"Does Ser Moryn ride him often?" Matthos asked.

"When he's sound. Which is less often than he used to."

Will led them past a pair of squires polishing stirrups.

"This is where you'll work in the mornings. If Ser Moryn is riding, you'll attend him. If he isn't, someone will tell you what needs doing. We take turns carrying the water. Don't leave it to the younger boys."

"I wouldn't," Matthos said.

Will glanced at him.

"Most say that."

Orryn thought the boy spoke much as the older squires at Grassfield had spoken to him when he first arrived.

They collected Matthos's travelling saddle from the Meadows horses. Orryn helped carry the bags, while Matthos insisted upon taking the saddle himself. It was heavy enough that he had to shift it twice before they reached the rack.

Will watched without offering assistance.

When the work was finished, he said he would return later and went to find another boy.

For a little while Orryn and Matthos were alone beneath the stable roof.

Matthos looked at the rows of saddles and folded horse blankets.

"I suppose this is mine now."

"Some of it," Orryn said.

"Not the grey gelding."

"No."

Matthos laughed, then sat upon an empty chest.

"I thought Father might choose Ser Olyvar."

"So did I."

"Would you have?"

Orryn considered.

"He seemed a good knight."

"He's a better rider than most men I've seen."

"Perhaps."

"And Lord Roxton serves with him. They speak of going to the Marches and inspecting the garrisons. I'd have liked to see that."

Orryn leaned against a post.

"You may still go places with Ser Moryn."

"I know."

Matthos pulled at a loose thread upon his sleeve.

"Did Father say why?"

"He said he had already asked Moryn."

"That can't be all."

"No."

They were quiet.

Outside, men were calling for two horses to be brought out. A bucket scraped across the stones.

Matthos looked up.

"Do you think Father chose wrong?"

Orryn had not expected him to ask.

"I don't know."

"You always say that."

"Not always."

"Often enough."

Orryn looked at the saddle Matthos had just hung upon its peg. The leather at one corner was worn, though Lady Jeyne had insisted upon having it cleaned before the journey.

"Ser Moryn seemed pleased to have you."

"He did."

"And he knows Lady Ashford."

"I know that too."

Matthos fell silent again.

At last he said, "I should have liked to choose for myself."

Orryn could think of no answer that would make that possible.

He remembered the afternoon his father had spoken of service at Grassfield. It had been an honour, everyone said, and he had been proud. He had not been offered two households and asked which he preferred.

"I expect most boys would," he said.

Matthos regarded him, then gave a short laugh.

"You'd probably choose the one with the largest books."

"I would choose the one where they fed me."

"Then you should serve Lord Leo. He has more food than anyone I've ever seen."

"More than you could eat?"

"Not that much."

They both laughed.

The sound made the stable seem smaller, almost familiar.

A horn sounded somewhere beyond the walls, followed by the clatter of hooves.

Matthos rose.

"I ought to find Will. He said he'd show me where to put my other things."

Orryn stepped away from the post.

"Then I should go back to Lord Alaric."

For a moment neither moved.

They had shared a chamber at Grassfield, ridden the same roads, quarrelled over duties and listened to the same orders. Orryn had often wished Matthos would leave him in peace.

Now he found it strange that the boy could turn down another passage without being expected to follow.

"Tell Father I'll see him at supper," Matthos said.

"I will."

"And don't tell him what I said about Ser Olyvar."

"Which part?"

Matthos gave him a weary look.

"You know."

Orryn thought of Ser Lucan's old warning that a thing might be true and still not be yours to say.

"I won't."

Matthos nodded and went to find his new companions.

Orryn returned through the stable yard alone.

He passed a groom arguing over oats, a servant carrying fresh linen, and two knights discussing whether their lord would permit them to ride south before the harvest. In the court above, a clerk was hurrying toward the administrative chambers with a roll of parchment beneath his arm.

A little earlier, Alaric had spoken with one Tyrell brother about roads and another about men who might march upon them. He had asked a third to take his son. All three had listened, and none had wanted precisely the same thing.

Orryn wondered whether Lord Alaric had known that before they came.

He suspected he had.

At the gate to the guest court, he looked back toward the sound of the practice yard. A fresh burst of laughter rose over the wall, and he could not tell whether Matthos was among those laughing.

He went to find his lord.
