Orryn had his hand on the latch before he knew he meant to stay.

The iron was cold under his green fingers. Behind him the pen scratched once and stopped, and out on the stair he heard the page's bare feet slapping upward, answering the bell. All the while Haverill the reeve was being taken apart, Orryn had been composing this, and it had been a good speech, reasonable and exact, with figures in it. Now it had gone out of his head like water out of a cracked basin, and what was left was the plain fact that Col Pyke, the lame grafter, had asked him to carry a thing, and he had carried it, and was not finished.

He shut the door instead. It closed louder than he had meant, with the page still on the stair, and when he turned round Lord Alaric was looking at him, and the left foot had begun to tap.

"My lord," said Orryn. "If it please you. A moment." His voice went up at the end like a child's, and he hated it. "You said once that a correction should be made in private. With the door shut."

"I said so of corrections." The pen was set down. "Is this one?"

"No, my lord. I don't know what it is. A request."

"Requests are made before the bell is rung, Master Orryn, not after." Yet he did not ring it again, and outside, the page reached the top step to find the door closed and stood there, audibly, breathing. "Say it. In order."

He drew breath and felt the order come apart in his mouth. His tongue was dry as bast. "Col Pyke said he would sooner be asked than carried. I told you that. But it is not only a message, my lord, it is—it is whose it is to tell. And tomorrow, at the block—" He stopped. His palms were wet. He wiped them on his doublet and left two pale green smears, and Lord Alaric watched him do it without any expression whatever. "Might it be only to look at the trees? With Master Tull. And not the cottage, nor the widow, so that she has no cause to think it is about her before her son has told her that it is."

For the space of three taps of the foot Lord Alaric said nothing.

"You are asking me where I may walk on my own land."

"Yes, my lord. No." It came out wrong in both directions. "I am asking whether you will."

"Why?"

It was the question he had prepared for least, and he found he was almost grateful for it, because the answer was not in any speech. "Because he asked, my lord. And because you asked me for the count, and it was true, and Master Haverill looked at me. I would sooner nothing else were done to anyone that way, if it need not be."

"For Pyke, then, or for yourself?"

Orryn thought of a lie and could not find one that would be of any use. "Both, my lord. I think both."

"A man who asks for a thing for two reasons has commonly one good one and one he is ashamed of." It was said drily, almost to the blotter. The foot stopped. "Now consider what you have asked. The widow works her old row every day the Father sends, from first light, on a ladder, with the whole slope under her. I am to walk the lane below with Tull and the reeve and my squire at my heel, and she is not to see me do it. Do you propose I go by the wall?"

Orryn had not thought of the ladder. He had thought of the cottage, and of Col's long wary face, and the word carried; he had not thought of a woman thirty feet up a pear tree. The heat climbed his neck. "No, my lord. I did not—"

"No. You did not." It was not unkind. It was merely exact, and somehow worse than anger. "She will know by noon whatever is done. I have said so. What I may decide is the manner of it." The pen was lifted and laid in its tray with a small click. "I shall go to the block by the long way, round by the brook lane, with Tull and the reeve. I shall not go among the rows, nor near her ladder. I shall look from the lane, and I shall not speak to the woman or her son of the trees, whatever either asks. I do not promise she will not see me. I promise I will not go to her door." The hazel eyes held him. "And you will carry no more words to that man, tonight or before I have seen the trees. Is that understood?"

Orryn had been thinking, with a small cold lurch, of a back stair and a dark yard before dawn. It seemed the lord had barred it before he had found it. "Yes, my lord."

"You have had what you asked, and you will find it is less than you wished." Lord Alaric drew the ledger toward him. "If the woman comes to the lane, and I think she will, for she never was one to wait on a ladder, it is you she will ask, not I. You asked for it. You will answer it. Now open that door, Master Orryn. You are keeping a child on the stair."

The page was the same truthful boy with the crooked badge, and he looked at Orryn's face with frank interest as he came in. He was sent to Verrall for last autumn's tithe-barn book. "The whole of it," said Lord Alaric, "not the abstract." And Orryn, going out past him, remembered Haverill's voice saying Last autumn, my lord, and the hand that had seemed to come down across it, and understood that the east block was not the only matter that would be looked into tonight.

*

Supper had been called by two bells, and he came in after the second. Arriving late is foolish, and everyone at the squires' short table by the screens knew it, and nobody said so. Osric Dunmere, the senior squire, had said the grace without him, in his high exact voice, naming each of the Seven; the pale eyes lifted once as Orryn slid onto the bench and then went down to his trencher, and the silver star at his throat caught the candle. He did not need to say that he was bound to say so. It sat on the board among the salt.

Stewed pears, as always at this season, with cloves, from the windfall baskets behind the screens. The smell went to the back of Orryn's throat, where it met the green bark still on his hands and became something he could not swallow. Down the board Alyn Merryweather was pouring cloved wine from the flask under the bench, and Baldric Haye, at his elbow, was watching Orryn's untouched plate with the attention of a boy from a poor house, who noted anyone who left good food.

"Did you tell him?" Piers Fallow asked, low, tearing bread. He sat on Orryn's other side, stocky and dark-curled, with the green stain still to his wrists. "All of it?"

"All of it. Verrall first, and then him."

"And Haverill?"

"Sent for." Orryn looked at the pears. "And then I asked him for something."

Piers stopped with the bread in his fist. "After. The same night."

"Yes."

"Seven save us, Grandview." He said it without scorn, with a sort of awe, as of a boy who had stepped out on the brook ice in March. He pushed the bread across. "Eat that."

Orryn ate the bread. It was the only thing on the board that tasted of nothing, and he was grateful to it.

*

He slept badly, in the squires' chamber with its six pallets, and woke twice to the sound of Perkin Fenwick talking in his sleep. The ache in his ribs, which had gone quiet in the nursery, had come back to remind him it was there. Towards morning he lay with his eyes open and went through it as he went through everything, in order, and could not make it come out. Whichever way he counted there was some part that someone else must carry, and he had set it down on a shoulder, and did not know whose.

The bell for first light came thin and cold. He dressed in the dark, in Lem's shirt, still smelling of pear, and went out across the yard, where the cobbles were slick with dew and the sky over the east wall had gone the colour of a pewter plate.

The east gate stood open. Beyond it the land fell away to the brook in grey folds, with mist along the water, and the first light touched only the top of the ridge, where the pears stood in their fifty-two rows like a host not yet told to march. He smelled wet earth and the sour of windfalls, and heard the slow, sleepy hum of wasps in the grass.

Hugh Tull, who knew every tree in the block by its wounds, was waiting at the head of the lane on his upturned basket. Lord Alaric came a little after, afoot, with Lyman Verrall at one shoulder and Tolman Haverill a pace behind the other, thin and grey and wet to the knee, as though he had been among the trees since before light, with a tally-stick in his hand yet. He did not look at Orryn. Nobody told Orryn where to stand, so he stood where he could see.

They went by the long way. The brook lane ran along the foot of the block between a hedge and the water, and through the gaps in the hedge the rows climbed away overhead, grey and orderly, hung with small hard fruit. At the foot of the old white pear, where the lane came closest to the slope, Tull stopped and pointed with his stick, not at the tree but a little beside it, as a man points who has been told not to touch. "There, my lord. And there. Four. And the rust along the row."

Lord Alaric stood in the lane with his hands clasped behind him, and looked, for a long time, with the left foot tapping. Then, without turning his head: "Master Haverill. The count."

"Four with the heart gone, my lord." Haverill's voice shook, and he took hold of it as a man takes a rope. "Six with the rust on the leaf. Eleven rows downwind of them, on the brook side. I went out this morning to count them again." He held up the tally-stick, absurdly, as if it were a pledge. "It is the same."

"It is the same as Master Orryn's," said Lord Alaric, and no more, and Haverill's shoulders came down a very little.

High on the slope, the saw had stopped.

Orryn had been trying not to look, and now he looked. Maud Pyke, Col's mother, stood on her ladder against the old row with one hand on the limb and the saw hanging from the other, small against the pale sky and quite still. She had been singing, he remembered. The tuneless song had been coming down the hill the whole while, and he had not marked it until it ceased. She looked at the lord in the lane, and at Tull, and at the reeve with his tally-stick, and at the squire in the borrowed shirt; and then she hung the saw on its hook, and set her foot on the next rung down, and came down the ladder sideways, as she had for the pears.

She did not hurry. She walked down the slope between the rows, wiping her hands upon her skirt, until she reached the gap in the hedge ten paces from the lord, and there she stopped, as though the hedge were a wall, and bobbed her head. She did not look afraid. Orryn knew afraid; he had seen it in Haverill's sleeves. This was a woman who had buried a husband and raised a lame son and worked the same row of trees for longer than Orryn had been alive, and she looked at the lord of Grassfield as a woman does who has been sent for and not yet told why.

"My lord," said Maud Pyke. "It's a long while since you came down my lane."

"Goodwife Pyke," said Lord Alaric. That was all. He turned his eyes back to the dead tree and kept them there, and Orryn felt his belly drop like a missed stair, for the lord had meant it exactly. I shall not speak to the woman of the trees. He had promised nothing more, and he would say nothing more; and the one person in that lane who had been told that the answering was his was Orryn.

The wasps hummed. The mist was lifting off the brook. Maud Pyke's eyes came round, without haste, from the lord to the squire, and stayed there.

"Is it my trees, young master?" she said. "Or is it me?"

What does Orryn do?

1. Tell her the truth as far as he can: the old row is sick, the lord has come to see it, and her son knows more and means to tell her himself.

2. Say only that the lord has come to look at the trees, and beg her pardon for saying nothing more.

3. Ask Master Tull, who has known her longest, to answer her, and stand back.

4. Something else of your own.
