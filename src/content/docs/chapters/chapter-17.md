He knew how it was done. He had watched it all his life.

He had seen it at Grandview in the great hall, when Ser Perrin, a friend of the house, knelt on the dais before his lord uncle Alester with his hands clasped between the older man's, and spoke the words in a voice that carried to the gallery. He had seen it in the yard, in a dozen small ways: a squire handing a lord his gauntlets, a page holding a stirrup, a young knight with a rain-wet face saying I am your man and meaning it or not meaning it. He had never once been the one to say it. But his knees knew what to do.

He stood up from the stool, put the half-eaten bread down on the table, and went down on one knee on the bare boards, in front of Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep.

"My lord," he said. His voice was quite steady. It surprised him. "I am your squire. I will pay what I can for what the old knight has to give, and what I am given I will bring to you, and to no one else, until you tell me otherwise. I will keep faith with that."

He had not been told to say it. Nobody had told him to kneel. He had put his hands together before him, and then, not knowing what else to do with them, held them out, palms joined, as he had seen Ser Perrin do. He looked at the boards. A knot in the plank was shaped like a pear.

For a time nobody moved. Verrall, over by the tray, had stopped chewing.

Then Lord Alaric crossed the room and took Orryn's hands between his own. They were hard, dry hands, calloused along the edge of the palm where a sword-hilt sat, and the right one had a stain of ink beneath the nail of the first finger. They were cooler than Orryn had expected. They did not squeeze.

"That is the form of a squire's pledge," the lord said, "and you have said it correctly, which is more than I did at your age. Get up."

Orryn got up. His knee ached.

"I will tell you what I think of it, since you were honest with me." Alaric released his hands and stepped back, and clasped his own behind him again. "Swearing is cheap. I have taken a hundred oaths in this room, from men who meant them and from men who wished to be thought to. It is not the pledge that costs, it is the keeping of it on the day it is inconvenient. I will hold you to it on that day. Not before." He paused. "But I will say that I had rather a boy who knelt unasked than one who had to be shown how."

It was not praise. Orryn knew that, and found that he did not mind. It was a small warm coin handed over a counter, and he put it carefully away.

"Verrall. Pen and paper."

"My lord?"

"A letter. To Ser Wystan at the Pear and Plough. He is not a young man, and I do not mean to send my squire and a cart of grain to knock on a Reach inn-door and ask for a secret." He went to the tall desk, drew out the stool, and then, with a visible effort, did not sit on it. He pushed it toward Orryn instead. "You have a fair hand? Wat says you write well. Sit. I will say it, and you will set it down, and Verrall will check the sum when we are done."

So Orryn sat at the lord's own desk, with the lord's own pen in his fingers, and the single ruled sheet before him. His heart was going fast again. He dipped the nib and wrote the date at the top, small and neat, as Maester Aylmer had taught him, and waited.

Lord Alaric dictated the way he did everything, in short exact sentences, with a pause between each for the pen to catch up. To Ser Wystan, sworn sword to the late Lord Gawen Grandison, at the sign of the Pear and Plough, in the Reach. Greeting. Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep sends his respects, and begs the favour of his company. There were three more lines of the same, courteous and rather stiff, each as clean as a measured plank. Orryn wrote them down. His hand, after a first wobble, steadied and ran. He was almost happy. It was the plainest feeling in the world: to be given a task, and be able to do it, and to have a man stand over him who knew exactly what he wanted.

"Lord Alaric offers hospitality at Grassfield for as long as it is the old knight's pleasure," the lord went on, "with a chamber and a table, and a horse for the road if he has need, and for his trouble the sum of twelve silver stags, to be paid on arrival. The lodging at the inn to be settled by Grassfield, at four stags the night, for the five nights the knight has stayed there on his way, which comes to twenty."

Orryn's pen stopped.

He had not meant it to. It simply stopped, an inch above the paper, as a horse stops at a ditch it has just seen. He looked at what he had written. Four stags the night. Five nights. And the sum he had been told to set down, which was twenty.

Four stags, five nights: twenty. The sum was right. It was the five.

His stomach went cold. Ser Wystan had been on the bench with the cup in his hand and the dust of the road still on his boots and his mule tied at the post, like a man who has only just come in. Nobody had spoken of five nights. It was a figure that had come out of the lord's head in the same breath as the rest, and had gone into his voice as if it had always been there.

"My lord?" Verrall said gently. He had come up behind them. He was looking at the paper over Orryn's shoulder with the careful blankness of a man reading something he had already seen. "The inn was settled, if I recall, on the report. Wat's book had one night."

"Wat's book had one night for the squire," said Lord Alaric. His voice had not altered, but the room somehow had. "The old knight was there before. He had been there at least four nights when the cart came, Wat says. I did the figure."

"Wat's book says he came in the day before, my lord. Not four days." Verrall's tone was mild, almost apologetic, a man pointing out a loose thread. "I think the figure may have gone astray. It would be one night, or two: four stags, or eight. I thought you would wish to know before the fair copy."

Lord Alaric did not answer at once. He stood very straight, and a muscle moved in his lean cheek. Orryn, sitting at the lord's desk with the pen in his hand and the wet ink drying on a number that was wrong, understood quite clearly what he was looking at. It was not the arithmetic. It was a man who had said a thing aloud, in front of a boy and a servant, and was now deciding whether he had been wrong.

"I did the figure," Alaric said again, more quietly, "from Wat's account of the day."

Nobody said anything.

Orryn found that he could not be certain. He had stood in the yard that evening and heard the cart come in, and seen the old man's boots, and he was nearly sure it was the first night. Nearly. And he knew what he was sitting at: the lord's own desk, with his pen. And he knew, from the lord's face and from Verrall's careful voice, that whatever he did next would be remembered.

What does Orryn do?

1. Quietly say that the old knight looked as if he had only just arrived, so the figure is more likely four than twenty, and let Lord Alaric decide what to do with it.

2. Write the figure as dictated and say nothing, leaving it to Verrall to correct, so that Lord Alaric is never told by his squire that he was wrong.

3. Put down the pen and say that he must have heard the number wrong, and ask to hear it again, so that Lord Alaric can mend it himself.

4. Something else of your own.
