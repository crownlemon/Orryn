Nobody spoke for the first hour, and by the second Orryn had begun to understand that this was how it would be.

The cart had a bench for two, and three of them sat on it. The elder groom had the reins on the left, the younger sat on the right with a whip he never used, and Orryn was wedged between, with Lady Maris's bundle on his knees and his sword laid down along the footboard where it rattled on every stone. The covered back was packed with everything he owned and a good deal that he did not, since his mother had found room at the last for a pillow, a second loaf, and a small stone jar of her own plum preserve that she had said was for the journey and had looked at him as if he would know what she meant. The grey horse in the shafts walked with its head low. The road went down from Grandview's ridge in long bends through oak woods, and in the shade of the trees the air was nearly cool, and then the trees would open on a stubbled hayfield and the heat would fall on the cart's canvas like a lid.

He had looked back once, at the first bend, and seen the Old Lion's square top above the oaks with the black lion hanging on its staff. At the second bend there had been nothing but trees, and he had not looked again, because he had decided not to and because it was no use.

The two men beside him were Alaric Meadows's, and he knew them only as one knows a shut door. The elder was called Wat. He was perhaps fifty, brown and long in the face, with a grey stubble that he scraped at every second morning and a soft Reach way of dropping the ends of his words, so that cart came out car' and Grandview came out Gran'view. He had said "Master Orryn" to Orryn at the gate and nothing since, except to the horse. The younger was called Pate, and Orryn took him for seventeen at the outside: a narrow, sandy youth with big hands, a scab on one knuckle, and an air of listening to something behind his ears. He had looked at Orryn exactly twice. Both times it had been at the sword.

That was all he had. He had spent the first mile making conversation in his head and the second discarding it. They were Alaric's men, and a man's household took its manners from the man; Steffon had told him that, and Steffon was usually right. If Lord Alaric's grooms did not talk to a stranger on the road, it was likely because Lord Alaric did not like his grooms to talk, and that was a fact about Lord Alaric, and Orryn put it away with the others. Numbered paragraphs. Third hour and not before. No horse.

At midday Wat drew up in the shade of a roadside elm, where a spring ran out of a mossed stone into a trough, and the three of them ate. Orryn unwrapped his mother's bundle on the footboard. There was a good deal more of it than he could finish: the loaf, the wheel of cheese, the honey cakes in a cloth gone translucent with the heat. He cut the cheese with his knife, because he had no notion of what else to do, and held out a piece to each of them.

Pate looked at Wat. Wat took his piece, turned it over, and said, "Obliged, Master Orryn."

"There are honey cakes," said Orryn. "Eight. I can't eat eight."

"Aye," said Wat, "you could, but it'd go ill with you," and the corner of his mouth moved. It was not a smile, but it was the nearest thing to one that Orryn had seen from either of them. Pate ate his cake in two bites, without chewing, and then very carefully licked the honey from his scabbed knuckle, as if he had been told not to waste it.

They watered the grey horse and went on. The road came down out of the oak woods into the valley of a slow brown river, and the country opened. It was good land, he thought, wheat in the lower fields gone the colour of old brass, and hedgerows heavy with dust, and here and there a farmstead with a thatched barn and a dog that ran out to bark at the wheels. He knew that land only as a name. His father had ridden this way once to see a man about a mill, and had spoken of it afterward in his flat voice as a place where the rent came late and the drainage was poor. To Orryn it looked like the end of a rope. Every hour, the farms were a little fewer and the hedges a little less tended, and he understood that this was not yet strange, only unfamiliar, which is another thing, and that the strange part would begin where the road stopped being Stormlands.

He asked Wat, in the middle of the afternoon, how far they were from the border.

"Sedge ford's an hour," said Wat. "Then three miles on, there's a stone. Then it's the Reach." He seemed to consider whether this was enough. "Lord Alaric's bridge is the other side. Half a day past that."

"Sedge ford," said Orryn. "Is that a keep?"

"A ford, Master Orryn." And that was all.

-----

He smelled the water before he saw it, a green, trodden, muddy smell that came up the slope on the back of the wind, and then the road came round a stand of alder and he saw Sedgeford.

It was a river crossing, nothing more, though somebody had been at some pains to make it more. The brown river that had followed them down the valley widened here into a shallow reach of gravel and slow water, perhaps eighty yards across, and on the near bank, where the road went down into it, stood a timber house on stone footings with a slate roof and a flag. The flag was yellow, and across it a bird stood on one leg: a heron, black, with its long neck bent back in a hunting curve and its beak laid along its breast.

Orryn knew it. His father had made him learn the devices within a day's ride of Grandview, setting the cards down on the table in the little room off the steward's court and turning them over one by one like a man dealing a hand he did not like. A black heron on yellow. Sedgley, of Sedgeford, a landed knight's house and a very small one, sworn to the Penroses of Parchments, as Parchments was sworn to Storm's End. Steffon had said something dry about it. Orryn tried to recall what, and found only the shape of the remark, a sort of shrug in the voice. Stands in the water and waits for something to come by. He thought that was it.

A rope was stretched across the road at the water's edge, a thick hempen cable slung between two posts, a little above the height of a horse's knee. It sagged in the middle. On the far side of it, under the shade of the toll-house eave, a bench stood out on the gravel, and on the bench sat a man with a book.

He rose when the cart came down the slope. He was thin and stooped, in a yellow tabard with a heron stitched on the breast and ink on his fingers, and he opened the book on his forearm as a man opens a door he has opened ten thousand times. There were six more, Orryn saw, sitting or standing in the shade or on the shingle: soldiers in leather jacks and yellow, with spears leaned against the wall and the incurious look of men who had been told to be bored. One of them was eating a plum. None of them had stood up.

Wat drew rein ten feet short of the rope. The grey horse stopped as if it had been asked a question it knew the answer to.

"Cart and horse, Reach-bound," said the thin man, to the air a little above their heads. "Three persons. State your lord and your errand."

"Meadows of Grassfield," said Wat, in his low voice. "Going home with a boy."

"Meadows." The thin man wrote it. "Two stags for the cart and horse, and a penny a head. That's two stags and three pennies, and the book will be signed."

Orryn had never heard of a toll at Sedgeford. He had not heard of a toll on the west road at all, and he had a flash of clear indignation, followed at once by the thought that this was a feeling he should keep to himself until he understood what was owed to whom. Two stags was the price of a good ewe. Three pennies was a loaf, at Grandview's prices. Neither was ruin. It was the principle he did not like, which he told himself was not a very useful thing to have.

"His lordship's purse is for the road," said Wat.

"This is the road," said the thin man, patiently.

"His lordship's purse is for his own road."

The thin man looked at him, then at the horse, then at the cart, and the pen stopped. "I'll fetch the master," he said, with the satisfaction of a man who has been looking forward to it.

Pate's hand tightened on the whip. Wat, beside Orryn, did not move at all.

The master of Sedgeford came out of the toll-house with a cup in his hand, still chewing.

He was a big man, in his middle forties, not tall but wide, with a barrel chest and a heavy brown beard in which a crumb of bread was caught. His brown hair was thinning at the crown, and he wore a yellow surcoat much laundered over a mail shirt that had been worn thin at the shoulders, so that it looked less like armour than like a habit. A heron was sewn on his breast, black on yellow, the stitches slightly crooked. He had a broad, high-coloured face, with small bright eyes buried in it, and a way of looking at the cart that was entirely and openly pleased.

"Meadows," he said. "Meadows' green, and the Meadows horse. Of course. I thought I knew the grey." He raised his cup, and drank, and wiped his beard with the back of his wrist. "Ser Osmund Sedgley, of Sedgeford, my lord's castellan of this crossing and its master in all but name, for the Penroses hold it of the king's lord at Storm's End and I hold it of the Penroses, and so on, back to the Conqueror's horse, I dare say. Who's this?"

"The boy, Ser," said Wat.

"I see the boy." The small eyes came to Orryn and examined him, the face, the golden hair under its travel-dust, the sword along the footboard. "He's not Meadows. Reach boys don't cut their hair like that. What are you, lad? Stormlander by the look. Whose?"

Orryn said, "Orryn Grandison, Ser. Of Grandview."

He had meant to say it quietly. It came out clearer than he wanted, in the way of a name said aloud in a place where it has not been said, and Ser Osmund Sedgley's eyebrows went up and stayed up.

"Grandview," he said, savouring it. "Alester's brother's boy? Steffon's? I know Steffon. A man who counts the pennies on the pillow." He glanced round at his men, who had at last begun to take an interest. "Well. Here's a thing, Harl. Here's a Grandison on a Reach cart, going into the Reach. Wasn't there a wedding?"

"Penrose, Ser," said the thin man.

"Penrose. That's right. My lord's nephew's marrying Grandview's girl." He turned back to Orryn, delighted. "Tied at last. Those two houses have been a long time a-tying. Which was it, Penrose and Grandview, that picked the winning side at Redgrass? I always forget." He waited a moment, for the effect. "A joke, lad. Don't look like that. I had it from my lord Penrose's own mouth that Grandview was prudent."

Some of the men laughed, and the one with the plum spat the stone onto the gravel. Orryn felt his ears go hot. He did not know what Ser Osmund meant by it, or rather he knew very well what he meant, and did not know what a person ought to do about it. It had been said in front of two grooms and six soldiers and a clerk, at a ford, by a man whose business it was to stand in the road. It was not an insult he could answer. It had not been quite delivered as one.

"We'd be obliged to cross, Ser," said Wat quietly.

"Of course you would." Ser Osmund took another mouthful from his cup. "And you shall, once the book's signed. A custom of this ford, Master Grandison, for two hundred years and more, and a good one. Any wagon bound west out of the Stormlands in Reach livery pays a stag a wheel-pair and a stag the horse, and a penny a man; and Stormland carts in Stormland colours go over free, being ours. It keeps the road in order. It keeps the ford. I've a bridge to build one of these years, and my lord Penrose to pay his due, and eleven men to feed, and the Reach has never sent so much as a barrel of cider for the privilege." He smiled. It was a broad, good-humoured smile, and Orryn found he could not entirely dislike it. "It isn't the money. It's the principle. One doesn't let a thing slide."

"I thought it wasn't the money," Orryn said, before he could stop himself.

"It isn't," Ser Osmund agreed warmly. "That's why I want it."

A silence followed. The river ran. Out on the gravel a kingfisher flicked from one bank to the other like a blue thread, and a soldier at the back put down his plum and picked up his spear, not hurriedly, only as one does when the afternoon begins to promise something. The rope hung in the road between the posts. It was only a rope. It would have taken Pate a single stroke of a knife to cut it, and a horse and cart no time at all to be through; and it came to Orryn, with the tidiness of a sum, that Ser Osmund's men did not stand beside it because they feared he would try. They stood beside it so that he would know that he had.

He glanced at Wat. The elder groom was looking straight ahead, over the grey horse's ears, at nothing, with an expression of such studied blankness that it was almost a kind of speech. His hands were quite still on the reins. On Orryn's other side, Pate had stopped listening to whatever lay behind his ears and was looking at Orryn instead, directly, for the third time. His eyes were pale and not unkind. They were waiting.

It came to Orryn then, in the heat and the green mud smell, that nobody on that cart was going to speak for him.

It was not a refusal. It was only the shape of things. Wat had said what he had been given leave to say: his lordship's purse is for the road. Whatever was to be said beyond that was not Wat's to say, and Pate was not sent to say anything. They were Alaric's men, and they had been told to fetch a boy. They had been told to fetch him exactly. Nobody had told them what to do when the boy was stopped at a ford by a man with a book. Whether that was a gap in Lord Alaric's orders, or the first of his questions, Orryn could not tell.

I shall find out how well.

He had no purse. He had a sword and a letter and a pillow, and a name that had just been taken apart in front of strangers with great good humour, and the name was not a coin here. The Meadows green was Alaric's. The Grandison name had been worth a nod and a joke. He could pay, if Wat would pay for him, out of Alaric's purse; or ask Wat to, in a voice that others would hear. He could argue the custom, which a man who knew it better would win. He could say the name of Lord Alaric Meadows in a loud voice and see what it bought, or what it cost. He could sit on the bench between two silent men and watch the shadow of the toll-house climb the gravel.

Ser Osmund Sedgley waited, one foot on the bench, with his cup in his hand and the good humour still on his face. He did not look hurried. He had stood in the water a long time, Orryn thought. He knew what it was to wait for something to come by.

"Well, Master Grandison?" he said. "The book's open. What shall I write?"
