The shutters had not yet been opened when Orryn woke.

Something had disturbed the loft. Not a voice, nor the stamping of the horses below, but the quieter sound of a man trying not to disturb anyone: a foot settling upon an old floorboard, a buckle put down upon wood, water poured into a basin.

Orryn lay on his side with his face toward the wall. The blanket had slipped from one shoulder during the night, and the straw beneath his mattress pricked his arm. He was about to turn over when he remembered the two grooms.

One of them was awake.

He moved his head a little.

The younger groom stood at the basin by the narrow window. He had opened the shutters just far enough to admit a grey blade of light and had set his folded clothes upon the chest. His shirt lay beside them. The man bent to wash, straightened and rubbed his face with both hands, sending drops of water down his neck and over the bare skin of his chest.

Orryn looked away.

Then he looked again.

The groom was not especially tall. He had the shoulders of a man who lifted heavy things, a small mark below his collarbone and forearms browned more deeply than the rest of him. As he reached for the cloth the muscles in his arm shifted beneath the skin. Orryn could see where a leather strap had rubbed a pale line across one shoulder.

He knew the mark. Harness and baggage straps made such marks, and a badly fitted breastplate could leave worse. There was nothing remarkable about it.

The older groom slept on, his breathing deep and even.

The younger man drew the cloth across his neck and rinsed it. A little water splashed onto the boards. He wiped it away with his foot, then returned to washing without looking toward the beds.

Orryn had watched men wash often enough. At Grassfield the squires did it together, sometimes in such a hurry that the basin was overturned and everyone went without. He had helped Alyn fetch water from the yard more mornings than he could count.

Alyn had always been untidy at rising. He left one boot beneath the bed, the other beside the door, and spoke of both as though somebody had stolen them. His hair was chestnut, and longer than the groom's. His shoulders were narrower too.

Orryn had not meant to compare them.

He found himself trying to remember whether Alyn bore a mark on his collarbone. He could remember Alyn's hands, his grin, the way he stood with one hip against a table when he was talking. He could remember the exact sound of his laughter when something delighted him.

That made Orryn uneasy.

The groom reached for his shirt.

For a moment Orryn wished he would take longer.

He was startled by the wish, though nobody had heard it. He lowered his eyes to the coarse edge of the blanket and lay very still until the rustling of linen had ceased.

When he looked again, the man was dressed. He lifted the strap of his travelling bag, examined a worn buckle and worked it through a fresh hole. His attention was so entirely upon the strap that Orryn felt foolish for having devoted so much of his own to him.

At last the groom took up his boots and went toward the stairs, carrying them to avoid waking the others.

The door shut softly.

Orryn remained where he was.

In the dimness he could still picture Alyn at the edge of the orchard, smiling as if there were no consequence to anything he did. He could remember the weight of Alyn's hand upon his shoulder on the day he left.

He did not know why one stranger at a washbasin should bring back so much of somebody he already missed.

The older groom snored once, turned upon his bed and settled again.

Orryn lay awake until it was light enough to distinguish the joints between the boards. Then he rose without a word, dressed and went down to the stable.

*

The innkeeper's daughter had put a basket of bread upon the table in the yard, with cheese wrapped in a cloth and a jug of watered ale. The stable boy was making an earnest attempt to brush the packhorse while the animal leaned its whole weight against him.

Orryn took the brush.

“Not there,” he said. “He'll step on you.”

The stable boy relinquished it with relief.

The packhorse had been difficult since Bitterbridge. Its temper was not improved by the smith's attentions the day before, though the loose shoe had held through the night. Orryn examined the hoof, found nothing amiss and gave the horse a piece of crust.

“That's why he leans,” said the younger groom.

Orryn looked up.

The man from the loft had come across the yard carrying a folded saddlecloth. He spoke as if they had been discussing horses all morning.

“Because you feed him after he behaves badly.”

“He would lean whether I fed him or not,” Orryn answered.

“Aye. But now he has a reason.”

The groom went on toward the tack room.

Orryn watched him go, then remembered the brush in his hand.

He bent to the horse again.

By the time Lord Alaric came down, Ser Bertram had saddled his own mount and was arguing with the innkeeper over the price of the rooms.

“You said the loft was included.”

“The loft was included for two men.”

“You made no objection when there were three.”

“I didn't know there were three until my daughter counted the blankets.”

“Do you charge for the blankets as well?”

“Not unless you take them.”

Alaric intervened before Ser Bertram could reply.

“Pay him,” he said. “And ask for the change.”

“What change?” the innkeeper demanded.

“The change from a reasonable price to the one you have named.”

The innkeeper stared at him, then laughed and returned two pennies.

Ser Bertram looked pleased with the outcome.

Orryn thought of the farrier and said nothing.

Matthos came out wearing his good riding cloak, though the morning was already warm.

“Father, shall I need this when we arrive?”

“Certainly, if you mean to be boiled alive.”

“Jeyne said I ought to make a proper appearance.”

“Your mother said you ought to wear it when we are presented. Fold it until then.”

Matthos gave the cloak to Orryn.

Orryn looked at him.

“It's not mine to carry,” he said.

“You have the packhorse.”

“So do you.”

“I'm not riding the packhorse.”

“You may put it in the bags yourself.”

Matthos looked ready to argue, but Alaric was watching.

He folded the cloak badly and fastened it beneath his own saddle.

The party set off before the heat became severe.

The road climbed through groves of fruit trees and broad fields divided by hedges. Beyond them lay a low country of villages, mills and prosperous farms. The houses nearest the road had tiled roofs rather than thatch, and the yards were enclosed with walls of pale stone. Every mile brought more carts travelling in the opposite direction, some piled with timber and grain, others with wine casks, baskets of fruit or lengths of cloth.

A merchant with a dozen mules passed them carrying narrow barrels marked with a bunch of grapes. Farther on they overtook a group of armed riders escorting a closed carriage. A painted shield hung at the carriage door: a green field bearing a golden tree.

“Rowan,” Matthos said at once.

Orryn knew the arms from Maester Perrin's roll.

The riders made way when they saw Alaric's banner, but one of them regarded the company with a look that lingered upon Matthos.

“His lordship rides to Highgarden?” he asked.

“I do,” Alaric answered.

“Then we wish you good fortune upon the road.”

The man bowed in the saddle and let them pass.

Matthos rode close to his father afterward.

“Were they from Goldengrove?”

“They wore Rowan colours.”

“Do you think Ser Moryn knows them?”

“Quite possibly.”

“Then perhaps they know him.”

Alaric gave a short laugh.

“That is generally how acquaintance works.”

Matthos smiled, uncertain whether the remark had been made at his expense.

Orryn looked back once. The Rowan escort had fallen some way behind, though the carriage remained visible between the hedges.

Lady Brenna had spoken of Rowan at breakfast as if Goldengrove were a place at the other end of a letter. Now its men were on the same road, riding toward the same castle.

He wondered whether those men would recognise Matthos when he wore Highgarden's livery.

He wondered whether they had already begun to remember his father's name.

*

As the day grew warmer the country became greener.

The Mander appeared and disappeared beyond the orchards, broad enough at one bend that Orryn mistook a distant pleasure boat for a house floating upon the water. The banks were cultivated close to the edge, with willow trees and landing stages where men unloaded baskets from shallow boats.

They crossed a small stream over a stone bridge. A toll collector stood beside it beneath a painted board, but he waved Alaric's party through after examining the banner.

“Does he not charge lords?” Orryn asked Ser Bertram.

“Sometimes.”

“Why not us?”

“Because he was told not to.”

“By whom?”

“Ask him on the way back.”

It was the sort of answer Ser Bertram gave when he knew the matter was more complicated than he cared to explain.

Orryn glanced behind them at the collector.

Two carts laden with sacks had stopped at the bridge. A man was holding up a folded parchment and pointing toward the painted board. The collector shook his head.

Orryn wondered what privilege the parchment claimed.

He could not hear their words, and the road carried him on.

By noon the traffic had thickened enough that Alaric was obliged to slow his party behind a wagon transporting carved stone. Three men walked beside the load, watching the wheels whenever the road dipped. The wagon was drawn by six oxen, and its driver cursed every incline as though he believed the earth had been arranged to inconvenience him.

“What are they building?” Matthos asked.

“Something heavy,” Ser Bertram said.

The answer made Orryn laugh.

Matthos did not.

They took their midday bread beneath a stand of trees overlooking the river. An old woman sold pears from a basket at the roadside. Alaric purchased a handful, then complained that they were hard as stones.

“They will soften, my lord,” she told him.

“Before or after I lose my teeth?”

“Before, if you have good teeth.”

Ser Bertram took two.

Orryn ate one despite its hardness and found the flesh sweet beneath the bitter skin.

The sun had passed its height when the road rose again. This time, beyond the trees, he saw pale stone high upon a green hill.

He straightened in the saddle.

There were walls, towers and roofs, so many that for a moment he could not tell where the castle began. One ring of white stone climbed above another. Tall, round towers stood among older square ones, and the highest walls were crowned with banners that stirred in the wind.

Below the castle the Mander curved through the valley, carrying boats whose sails flashed white in the afternoon light.

Matthos gave a shout.

“Highgarden!”

Alaric turned in the saddle.

“Keep your seat, boy. It isn't likely to run away.”

But Orryn had scarcely heard him.

The closer they rode, the larger the hill became. He could see gardens terraced above the outer road, groves of trees among the walls and long banks of flowering shrubs, pink and white and yellow. The castle appeared to have grown out of the hill rather than been placed upon it.

He had seen Grandview standing above its stony country, and Grassfield Keep with its square towers and busy lower gate. Bitterbridge had astonished him with its traffic and river.

Highgarden made all three seem far away.

He looked for the golden roses Perkin had claimed the knights wore upon their armour, but the moving figures upon the walls were too small to distinguish. He saw banners, though: gold upon green, bright against the pale stone.

Then the road turned, and for a little while the walls disappeared behind an orchard.

Orryn felt absurdly disappointed.

“Still there,” said Ser Bertram, who had seen him looking.

“I know, ser.”

“Good. I've known men ride straight into the hedge because they kept staring at it.”

Orryn watched the road.

When they came round the orchard, Highgarden stood before them again, nearer than before.

*

The outer gate was not difficult to find.

The road led toward it through a broad approach lined with low walls and planting beds. There were horsemen, merchants, servants, petitioners and several parties of noble visitors, all moving at different speeds toward the same opening.

Guards in green cloaks stood beneath the arch. Above them the golden rose of House Tyrell flew beside the banners of the Reach.

A clerk at a table was recording names.

Orryn dismounted when Ser Bertram told him to and went to the packhorse. A groom came to take the animal, but he kept hold of the reins until the man showed him a brass token stamped with a rose.

“Your name?” the groom asked.

“Orryn Grandison.”

“The horse's.”

“It hasn't one that I know of.”

“Then we'll call him Packhorse.”

Orryn was not certain whether the man was joking.

Behind him Alaric had given his name to the clerk.

“Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep. My son Matthos, Ser Bertram Corliss and my squire, Orryn Grandison of Grandview.”

The clerk repeated the names as he wrote.

“And your purpose, my lord?”

“I have come to pay my respects to Lord Leo and to present my son for service with Ser Moryn Tyrell.”

The clerk's pen moved.

“Service with Ser Moryn,” he said.

Matthos looked pleased.

Alaric added, “Provided Ser Moryn is willing.”

“Of course, my lord.”

The clerk wrote again, though Orryn could not see whether he changed anything.

Another official approached carrying a narrow board covered in notes. He examined Alaric's letters and bowed.

“Lord Meadows. You are expected. His lordship has been told of your arrival.”

“Which lordship?”

“Lord Leo, my lord.”

Alaric looked surprised.

“He has?”

“Yes. He will receive you presently, if you will follow me.”

The official gestured toward an archway inside the gate.

Orryn glanced at the packhorse.

“Go,” Ser Bertram said. “I'll see to these.”

Orryn returned to Alaric's side.

They passed into a wide court beyond the outer wall.

It was larger than the whole lower yard at Grassfield. Carriages stood beneath a long covered arcade, and stablemen led horses toward buildings at the far end. A fountain played in the centre of the paving, with water running down the sides of a stone basin worn smooth by years of use.

A party bearing the grape clusters of House Redwyne was unloading chests from a wagon.

On the other side of the yard, three men in Rowan colours waited beside their horses. One held a folded paper and was speaking with considerable impatience to a steward.

“Those chambers were promised,” Orryn heard him say.

The steward answered something too quietly for him to catch.

“The Arbor has half the west range already,” the Rowan man replied. “Will they have the other half before supper?”

A Redwyne servant glanced toward them.

The man holding the paper noticed and lowered his voice.

Orryn would have liked to hear more, but the Tyrell official had already led Alaric beneath the next arch.

The passage opened upon a garden enclosed by white walls.

There were roses growing along the columns, pale flowers gathered so thickly that their scent mingled with the smell of damp stone. Water passed through a narrow channel beside the path and disappeared beneath a little carved bridge.

A group of men stood near the far side of the garden, beside a low table set with cups.

One of them turned at their approach.

Orryn knew him at once.

Not because he had ever seen the man before, but because all the others seemed to make room when he moved.

He was slender and silver-haired, with a neatly kept grey beard and a straight back. His doublet was green, worked with gold about the collar, and he wore no armour. An old sword hung at his hip, its scabbard polished by use.

He was smiling.

“Alaric Meadows.”

Lord Leo Tyrell came forward before Alaric had finished bowing.

“Seven save us, man. Have you forgotten the road to Highgarden?”

“My lord.”

“Don't ‘my lord’ me as though we are strangers. Come here.”

He took Alaric by the arms and held him at a distance, inspecting him.

“You have grown thinner.”

“It has been a dry year.”

“It has been a dry year everywhere. That is no reason to let a good Reach lord waste away.”

“My household feeds me well enough.”

“Then your servants deserve better wine.”

One of the knights beside the table laughed.

Orryn found himself smiling before he remembered to lower his eyes.

Leo released Alaric.

“I had word from Armond that you meant to come. He writes a good hand for a man who spends so much of his time telling me he dislikes letters. How did you find Bitterbridge?”

“Prosperous, my lord.”

“It always is when Armond is complaining. And my daughter?”

“Lady Brenna was in good health.”

“Was she working?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Then she must have been well. I have never known that girl to keep a book closed when there was a debt to be found inside it.”

His smile softened.

“And Jeyne? Does she keep you in order?”

“As well as any man could expect.”

“Then she is an improvement upon her father.”

Alaric laughed.

Leo's eyes moved to Matthos.

“And this is the boy?”

“My son and heir, Matthos Meadows.”

Matthos came forward and knelt.

“Rise, lad. Let me look at you.”

Matthos stood.

He had put on the good cloak from his saddle. It was green, fastened at the throat with a little silver clasp, and the heat of the afternoon had already reddened his face.

“You have your father's eyes,” Leo said. “Has he brought you here to learn your manners?”

“To serve Ser Moryn, my lord. If he will have me.”

“Ah, my Moryn.”

Leo looked at Alaric.

“You mean to put him among my son's knights?”

“If Ser Moryn finds him suitable.”

“He'll find him a horse and ask whether he can keep his seat, I should think. The rest may be learned. How old are you, Matthos?”

“Fourteen, my lord.”

Orryn looked at him.

Matthos had begun telling people he was fourteen soon after his last nameday, though Orryn still sometimes thought of him as thirteen. At least today he had answered without a boast.

Leo nodded.

“Old enough to learn, young enough to be forgiven what he gets wrong. A fortunate age.”

Matthos brightened.

“Can you use a lance?” Leo asked.

“A practice lance, my lord.”

“Good. I have never cared for boys who claim to ride better than the horses can carry them. They'll break their necks proving it.”

He turned to Alaric again.

“Moryn shall know you are here. He has his own business this afternoon, but you need not chase him through the yards. I keep him somewhere within these walls, though sometimes I wonder where.”

There was laughter from the attendants.

Alaric inclined his head.

“I am grateful, my lord.”

Leo's attention shifted once more, this time to Orryn.

“And who is this? He has been standing so quietly that I might take him for a particularly solemn page.”

Alaric turned.

“My squire, Orryn Grandison of Grandview. My kinsman through my aunt Ellyn.”

Orryn stepped forward and knelt.

His knee struck a little stone caught between the paving slabs. He hoped nobody had heard it.

“A Grandison,” Leo said.

Orryn raised his eyes.

The old lord was studying him with interest, though not the severe interest of a master-at-arms judging a boy's stance.

“From the Stormlands?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And serving Meadows. Then the storm has come among the roses after all.”

A few men laughed.

Orryn was not sure whether he was expected to answer.

Leo appeared to notice.

“Are they treating you well at Grassfield, lad?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Do they work you?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Do they feed you?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Then Alaric has made a good beginning. I shall ask again at supper, and perhaps you'll be brave enough to contradict him.”

Orryn could feel the heat in his cheeks.

Alaric smiled.

“Grandison is honest, my lord, sometimes to the inconvenience of those who employ him.”

“An unfortunate quality in a courtier,” Leo said. “A useful one in a squire.”

He rested a hand briefly upon Orryn's shoulder before turning away.

The touch was light. Orryn scarcely felt it through his tunic.

Yet he stood straighter afterward.

Leo beckoned toward the table.

“Come, Alaric. Have something cold before your journey becomes a heroic tale. You shall have chambers prepared, and I will hear what you have brought from Grassfield when you've rested.”

Alaric followed him.

Orryn remained close enough to attend, though he found himself looking at the rose-covered columns, the water channel and the servants waiting beyond the arch.

At Grassfield, Alaric might greet a visiting landed knight with wine and a seat by the hearth. At Bitterbridge, Armond had embraced his son-in-law before the company. Here Leo Tyrell, lord of all the Reach, had crossed a garden to welcome one of his bannermen as though Alaric were a guest he had been impatient to see.

Orryn remembered Armond's certainty that Leo could settle the quarrel between Rowan and Redwyne.

He understood the confidence a little better now.

But the Rowan men in the outer court had not looked content, and neither had the steward speaking to them.

*

The refreshments lasted longer than Orryn expected.

Leo wished to know about the drought, the roads near Grassy Vale and whether Alaric had seen any improvement in the river's flow after the last rains. Alaric answered carefully. There had been little rain, and what had fallen was unevenly distributed.

Leo asked whether the orchards had borne fruit.

“Enough to make us grateful,” Alaric said. “Not enough to make us rich.”

“A condition shared by most of my friends.”

He asked after the Fossoways of Cider Hall and whether the arrangement for Leonette remained agreeable.

“It does,” Alaric answered.

“Good. They make better husbands than their apples suggest.”

Orryn knew the Fossoway arms and wondered whether there was a joke he had missed.

The old lord did not pursue it.

He turned to a knight at his side to ask whether the day's riding had been completed. The man reported that several young horses had been exercised and that one had thrown its rider into a ditch.

“Did he break anything?”

“His pride, my lord.”

“Then have him ride it again tomorrow.”

Leo laughed and reached for his cup.

The knight looked relieved.

From where Orryn stood, Highgarden's lord seemed to know everything he wished to know and little that he did not. He gave instructions without appearing to give them, and men moved before he had finished speaking.

It reminded Orryn of something his grandfather Gawen's old sworn sword had once told him: a lord's strength was not always in the number of swords he commanded, but in the men who understood what he wanted before he spoke.

He could not remember Ser Wystan's exact words, and perhaps the old man had said something quite different. The thought remained.

A page brought a message written upon a narrow sheet.

Leo read it, made a face and handed it back.

“Tell them I shall hear the matter tomorrow.”

The page bowed and withdrew.

Alaric glanced toward the letter.

“Business, my lord?”

“Always business. The trouble with having a large house is that everybody expects you to attend to it.”

“And your sons?”

“They attend to quite enough without my help.”

Leo smiled, but Orryn thought the smile had altered a little.

“Garth will wish to see you before you leave,” he added. “He has taken a great interest in the eastern roads and the obligations of the houses that hold them.”

“I had meant to pay him my respects.”

“Good. He will make sure you have paid them in full.”

The old lord laughed at his own joke.

Alaric laughed too.

Orryn thought of Brenna's letter to Garth's officers, and of the old tolls of Bitterbridge. He looked toward his lord, but Alaric was drinking.

Leo set down his cup.

“And now I have kept you standing long enough. You must have your chambers. We shall speak again this evening if the Seven permit me to escape my own stewards.”

He rose.

Alaric and the others bowed.

The old lord stopped beside Matthos.

“Remember what I said about the horse, boy. It will not know you are a lord's son.”

“No, my lord.”

“And neither will the ground when you fall.”

Matthos managed a smile.

Leo went away through an arch with his attendants following.

For a few moments the garden seemed unusually quiet.

Then servants began moving again, collecting cups and carrying trays.

A household officer approached.

“If it please you, Lord Meadows, your chambers are prepared.”

Alaric followed him.

Orryn went after.

*

They were lodged in the inner part of the castle, though not in the range nearest the great hall.

The officer explained that visiting lords often stayed in the garden court when Highgarden was crowded. From its upper gallery one could reach the chapel, the outer walks and the approach to the great hall without crossing the servants' yards.

Orryn tried to remember the route.

There were turns beneath high arches, two flights of steps and a passage opening onto a courtyard where a fountain trickled beneath an old stone figure holding a horn of plenty.

He knew by the time they reached their rooms that he could not have found his way back without assistance.

Alaric's chamber was large, with glazed windows, a carved bed and a hearth that had not been lit. Beyond it lay a small closet for clothes and papers. Matthos was given a chamber across the passage, to be shared with another boy if the household required it.

Orryn had a narrow bed in the room adjoining Alaric's.

There was a washstand, a chest and a window looking onto a court where servants carried linen beneath a trellis of climbing roses.

It was a better room than the one he slept in at Grassfield.

He was not sure whether that pleased him.

“Grandison,” Alaric said from the larger chamber, “where are the papers?”

“In the leather case, my lord.”

“And the letter for Lord Leo?”

“Inside, beneath the one for Ser Moryn.”

“Good. Bring it here.”

Orryn fetched the case.

Alaric opened it and examined the seals.

“Nothing broken?”

“No, my lord.”

“Then put it beside the desk.”

Orryn obeyed.

His lord took off his travelling cloak and handed it to him.

“Have that aired. And see that Matthos has washed before supper. He looks as though he has ridden through every ditch between here and Bitterbridge.”

“Yes, my lord.”

Alaric paused.

“And get something to eat yourself. They feed their horses better than most men feed their guests here, but I believe they'll manage to find you bread.”

Orryn bowed and went out.

Matthos was already exploring the passage.

“Did you hear him?” he asked.

“Who?”

“Lord Leo.”

“I was standing beside you.”

“He said Ser Moryn would test my riding.”

“He said he might.”

“He asked if I could use a lance.”

“He did.”

Matthos looked toward the windows.

“I wonder where they keep the practice yard.”

“Someone will show you.”

“I'd rather find it.”

Orryn thought of the many turns they had made coming from the garden.

“Then you'd better ask someone who knows.”

Matthos looked at him with impatience.

“You're afraid of getting lost.”

“Yes.”

“Highgarden isn't a forest.”

Orryn remembered the walls, the gardens, the hedges and the passages.

“It might as well be.”

Matthos laughed.

“Come on, then.”

“Your father told me to see that you wash.”

“I can wash later.”

“He said before supper.”

“Then there is time.”

It was not Orryn's duty to follow Matthos wherever he went, but he could see no better way of preventing the boy from wandering into a part of the castle where he had no business.

They went together.

*

They reached the practice yard by following the sound of steel.

It was a broad enclosure beyond the stables, with a packed-earth floor and long weapon racks beneath a covered walk. More young men were gathered there than Orryn had ever seen training at once.

Some practised with wooden swords and shields. Others worked at the quintain, while older squires tended horses or carried armour toward a low building at the far end.

A master-at-arms was correcting a boy's guard.

“Again,” he said.

The boy struck.

“Not harder. Better.”

He struck again.

Matthos went straight to the railing.

Orryn remained a pace behind.

Two of the older squires were sparring with blunted swords. One wore a green surcoat bearing a small golden rose. The other had no badge Orryn could see. Both moved more quickly than the boys in Ser Everard's dawn drills, though neither fought with the ease Ser Androw Ashford had shown when he visited Grassfield.

The squire in green lost his footing and stumbled. His opponent struck his shoulder before he could recover.

“One,” the master-at-arms called.

The fallen boy swore.

Orryn thought of Perkin and the mornings when he himself had been too slow to remember which foot belonged behind the shield.

Matthos watched with shining eyes.

“They're good,” he whispered.

“Some of them.”

“Better than you.”

“Most of them.”

Matthos grinned.

A boy carrying a bundle of practice shields passed close enough to hear.

“Looking for someone?” he asked.

“Ser Moryn Tyrell,” Matthos answered.

The boy stopped.

“He isn't here.”

“I am Matthos Meadows, of Grassfield. My father has come to present me to him.”

The shield bearer shifted the weight in his arms.

“Then you'll be told when he wishes to see you.”

“Do you serve him?”

“No. I serve Ser Rycherd's cousin.”

“Which Ser Rycherd?”

The boy stared at Matthos.

“Lord Rowan's nephew. He's a knight.”

Orryn had not known there was a Rowan nephew here, and could not tell whether the boy was boasting of a connection or merely explaining it.

Matthos looked embarrassed.

“Oh.”

The shield bearer nodded toward the covered walk.

“If you want Moryn's men, they're usually down by the lower lists. But don't go asking every squire you meet. There are too many of us, and half will tell you lies for the pleasure of it.”

He went on with his shields.

Matthos watched him go.

“He was rude.”

“He told you where to find them.”

“He spoke as though I were stupid.”

Orryn remembered his own first days at Grassfield, when nobody had explained which knight slept where, which servants could be asked for things and which men were too busy to be interrupted.

“He didn't know who you were,” he said.

“I told him.”

“That didn't seem to help.”

Matthos glared at him.

Orryn returned his attention to the yard.

An older squire stood by the armoury with his sleeves rolled, holding a leather strap in one hand while another boy tightened a buckle upon a breastplate. Orryn found himself watching the easy way the men worked together. One said something, and the other laughed.

For a moment Alyn's face came to mind again.

Not the face he had worn when he left Grassfield, but his old careless smile, the one he gave whenever a dull task became amusing.

Orryn looked down at his hands.

The skin at the base of his fingers had thickened from reins and sword grips. A little cut beneath his thumb had opened again.

He pressed it with the edge of his sleeve.

“Are you coming?” Matthos demanded.

Orryn looked up.

“Where?”

“Back. I don't want Father to think I've been wandering.”

“You have been wandering.”

“Then don't tell him.”

Orryn followed him toward the arch.

He had promised no such thing.

*

The passage back to the guest court was not where Matthos expected it.

They passed one stair twice, then emerged beneath an arcade overlooking the broad outer enclosure. From there Orryn could see the lower part of the famous hedge labyrinth between two rings of walls. It looked less like a garden than a green fortress, with narrow paths disappearing between banks of briar thick enough to conceal a horse.

A gardener stood on a ladder trimming the upper growth. Below him two servants carried away baskets of cut branches.

“It's a maze,” Matthos said.

“I can see.”

“Do you suppose they let people go in?”

“Probably.”

“We should try it.”

“Not before supper.”

Matthos sighed as though Orryn had personally invented all the obligations of the household.

They continued along the gallery.

Voices came from a chamber with its door half open.

“…the lord of Goldengrove was promised his own men in the west range.”

“Then let Goldengrove send them to him. Those rooms are occupied.”

“By the Arbor's people.”

“By guests of Highgarden.”

Orryn recognised none of the speakers.

He slowed a little, but Matthos had already reached the stair.

“Come on.”

Orryn followed.

The argument went on behind them.

He wondered whether the Rowan man in the courtyard had found his chambers yet.

He also wondered whether Lord Leo knew that two of his guests' households were quarrelling over rooms while he sat among the roses.

Perhaps he did.

Perhaps that was why he had chosen to sit among the roses.

*

They found the guest court at last with the help of a maid carrying folded linen.

She directed them through a passage neither had noticed earlier, then watched until they turned the correct corner.

Matthos went to wash.

Orryn fetched water for Alaric and hung his travelling cloak near the open window. The air was still warm, but the sun had begun to lower beyond the garden walls.

He checked his lord's papers once more.

The letter to Leo lay beneath the one addressed to Moryn, just as he had left it. A smaller folded paper carried Garth's name. Orryn had seen Alaric add that one before they left Grassfield, but he did not know what was written inside.

A servant came to the door.

“For Lord Meadows.”

Orryn took the message.

It was a narrow slip bearing a green wax mark and a few lines of writing. He carried it to Alaric.

His lord read it and looked thoughtful.

“Who brought this?”

“A servant, my lord.”

“Did he say from whom?”

“No.”

Alaric held up the slip.

“It says the household officers will receive me tomorrow after the morning meal, if I wish to discuss the business I have brought from Grassfield.”

“Will you go?”

“I expect I must.”

“Is it from Ser Garth?”

“It is from his people.”

Orryn nodded.

Alaric folded the note.

“Lord Leo receives me himself, and Garth sends a clerk. That's Highgarden for you.”

He said it with a smile, though Orryn could not tell whether he was pleased.

“Ser Moryn has not sent for Matthos yet,” Orryn said.

“No.”

“He went to look at the practice yard.”

“I thought he might.”

“He wants to serve there very much.”

“I know.”

Alaric put the message beside the leather case.

“Do you?”

Orryn looked at him.

“Do I what, my lord?”

“Want to serve here?”

The question surprised him.

He had thought a great deal about Highgarden on the road, but he had never considered that he might remain.

“I serve you, my lord.”

“That was not the question.”

Orryn looked toward the open window.

Below, a servant was watering the roses in a stone basin. Another crossed the court carrying a tray of cups.

“It is a great place,” he said.

“It is.”

“And there are many knights.”

“There are.”

Orryn hesitated.

“I don't know them.”

Alaric regarded him for a moment.

“No. Neither do I know them all.”

He rose to wash.

“We shall have time enough to learn some names before we go. Bring me my blue doublet, Grandison. The one Marya insisted I take.”

Orryn fetched it.

*

That evening the great bells sounded across Highgarden.

Orryn heard them from the guest court while he was waiting for Alaric to finish dressing. Their voices came one after another from different towers, some deep and slow, others high enough to tremble in the air.

He could not tell which summoned the household to supper and which marked the hour.

A man passed beneath the gallery carrying a green banner rolled beneath his arm. Two boys followed with candle stands. Farther away, beyond an arch, someone was playing a pipe.

It had been a long day.

He had seen the Mander spread below Highgarden, the three climbing rings of white walls, the hedge labyrinth and more knights than he could remember. He had knelt before Lord Leo Tyrell and been spoken to kindly.

And he had heard men quarrel over rooms before they had even been assigned them.

Matthos emerged from his chamber in his good cloak, the clasp now polished.

“Father ready?”

“Nearly.”

“Do you think Ser Moryn will be at supper?”

“I don't know.”

“He might be.”

“He might.”

Matthos adjusted the cloak.

“I hope he is.”

Orryn looked past him toward the western gallery.

Somewhere within those walls were the knights Matthos hoped to serve, the clerks who kept Garth's accounts, the old lords who came to Moryn for friendship and the women whose letters might one day settle Elyce's marriage.

Orryn had met none of them yet.

He had met Leo.

That seemed enough for one day.

Alaric called for him from within the chamber.

“Grandison! My belt.”

Orryn went to fetch it.

As he buckled the sword about his lord's waist, he remembered the younger groom at the inn and the stillness of the loft before dawn. The memory felt strangely remote from the scented courtyards of Highgarden, though it had been no more than a day's ride away.

Then Alyn came into his thoughts again, as readily as ever.

Orryn tightened the belt one hole farther than he meant to.

“Not so fiercely,” Alaric said. “I should like to eat my supper.”

“Sorry, my lord.”

He loosened it.

Alaric took up his gloves.

“Come along. We mustn't keep the Longthorn waiting.”

Orryn followed him out.

The passage turned beneath an arch of carved roses, and beyond it the bells were still ringing.
