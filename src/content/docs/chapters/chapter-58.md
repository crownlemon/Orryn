Grassy Vale, 208 AC

Ser Bertram held out until the second noon.

They had stopped to water the horses at a well that had once fed a brook and now fed nothing: a stone ring in a field of yellow stubble, with a bucket on a rope and a boy of ten to draw it, who wanted a copper for every horse. Lord Alaric gave him two and did not argue the count. Orryn held Rook’s head while the dun drank, and listened to the bucket scrape bottom, and to Ser Bertram Corliss, who had spoken perhaps a dozen words since Bitterbridge and now wiped his mouth on the back of his glove and spent a dozen more.

“He’ll go over to Moryn.”

“Who will?” said Alaric. He was watching the boy, not the knight.

“Caswell. Who else? He sent a Tarly out of his hall on his ear and told his own lady wife to hold her tongue, and all for the pleasure of telling Ser Garth no.” Ser Bertram was six and forty, lean and grizzled, with a scarred cheek and a way of sitting a horse as though it owed him money. He was Lord Alaric’s household knight and Baldric Haye’s master, and the one man at Grassfield who had never troubled to hide that he thought the son a lesser lord than the father. “A man who does that is looking for somebody to say yes to him.”

“He told me as much at the mounting block,” said Alaric, “while you were quarrelling with the sumpter. He rides to Highgarden after the new moon. He means to hear Lord Leo and Ser Moryn both on what Lord Osmund did at Cress Hill.”

“Both.”

“Both. Those were his words.”

“They would be.” Ser Bertram spat into the dust. “It’s what a man says when he’s chosen already and wants the road to look long. Moryn will tell him a lord’s court is a lord’s own, and Lord Leo will tell him all is forgiven, and he’ll come home a Moryn man with a cask on his saddle and swear he heard both sides.”

Orryn waited for the argument. Lord Alaric argued with Ser Bertram as a matter of course, over horses and rations and the order of a march. But he only watched the boy haul up the bucket, and said “Likely,” in the voice of a man naming rain.

“My lord?” Orryn said, before he could stop himself.

“Oh, I think he goes, Grandison. I think he’s gone already, in his own head.” Alaric took the bucket from the boy and tipped it over his wrists and let it drip. “When I was nineteen I stood at a window in this vale and wondered whether Daemon’s banner might not be the right one to ride under. You know that much. Lord Armond, Lady Jeyne’s father, tells it to anyone who sits still long enough. And it was Armond who came down the road with Lady Brenna beside him and put me on a horse for the king. I was angry a year. After that I was grateful. Ever since, he has told me which way to ride.” He shook the water from his fingers. “I think I shall be sorry when he stops.”

“He’ll not stop for long,” said Ser Bertram. “He’ll find someone else to tell him.”

“That,” said Alaric, “is what I meant.”

They remounted. Ser Bertram let his horse fall back beside Rook, and said, to the air over Orryn’s head, “And what was that at the gate? Tell Jeyne, and then nothing. A woman doesn’t stop for no reason.”

“She gave me the whole of it on the wall, the night before,” said Alaric, “and found she could not say it twice, with Armond looking. It’s this. She will help her mother look for a proper husband for Elyce.”

“Proper.” Ser Bertram turned the word over like a coin he suspected. “Kind of her.”

“Yes.”

“Kind,” said Ser Bertram again, and put his heel to his horse, and went on ahead.

Orryn rode on a while with the word. Proper. He tried it as help, and it sat well enough: Armond’s young second wife, a Tyrell with a Tyrell mother, offering what she had to her stepdaughter’s girl. Then he tried it as the other thing, and that sat well enough too. He had watched Lady Brenna cut her mutton into very small pieces. Lady Jeyne spoke of her stepmother, when she spoke of her, the way she spoke of weather, as a thing one dressed for. A kindness from Brenna might be a kindness. It might be a hand laid on Elyce’s marriage that was not Jeyne’s, and meant to be felt. He could not tell, and by evening he had stopped trying.

It was not Brenna that he thought of on the second afternoon.

He did not mean to think of Joffrey Caswell, Lord Armond’s son, the boy with the lute. It happened as the sun happened, a little at a time and then all over him. The gallery, with the river going dark in the window. The bench so narrow that a shoulder had rested against his own, and he had been very careful not to think about it, and had thought of nothing else. The cup taken out of his hand without asking, and drunk from, and given back. You stayed. And at the last, from the window above the gate, a thin pale figure leaning out with something across his arm, and a hand lifted.

Joffrey was fifteen, a year the elder, and a year had done things to him that it had not yet done to Orryn. His voice had broken and settled, a dry low thing that went where it was told. Orryn’s still went where it liked. With Alyn he had always known the name of what he felt, and had hated knowing it. With this he had no name at all. He did not know whether it was the same thing grown smaller, or some other thing altogether, or only that someone had looked at him as he looked at others and had not looked away. That was what he kept turning over, there on the dun’s back with the dust in his teeth. Not the shoulder. The not looking away.

And ahead of him, on the bay, the back of Lord Alaric’s neck, brown with the road.

There was the other thing, too, the one he had carried since Highgarden like a coin sewn into a hem. Lord Alaric had never spoken of the terrace at Highgarden, or the widow in his arms there, and Orryn had never asked. But on the night after, Alaric had sat on the edge of the bed with one boot off and talked to him as a man talks to a man: about the wine, about the dancing, about how a lord of thirty is called a greybeard by the boys and a boy by the greybeards. He had laughed at Orryn’s answers. Orryn had been laughed at often in fourteen years, and never as though his answers were worth the laughing. He wanted that again. He wanted it badly, and some part of him knew how little of the wanting was proper to a squire.

Lady Meriel Vyrwel was a name he would say to no one. Not Lady Jeyne, not the squires’ hall, not Alaric himself. It was not his to say. He had settled that at the arch, and he settled it again each night since.

*

They came into Grassy Vale on the third day, in the heat of the afternoon, by the ridge road. From the top of the ridge Orryn saw what the dry had done while they were gone.

The orchards lay below in their even rows, and they were grey. The dust had settled on the leaves until the whole east side looked as though it had been brushed with flour. The brook ran in the white throat of its own bed, the stones bare and bleached and the mud between them cracked into plates. The wheel at the lower mill hung still, its paddles furred with old green. Past it the keep sat on its low rise, tawny walls and slate roofs and the squat tower of the sept, and the banner on the gate hung straight down as though it had been nailed.

Lady Jeyne Meadows did not come down the steps for guests. She stood at the top and let them climb. Orryn had noticed it his first day at Grassfield, and had spent a year noticing everything else about her: that she was eight and twenty and looked less; that her dark chestnut hair was always netted and her brown eyes never doing what her mouth was doing; that she had praised him three times in a year, and he could have told you the days. She was the most pleasant woman Orryn had ever been afraid of.

Today she came down.

Elyce was before her, as she was before everyone. She was twelve, Lord Alaric’s youngest, with soft brown hair and wide hazel eyes and freckles across her cheeks, and she was held to be the sweetest child in the keep. Orryn had been a year at Grassfield and had begun to suspect that everyone meant something different by it. She ran across the yard with her skirts in her fists and did not look at her father at all.

“Where is Matthos?”

“At Highgarden, my lady. He’s well.” Orryn slid down from the dun. “He bids me tell you that you’re not to ride Brindle. He says you can’t hold him.”

“I can so hold him.”

“He said you’d say that.”

“Then he might have said something kinder.” She looked at him with great suspicion. “Is he wearing green? Does he look foolish?”

“He looks well.”

“Foolish, then,” said Elyce, satisfied.

“Elyce,” said Lady Jeyne, and Elyce was quiet. She had not raised her voice. She had come down the last of the steps with Marya on one side of her and Septa Lenora on the other, and she walked past her daughter and across the dust to her husband as he swung down from the bay.

Marya Caswell was thirty, neat and plain, in a gown that had been good twice before: Lady Jeyne’s cousin from the poor side of the Caswells, and her shadow. Septa Lenora was narrow and straight, with grey hair under a plain veil and a crystal at her breast. She had been Lady Jeyne’s governess before she was anyone’s septa, and she was the only woman at Grassfield Orryn had ever seen stop Elyce crying by looking at her.

“My lord,” said Lady Jeyne. “You will want to wash. But before you go in, I should like you to hear a thing from me, and not from the kitchen.”

“Is it Matthos?” He had gone quite still. “Has there been a rider?”

“There has been nothing. Give me your hands.”

He gave them. She took them in hers, the dust and the rein-marks and all, and held them. She spoke in her ordinary voice, as though she were naming the guests for supper.

“I am with child.”

A groom crossing the yard with a bucket stopped where he was. Orryn heard water slop on stone.

“You are—” said Lord Alaric.

“Before the year turns, by Maester Perrin’s reckoning and my own.”

“Since when?” He stopped. “You did not write.”

“A letter passes through too many hands, my lord.” She did not glance toward the maester’s window, which Orryn thought was a sort of glance. “I wished to say it to your face.”

Orryn had seen Lord Alaric angry, and shamed, and tired past speech. He had not seen this. It came up from somewhere under the ribs and broke across his face like a boy’s. He stood there with her hands in his, as if someone had handed him a cup too full to carry, and then he laughed, loud enough to startle the pigeons off the gate-tower. He lifted her off the ground, in his own yard, before his own grooms, and Lady Jeyne said, “Alaric,” in a voice that would have stopped a horse. He set her down and kissed her hand, and then, because that was not enough, her mouth.

A groom grinned. Septa Lenora’s lips went thin, as though kisses were a thing to be scheduled. Marya Caswell’s face did not change at all, and Orryn understood from that that she had known for a moon.

“Is it a boy?” said Elyce. “I want a sister.”

“You will have what the Seven send,” said Lady Jeyne, “and be grateful for it.”

Orryn found that he was smiling. He was glad. That was the plain truth, and he held on to it, because it was true, and because the other thing had come up under it whole, with no warning: the torch, the arch, the pale green of her gown, the back of Alaric’s head bent down. Lady Jeyne’s hands were in her husband’s. He looked at Rook’s ear until it was only an ear.

“Grandison!” Alaric was looking at him over his wife’s head, bright-faced and shameless. “Did you know?”

“No, my lord.”

“Nor did I! Nor did anyone!”

“No one knew,” said Lady Jeyne, “which is the only way I have ever found to keep a thing.”

Orryn’s stomach dropped a very little. It rose again. She had not looked at him. There was no reason in the world that she should.

“Take the horses,” said Lord Alaric, laughing still. “Take them slowly, Grandison. I am told I am to be sat down.”

*

The long stable was the coolest place in Grassfield, and that was not saying much. The air under the rafters hung thick and brown, with flies in it, and a smell of old straw and horses sweating in their stalls. Orryn led the bay and the dun down the aisle with the dust still on his teeth, and found that he was not the only person in the stable who had come to look at animals.

Ser Wyman Latham stood at the end of the aisle with a halter over his shoulder. He was the master of horse and hawks: forty-two, lean and weathered, with a close beard and big calloused hands that went very still around any animal, and rather less still around any man. He was the best horseman in the keep and the squires adored him. Beside him, with one hand on a stall door and the other holding her skirts out of the straw, stood Lady Leonette Meadows.

“You’re back,” she said. “Good. I want somebody who’ll tell me the truth. Ser Wyman only tells me which one he likes.”

She was fourteen, Lord Alaric’s eldest, and she had her mother’s dark chestnut hair and green-brown eyes and a lively, practised smile that she had never in a year aimed at Orryn. She wore a yellow gown that had not been made for a stable. Her father had promised her a horse for her dowry, to ride to her husband’s house on, and every day of the dry she had walked the long stable to choose one.

“I’m to say if it’s the white or the grey,” said Orryn.

“You’re to say which one I look best on, and then Ser Wyman is to say why I can’t have her.”

“The white mare is lame in the near fore, my lady,” said Ser Wyman patiently. “She’s been lame since the second moon. The grey is sound. The bay has a mouth like iron and the sorrel bites.”

“I don’t want a horse that’s sound. I want one that looks well when someone holds it by the bridle at the sept door.” She stepped back and looked at the white mare, head on one side, as she might have looked at a ribbon. She did not touch it. Orryn had noticed that she never touched any of them, except to see how they stood beside her gown. “What did Matthos say?”

“He says Elyce is not to ride Brindle.”

“She’ll ride him the moment he’s out of sight. She always does.” Leonette’s eyes came round to him. They were sharp, and they missed very little, and she used them like a cook’s boy with a skewer. “Did you see my father dance? Marya says he dances when he’s drunk.”

“Lord Alaric danced at Highgarden, my lady.”

“With whom?”

“A great many people.”

“You’re a very poor liar, Grandison, and I think it’s your best quality.” She laughed, a quick bright thing that did not want an answer. “No matter. I’ll have it out of Marya. She tells me everything she tells my mother, only slower.”

Orryn thought her the most spoiled creature in Grassy Vale. He thought it plainly, without heat, as one thinks of the weather. He also thought, uncomfortably, that she was the cleverest person in the stable.

“I’m to be married out of this keep within the year,” she told the grey mare, and then him, since the grey mare did not answer. “My lady mother says so, if the Footlys can agree on a day. They can’t. Lord Bennard says one, and Lady Catrin says another, and Lady Ysella says any day will do so long as it is not hers.”

“Lord Bennard is Ser Garlan’s father?”

“He’s the stout one who agrees with whoever spoke last, and goes out afterward to look at his roads so that he needn’t hear the answer. And Lady Catrin is the one who is a Lothston.” She said it in a high flat voice, and Orryn knew it was somebody else’s. “She told me before she told me her son’s age. She says Harrenhal at him the way our septa says the Seven. You’re meant to be afraid.”

“Are you?”

“Of a woman who wears her castle like a necklace?” Leonette considered the grey mare. “I’m going to be her daughter. I’ll find out what she’s afraid of, and then I shall know.” She turned her head. “Ser Wyman, is that horse sound?”

“As a bell, my lady.”

“I’ll have her, then.” She said it as though she were doing the grey mare a kindness. Ser Wyman, who had known from the start which horse she would take and why, only bowed and went to find a halter.

“What are the rest of them like?” said Orryn. He had not meant to ask.

“The Footlys?” She seemed pleased to be asked. “Ser Lucan is the uncle and talks about walls. Lady Ysella is twenty-eight and thinks she should have been born the son, and looked at my gown at the betrothal the way you look at a horse you’ve been told is going to somebody else. And Lady Mara is the little one, who’s seventeen, and sweet as milk and about as clever, and is to be a septa. I like her. One can’t help it. It won’t matter.”

“And Ser Garlan?”

“Ser Garlan is kind to everyone.” She said it as a charge. “Which means to no one. He looks at you like a good dog. If you dropped a plate, he’d say it was the plate’s fault and mean it. He has never once in his life said anything he did not mean, and nothing he has said has ever been worth the meaning.” She said it fast, and flat, and for a moment she was not smiling at all. “He sent me gloves.”

“They were good gloves.”

“Everything he does is good.” She picked a straw from her sleeve. “Did you hear anything of Longtable, Grandison, while you were gone?”

It was lightly asked. Orryn kept his face still, which was no great skill and which he had been practising since the herb garden. “No, my lady.”

“Nothing?”

“Lord Alaric’s squire hears nothing that isn’t for the squires’ yard.”

“That was Mother’s voice.” But she had stopped smiling. For the length of a breath he saw what was under it, a girl he would rather not have known, who had been kissed in a herb garden by someone who was not coming back. Then the smile came on again, like a glove. “No matter. He’s well, or nobody’s said. I’ll have the grey, I think. It goes with the yellow.”

*

It was told that night in the solar, to all of them at once, with the shutters open on the dust and one branch of candles on the table. Lord Alaric had meant to give it to Verrall in the morning, a piece at a time. Lady Jeyne had other ideas.

The room was made for four, and held ten. Lady Jeyne sat in the carved chair by the window, where Alaric had put her and where she had let him, with her hands folded over a bodice that had been let out at the seams. Orryn saw it now, and could not stop seeing it. Marya Caswell sat on a stool at her elbow. Septa Lenora had her needlework by the other window and gave no sign that she was listening, and Orryn did not believe it.

Lyman Verrall, the steward, sat nearest the candles with his sleek sandy head bent and ink on his cuffs and a smile ready. He was four and forty and the best-humoured man at Grassfield, and he had been kind to Orryn from the first day, in a way Orryn had begun to understand was a habit, like a good seat on a horse. Maester Perrin stood by the door, thin and stooped, with a long nose and a grey beard trimmed to a point, the links of his chain clicking when he turned. Piers said he would answer any question put to him, and a few that had not been. Ser Everard Blythe, the master-at-arms, had found a cup before he found a chair; he was tall and silver-haired and had once been handsome, and he had taught Orryn the proper way to fall off a horse. Ser Wyman sat on the end of a bench with his hands between his knees. Ser Lucan Marwick, who was Osric’s master, held himself as if his collar were a duty. And Ser Bertram stood against the wall with his arms folded, still in the dust of three days’ riding, like a man who had been brought as a witness and meant to be one.

Orryn stood behind Lord Alaric’s chair with the wine. Nobody had told him to go. Nobody told him he might stay.

“Highgarden,” said Lord Alaric.

He had washed and put on a clean shirt, and he stood with his back to the empty hearth, meaning it to be quick. “Lord Leo is well, and feeds every man who sits down, and has a hundred things to forgive. He has three sons and cannot say no to any of them. Ser Garth wants roads and wants the grain on them counted. Ser Moryn wants friends. Ser Olyvar wants a war, and has not the spears for one. Matthos is with Ser Moryn, and will learn to sit a horse. He will learn to dice as well. I have told him not to.”

Ser Everard laughed into his cup. “They never listen, my lord.”

“No.”

“And the grain?” said Verrall.

“Ser Garth means to send Highgarden’s grain out under his own seal, ten carts to a string, two spears to a cart, let out at a fixed share where any lord on the road can watch it go. It is the first plan in the Reach I have heard that has a number in it. I told Lord Osmund Leygood, the Justiciar, that I would give grain in a dry year and guard his carts on my own side of the road. I did not give him my court.”

“A man who guards another man’s carts,” said Ser Lucan, “is that man’s man, my lord.”

“A man who guards a cart guards a cart, Ser Lucan.”

“As you say.”

It was how Ser Lucan said everything: as though orders were moral law, and Alaric’s the commonest kind. Maester Perrin said nothing at all, which Orryn had learned to take as a sign that he was listening hardest.

“And Bitterbridge, my lord,” said Lady Jeyne.

“Lord Armond is well. He sends his love, and wants it said that the Mander is lower than in his father’s time. A Tarly knight rode in on Ser Olyvar’s business, asking for ten spears and the bridge. Lord Armond said no to both.”

“And more than that,” said Ser Bertram, from the wall.

Lady Jeyne turned her head. She did it slowly, as a person turns who has been waiting. “Ser Bertram?”

He told it flatly, and with something nearer pleasure than Orryn would have liked to name. He told it from the beginning. “The young knight called Lord Alaric Ser Moryn’s man, my lady. At the board, before Lord Armond and Lady Brenna and the household. Said Moryn at least leaves a lord his own court. Said he’d sooner follow him, as Lord Alaric had done.”

The candles burned straight up in the still air. Verrall’s smile did not move, but Orryn saw it become a thing he was holding up. Ser Everard said, “Ha,” softly, into his cup. Ser Lucan nodded once, as at an offence confirmed. Septa Lenora’s needle had stopped.

“And what did my husband answer?” said Lady Jeyne.

“That he was his own man.” Ser Bertram said it exactly, with no my lord in it, as he might have given back a thing he disliked. “‘My own. It is a small side.’”

“Small is true,” said Lady Jeyne.

“A man who has a side of his own is a rare thing, my lady,” Verrall said warmly, and turned his head to Alaric. “It does mean one has to feed him oneself.”

“And I have a message,” said Lord Alaric, “from Lady Brenna to Lady Jeyne. She gave it me on the wall, and began it again at the gate. She will help her mother look for a proper husband for Elyce.”

It went round the room like a draught. Marya Caswell’s eyes dropped to her lap.

“How kind,” said Lady Jeyne.

She said it so lightly that Orryn could have sworn there was nothing in it at all. Then she added, “My stepmother has been a great help to me, in her time,” and he was no longer sure.

“My father will be a Moryn man by the next moon,” said Lady Jeyne, to all of them, in the voice in which she gave out the keys. “He will not say so, and he will believe that he has not. He has never in his life stayed angry with a man who fed him. Every road from this keep to Highgarden goes over his bridge, and the people on the far side of it are Ser Moryn’s friends. As for Longtable.” She turned the ring on her finger. “Lady Odessa wrote to me at every turn of the moon until Ser Alyn went. She has not written since. Lord Merryweather faces whichever way the last guest was facing, and his lady has put her daughter’s marriage under Ashford, and Ashford’s aunt is Ser Moryn’s wife. When Longtable chooses, it will not be asking Grassfield.”

“My lady has a clear head for the Mander lords,” said Verrall.

“I have a father. It comes to the same thing.”

Nobody laughed. Ser Wyman said quietly that the horses had begun on the thatch of the lower stable, and Lady Jeyne said, “We have no hay,” and let that be the end of it.

“You will hear it said in the market,” said Lord Alaric. He had not moved from the hearth. “You will hear that I am Moryn’s man, or Garth’s, or Lord Leo’s. Let them say it. Say what you saw and nothing after it. This house stands as it did. We give grain to the reeve who comes to ask for it. We keep our own quarrels. We send our spears when Lord Leo calls, and not before.” He looked at each of them in turn. “That is all.”

They went out one by one, the knights first, then the maester, then Verrall, and Septa Lenora gathering her work. Marya Caswell lingered a moment at the door, and Lady Jeyne said, “Marya,” not unkindly, and she went.

Then there was only the candle, and the three of them, and Orryn with the jug.

“You asked,” said Lady Jeyne.

Alaric did not pretend. “I asked.”

“Lady Arlanna. Before I had given you leave.”

“Before.”

“I see.” She sat quite straight in the carved chair with her hands on her belly, and looked not at him but at the dark window. “Then it is asked. I shall not ask it twice. Tell me what she said, all of it, in the order she said it.”

“She said she would look. She named no one. She said there were houses that kept their old friends, with sons whose mothers were tired of them.”

“That’s Arlanna. She will look until Elyce is eighteen, and then find someone who is tired of his mother.” Jeyne turned the ring again. “Brenna’s help will be of use, then. Whether I want it or not. It is a hand on Elyce’s marriage, and it is not mine. She means it kindly, or she means me to see her mean it. Or both. She has never in her life sent a thing that could be read only one way.”

“I thought so,” said Alaric.

“I know you did. You thought so with your whole face, at the gate.” She looked at him at last. “I shall be brought to bed before the year turns, and I will not be brought to bed in a house that has chosen a side by accident.”

“It is not an accident. I chose it. Mine. I would sooner he ate Grassfield bread than Moryn’s.”

“Bread.” She said it flatly. “There is a brook that runs at night, my lord. We shall see whose bread it is by the year’s end.” She turned her eyes, briefly, to the boy standing behind her husband’s chair, and then away. “Master Grandison is very quiet.”

“I told him to be,” said Alaric.

“He has been very quiet before, I think. He is a boy who hears everything and says nothing. He has kept worse for me.”

Orryn felt Alaric look at him, a half beat, a glance, as one looks at a thing one has set down and must remember to pick up. He held the jug quite steady. He thought of a herb garden, and of what he had been told to keep, and of another thing, which no one had told him to keep and which he would keep anyway.

“Master Grandison will want his bed,” said Lady Jeyne.

“Not yet.” Alaric had picked up his cloak from the chair. “I want him to walk down to the mill with me. Hob opens the stop at dark.”

*

They went down by the brook lane with no light but the moon, between the grey rows of the orchard, with the dust rising under their boots and settling again. Lord Alaric walked fast, as he always did, with his cloak over his arm, and for a long way he said nothing. Orryn kept half a step behind. Then the lane opened on the mill-pool, a bowl of cracked clay with a green puddle at the bottom of it, and Alaric sat down on the bank with his boots in the dry weeds and said, “Sit, Grandison. You look like a heron on one leg.”

Orryn sat.

“Four,” said Alaric, to the pool. “I thought I was done with being afraid of babies. I held Matthos the first time as if he were a wedge of cheese. Leonette cried every time I came into the room until she was three.” He laughed, short and quiet. “Did you see her? In the yard, before every groom in the vale. Fifteen years, and she has never once told me anything where I could answer. A hall. The sept. A yard full of people. She waits until I cannot shout.”

“Did you want to shout, my lord?”

“I wanted to do something. I’m told one mustn’t do it in front of grooms.” He shook his head. “A woman will tell you the most important thing in the world in front of as many people as she can find, Orryn. Remember that. Seven help you if she’s ever angry in a hall.”

He had not said Grandison. Orryn did not remark it. He sat with his arms round his knees and the heat of the day going out of the clay under him, and he laughed, because it was funny, and because Alaric had said it the way one says a thing to a friend, with his shoulder half turned and his eyes on the pool.

“Bertram said it exactly,” said Alaric. “My own. It is a small side. Like a man reading out my debts.”

“He said it exactly, my lord.”

“He would.” Alaric picked up a pebble and turned it. “He’s right, though. It’s very small. It’s me and a keep and a brook that runs at night, and a wife who has told me, in front of everyone, that she’ll see whose bread it is by the year’s end.” He did not seem unhappy. “You were quiet in the solar.”

“You told me to be.”

“I didn’t tell you anything on the road home. You were quiet then, too. What are you thinking about, down there at the back of the column, with your mouth shut?”

Orryn looked at the pool. He was thinking of a lute, and of a terrace. He felt his face go warm in the dark.

“Matthos, my lord,” he said.

“Aye.” It was the right answer, he saw, or near enough. “So am I.”

They sat. A bird called once in the orchard, and stopped. Lord Alaric’s face in the moonlight was young, and loose with a gladness that did not ask what it cost. Orryn thought of the arch and did not ask either. It was not his to give, and he would not give it; the rest he could take as it was offered, a man in a dry vale with a child coming, laughing at the dark.

Then he heard the water.

He heard it before he saw it, a small dry whisper in the stones upstream, as though the bed were clearing its throat. Hob Marsh had opened the stop. Orryn saw the lantern a long way up, a yellow dot bobbing, and one of the miller’s sons with it. Then the whisper grew, and there was a smell that he had forgotten, cool and green, and the first thin sheet of it came down over the white stones in the moonlight, no more than a hand deep, and spread into the mill-pool and sank into the clay.

“There,” said Lord Alaric. “That’s all of it.”

It was not much. It went into the pool and turned the puddle slowly brown. But it was moving, and as they climbed back up the lane toward the keep, with the sound of it behind them and Alaric’s step beside his own, Orryn did not ask where it was going.
