He meant to say that the lord had come to look at the trees. It was the small true thing, and he had the first words of it ready in his mouth. He saw Maud Pyke's weathered face turned up to him in the thin light, waiting without any hurry for whatever a lord's boy had to say to her, and he thought of Col setting down his bundle so carefully, as a man sets down something that might break. He thought of the lame man's voice saying that's mine to do.

"It's the trees, goodwife," said Orryn. "The old row. It's sick."

It was out before he could stop it, and once the first of it was out the rest came after, like pears from a split basket. He could hear that it was in the wrong order. Four trees with the heart gone, he said, and six with the rust on the leaf, and eleven rows below where the wind took it off the brook; Master Tull had shown them, and he had counted it for the lord, and the lord had come to see. "And Col knows," he said. "He has known since spring. He cut out the worst of it and burned it and put clay on the wounds, and he didn't want you told before the leaves were down. He said he would tell you himself. He said it was his to—"

He stopped, because that was the one thing he had been asked to carry and the one thing he had just let fall. It lay in the lane between them. Nobody picked it up.

The wasps hummed in the grass. High on the slope a pear dropped, and bumped, and was still.

"Since spring," said Maud Pyke.

She said it flatly, as one says a number, and her face did not change much. Her hands, which had been wiping themselves on her skirt, were still. "There was smoke from the block one wet morning in April," she said. "I took it for prunings. I thought it was early for it." And then, to the air, without any heat at all: "He is a close lad. He never would be asked."

She looked up the slope at her old row, at the grey limbs and the small hard fruit, slowly, tree by tree, as though counting; and Orryn understood that she was finding the four.

"Goodwife—" said Hugh Tull.

"Hugh Tull." She turned the whole of her body to him, as if it were a door. "Thirty years I've known you, and you put it in a boy's hands before you put it in mine."

"I put it in the reeve's, Maud, in the spring." The old man did not look away from her, though the stick in his hand had begun to shake a little. "He sat on it. Col asked me to hold my tongue to you and I held it. I was not sure I was right to." He drew a breath. "I am not sure now."

"No," she said. "I don't suppose you are."

She looked at Tolman Haverill then, only looked, and the reeve took a half step back and found there was nothing behind him but hedge. His mouth moved. Nothing came. Then she looked at the lord.

"My lord. Did you know of it?"

The wasps went on, or he stopped hearing them. Lord Alaric had not moved his eyes from the dead tree. "Goodwife Pyke," he said, "I have bound myself not to speak to you of this today." He said it quite evenly, and that was all, and did not say who had bound him, or how.

Maud Pyke's eyes went to Orryn. They stayed on him a long moment, narrowed a little, as they had when he bit the sour pear, though there was no laughter in it now; and then something in them eased, and he thought she had worked it out whole: the lord's silence, and the long way round, and who had asked for it. He felt the heat go up from his collar to his hair.

"Master Haverill," said Lord Alaric, to the tree. "No ladder is to go up that row today. Not hers, nor any man's."

"My lord," said Haverill, hoarsely.

"What will he do with it?" said Maud Pyke. It was the squire she asked, not the lord. "My row."

"I don't know, goodwife." That at least was entire. "He has not said. There are figures. Master Verrall made them. Nothing is decided."

"But you've a plan for it."

"I said what I would do. It isn't mine to promise."

"You had a plan for my row before I'd been told it was dying." She did not raise her voice. It would have been easier if she had. "And my Col, was he in it?"

"Yes."

"As what?"

He thought of Verrall's pen, and the figure, and the way he had said on the orchard account, so that it is seen to be wages. "As work, goodwife. For a wage. To grub and to graft."

"A wage," said Maud Pyke. She weighed the word as she might have weighed a pear in her hand. "Not bread."

"No."

She said nothing for a time. A little of the mist had gone from the brook, and the sun had got down off the ridge to the highest rows, so that the rust on the leaves showed orange, and Orryn saw how many of them there were.

"I'll find my son," she said. She wiped her hands on her skirt once more, though they were clean, and bobbed to the lord, and did not bob to anyone else.

"Goodwife," Orryn said. He had not meant to speak. "I am sorry."

"Aye," said Maud Pyke. "I expect you are." And she turned her back on the lane and went off along it, towards the brook end, walking steadily and not fast, with a straight back, and Orryn watched it go.

It was not far to the nursery. She had gone perhaps forty paces when a man came round the hedge-end with a bundle of bare twigs on his shoulder and a hitch in his stride, quick for all that, like a man who has long stopped noticing it. He had been working at the hurdles, Orryn supposed; the mist had hidden him, and the voices must have carried down the lane. He stopped dead when he saw his mother coming. She stopped too. Nobody in the lane could hear what was said, if anything was, and no one moved to find out. She said something short. Col Pyke did not answer. He looked past her, over her kerchiefed head, up the lane to where the lord stood among the reeve and the steward and the old man with his stick, and the squire in the borrowed shirt, and his eyes found Orryn, and stayed.

It was not anger. Orryn could have borne anger. It was the look of a man who has set a thing down carefully, and watched a boy pick it up and carry it, and let it fall where it would break. Orryn thought of the lame man's hand turning his thumb to the light, a courtesy that had seemed almost an afterthought, and wished very much to be somewhere else, and knew that there was nowhere else.

"Well," said Lord Alaric, quietly, to nobody. "It is told." He turned his head at last, and the hazel eyes went over Orryn without stopping. "Master Verrall. The figures, at noon."

What does Orryn do?

1. Go down the lane to Col at once and tell him himself how it came to be said, and that it was his own doing.

2. Stay at the lord's side and let mother and son have it out; if Col wants him, he knows where to find him.

3. Ask Lord Alaric what is to be done now, with the block seen and the plan still to be decided.

4. Something else of your own.
