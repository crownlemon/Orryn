The honest answer was long, and the short one was not honest. Orryn sat with his wet shirt cold on his back and found, somewhere between them, a third thing that was neither, and he did not much like himself for finding it so quickly.

"I was beaten, my lord," he said. His voice came out level. "Master Matthos has the wrist and the knees for it. My last shoe went a full yard past the stake. I stood a long while at the line before I threw it."

Verrall's pen, which had begun to move again, stopped.

Lord Alaric did not move at all. The foot was still, the inky hand lay flat upon the table, and Orryn understood that he was being read the way Verrall's ledgers were read, down the column and across, for the figure that did not belong. "Past," said Lord Alaric. "Not short."

"Past, my lord."

"A boy who is not trying drops it at his feet. A boy who is trying too hard overthrows." The hazel eyes did not blink. "A yard is a great distance in horseshoes. It is a poor sum, but I follow it."

At the end of the table Verrall coughed into his fist, very gently.

"Once is a courtesy," said Lord Alaric. "A second time it is a habit, and I will not have a habit of managing my son in this house. Remember which it was."

"Yes, my lord."

He had expected anger, or something worse, which was praise. He had not expected to feel what he felt, which was understood, and a little afraid of it, as a man might be afraid of a candle that had lit itself. Lord Alaric drew a fresh sheet towards him and dipped his pen, as though the matter were closed, and perhaps it was.

"This afternoon," he said, writing, "you will go to the orchards with Piers Fallow. Hugh Tull will meet you at the east gate. He has kept my trees since my father's day." The pen scratched. "Piers's father is the richest merchant in the vale and holds the lower mill of me, though he has not turned a stone himself in years. The boy knows what land looks like from underneath. You will learn what it looks like from above. You can spend a throw, it seems. Go and see what a field spends. Tull will tell you what owning it asks of a man. It asks a good deal more than people suppose. Bring Verrall what you saw in figures, where you can, and me in words, where you cannot."

"Yes, my lord."

"Eat first," said Lord Alaric, and did not look up again.

Dinner was a heel of bread and cold bacon eaten standing in the kitchen yard, and Piers Fallow was already at the east gate when Orryn got there, in a doublet a good deal finer than anything else in Grassfield and a temper to match. He was fifteen, stocky, with dark curls and quick brown eyes, and he was leaning on the gatepost with the air of a boy who has been sent on an errand beneath him and wishes it known.

"So we are sent for a walk," Piers said. "He doesn't send anyone anywhere without a reason. What did you tell him?"

"That I was beaten."

"Were you?"

"By a ring."

Piers looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable. "And a yard," he said, and pushed himself off the post and went through the gate, and Orryn had to hurry after.

The east block ran up the long slope behind the keep in rows so even that they might have been ruled with a straightedge. The early pears nearest the walls had been picked clean, but out here the late ones still hung hard and green, and the grass between the trunks was cropped short by sheep. There were ladders against some of the trees, and whitewash on the lower trunks to keep off the grubs, and the air was thick with wasps and the smell of sour windfalls going soft in the sun. A man was waiting for them at the head of the first row, sitting on an upturned basket, and he did not get up. He was sixty or more, bent like one of his own trees, with hands knotted like roots and a billhook at his belt. When he spoke it was slowly, and he spoke the first words to a pear tree.

"Hugh Tull," he said to the boys, afterwards, as if the introduction had been an afterthought. "Thirty years the lord's trees. Three reeves and two lords, and the trees still here." He stood, with a noise in his knees. "His lordship says you're to be shown. So."

He showed them. He walked them up the rows with his hands behind him and explained as he went, in a voice that never hurried, the way a man talks who has been right about the same things for thirty years and has stopped needing to be heard. A row was held, not owned. The holder tended it, took two parts of the fruit in three, and gave the lord the third and a day's labour at the picking. A pear took seven years from the graft to its first fruit and ten to a full crop, and bore for thirty after. "Whoever plants a pear," said Tull, "plants it for his grandson, and a lord who forgets that loses his orchard in forty years and never knows why."

Orryn counted without meaning to: fifty-two rows in the block, sixty trees to the row, and tried to work what a third of a good year came to and lost it among the wasps. "Three thousand one hundred and twenty trees," said Piers, flatly, before he had finished. "A third of the fruit is a thousand and forty trees' worth. If they all bear." Orryn said nothing. He was not so much annoyed as relieved that someone had said it, and that it had not been him. Tull glanced at Piers, then at Orryn, with the faint look of an old man who has watched two colts try the same fence.

"If they all bear," Tull agreed. "Come."

The oldest row stood at the top of the slope, where the trees were as thick as a man's waist and grey with lichen, and an old woman was on a ladder among the branches, sawing out a dead limb with a short saw. She was bent as a hook, with a face like a winter apple and a voice that carried to the bottom of the hill. "Lord's boys!" she called. "Come for the fruit? Not ripe yet. Come on, then, take one anyhow."

"Maud Pyke," said Tull quietly. "She holds this row. Her husband's father set those trees."

She came down the ladder sideways and pressed a hard little pear into each of their hands. Orryn bit into his and winced, for it was sour and gritty, with a thread of honey under the sour at the very end, and Maud Pyke laughed at his face and said that the trick was to wait. "Seven years, my husband used to say, and that was only for the first one." She patted the trunk beside her as one pats a horse. Her hands were as hard as the bark. He ate the rest of the pear, because there was nothing else to do, and he liked her, which made it worse.

Tull let her talk of the weather and her son's knee and the price of salt, and then took them a little further up the row where she could not hear. He put his hand flat on a trunk. The bark there was black and sunken in a long ragged patch, and when he pulled at it, it came away in a wet flake and the wood beneath was brown and soft. "Canker," he said. "Heart's gone in four of her trees, and the rust on the leaf in six. It was two last spring." He turned and pointed down the slope, where the wind came off the brook. "And that way is the wind. Eleven rows lie below her. By next summer it will be in all of them, and then I have a block full of dead trees and nobody to pay."

"Then cut it," said Piers.

"Cut it, and she has nothing for seven years. Not the crop, nor her two parts, nor his lordship's third. A widow and a lame son, and nothing to eat but what the lord gives them or what the town will, and a widow who is put out goes about the vale telling everyone what the lord did to her." Tull's voice did not change. "Or a lord who carries her for seven years, bread and rent forgiven, and has other holders say, why not ours? That is the cost of cutting. The cost of not cutting is eleven rows. Pick."

"My father holds her note," said Piers.

He said it without looking at either of them, and without a trace of the boast that usually went with Dunstan Fallow's name. "From the wet spring. Three silver stags. He lent it kindly. He lends everything kindly." His mouth went tight. "It's all in his book. Every house in this vale is in his book. I used to think that was what land was, when I was small. Debt that stays where you put it."

Tull said nothing to that, which seemed to be his way of agreeing. "Nobody's told her," he said, to the trunk. "I told the reeve in the spring. Master Haverill said he would carry it to his lordship, and I think he means to, and I think he has not found the day." He did not say it unkindly. He said it as he might have said that the brook was low. "So. That is what a row asks. Not the having of it. The deciding who goes hungry, and when, and telling them. You will not say a word to her. It is not yours to say."

Down the slope the sheep were moving along the grass, and above them Maud Pyke had gone back up her ladder and was singing something tuneless to the dead limb as she sawed it. Orryn found he was holding the core of the pear. He had thought that owning was having. It had not once occurred to him that a lord might lie awake over a row of trees.

Tull had turned and was looking at him, quite steadily, out of eyes the colour of old pewter.

"Say it were yours, young master," said Hugh Tull. "What would you do with the row?"

What does Orryn do?

1. Say he would cut the row, and ask that Maud Pyke and her son be carried for seven years, knowing the cost would fall on the lord's book and on Lyman Verrall's page.

2. Say he would wait a season and watch the downwind rows, and accept the risk of the canker spreading.

3. Say it is not his to say, but draw Piers aside and ask whether his father would carry Maud's note through the seven years, which would put Orryn in Piers's debt.

4. Something else of your own.
