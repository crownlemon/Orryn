"I will not ask for the supper," Orryn said. "Or the bed."

His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. He heard it as if it were someone else's, a boy's voice with a man's flat courtesy laid over it, and he went on before he could think better of it. "I have no coin, goodwife, and I will not have it written that I took what I could not pay for. I will sleep in the stable with the horse. If you can spare me water, I will take that, and nothing else."

The innkeeper looked at him a long moment. Then she looked at Wat, who had not moved, and then at the slate in her hand, as if the figures on it had been rude to her.

"Water," she said. "The well is the same well it has always been. There's no stag in that."

"Then I thank you for it."

"My name is Marra Pell, young ser. It was my mother's inn before me and her father's before her, and I've fed more lordlings than there are pears on that hill, and I'll tell you this for nothing as well." She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, one finger at a time. "I've seen a boy sleep in my stable out of pride, and I've seen one sleep there out of shame, and I've seen one do it out of sense. They look much the same in the morning. It's the straw that tells."

She turned and went in. The door shut behind her with a sound that was neither a slam nor a courtesy.

Wat had not said a word. He drew a small, flat purse from inside his coat and weighed it in his palm, not to open it, only to feel the heft, and put it away again. "Master Orryn," he said, "I'll write it as you've said it."

"Write it as I said it," Orryn answered. "Exactly."

The corner of Wat's mouth moved. "Aye. That I can do."

The stable was warm and low and smelled of horse and old hay and the sharp green sweetness of the crushed pear windfalls that someone had swept into the corner. Rook stood in the end stall with his head over the half-door, and he regarded Orryn with a patient dun eye, as a man might regard another man who had come to the same pew late. Orryn found a bucket, hauled it up from the well himself, because he thought it would be a poor thing to ask the stable-boy to do for him what he had just said he would do without, and carried it back with both hands. The dun drank for a long time. Then it lifted its dripping muzzle and blew, and Orryn laughed, and was surprised at the sound.

"You are lent to me," he told the horse quietly, "and I am lent to Grassfield, and neither of us is anyone's. We shall do very well."

The horse said nothing, as was proper.

He made a bed in the empty stall beside it, out of the straw that was already there and had not been charged for by anyone. It was dry. It was not unkind. He lay on his back with his cloak over him and his boots on, and watched the beams go dark overhead, and the last light drain out of the gap above the door. Somewhere across the yard, behind a shuttered window, the kitchen was loud with supper: a clatter of pans, Marra Pell's voice rising, a man's laugh, and under it a lower sound that might have been Ser Wystan telling a story to people who had not heard it before. Orryn tried to make out the words, and could not, and then did not try.

The one that burned came in.

He turned it over in the dark, as one turns a stone from a stream. Storm's End had answered. Someone at Storm's End had taken a pen and written to Gawen Grandison, at Grandview, in the nine days that he had waited, and that letter had come into the keep and been read by someone and put on a fire. By whom? Ser Wystan had not said. By Gawen, who might have burned it from shame, or from rage? By Alester, who had shouted from the second day that they should ride? By Steffon, who stood at the foot of the stair and counted?

He could not decide. Each of them was a man he knew, and each of them could have done it, and he found, rather to his dismay, that he could not tell which of them he wished it had been.

Sometime after the kitchen had gone quiet, the half-door creaked.

Orryn was up on his elbow with his heart going before he had thought of who it might be. It was Pate. The groom was a long dark shape against the slightly lesser dark of the yard, with something bundled under one arm. He stood in the opening without speaking, as he did everywhere, and Orryn saw the white of his eyes move over the stall, the horse, the bed of straw.

"Master Orryn."

"Pate." Orryn's own voice came out hoarse. "Is it morning?"

"No." Pate came in and set down the bundle on the straw by Orryn's feet. It was a horse blanket, folded, smelling of mule and tar. On top of it lay the heel of a loaf, and a bit of hard cheese wrapped in a cloth. "Wat said not to."

Orryn looked at the bread. "Then why—"

"Wat said not to," Pate said again, and there was something in the way he said it, not quite a defiance, more the plain patience of a man who has done a thing and is explaining to a horse why. "He said you'd said it, and it was your say. But he said it to me and not to the cart." He rubbed his big hands down the front of his coat. "It's a cold night for the blanket. The straw's damp at the bottom."

It was the longest speech Orryn had heard from him since the dun's hooves. He understood, then, quite clearly, that Pate had been told nothing of what he might do. Wat had said, not to, in the voice of a man who wishes to be able to say later that he did. And Pate had heard the other thing under it, the thing that Wat had not said, and had come across the yard in the dark to do it.

He did not know whether it was an offence against what he had said at the door. He thought it probably was. He thought, too, that a thing could be an offence and still be a kindness, and that he had not yet learned how to hold both.

"I said I would take nothing," he said slowly.

"Aye."

"This is not the inn's. It is yours."

"It's my own bread, Master Orryn." The big face did not change. "It's not in any book."

Orryn looked at him for a long moment in the dark, and then at the blanket, and found that the thing he had set in his chest at the door, so hard and clean and exact, had a small crack in it where the cold was getting through.

"Thank you, Pate," he said.

Pate nodded once and went out, and the door creaked behind him. Orryn lay for some time with his hand on the rough wool. Then he drew it over himself, and it was warm.

He woke in grey light with the dun's breath on his hair.

The yard was already busy. A stable-boy was drawing water. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney in a straight line, and the mule stood harnessed and sullen between the shafts, and Wat was waiting by the cart with his hands behind his back and the little book open on the board beside him, the way a clerk waits at a toll-house. He did not say good morning. He read, in his soft, flat Reach voice, from the page.

"Master Orryn Grandison, being offered supper and a bed at the Pear and Plough and unable to pay, declined both of his own wish, and slept in the stable. Took water. Took nothing else from the inn. Nothing owed." He looked up. "That's what I've put. Is it right?"

Orryn thought of the blanket folded on the straw behind him, and of the bread he had eaten at the first grey of dawn, and of the sentence Wat had just read, which was true and was not the whole truth.

"It is what I said," he answered.

"Aye." Wat shut the book. "It's what you said."

Across the yard, the old knight on the mule was already moving off at a walk, his back very straight under his cloak, his one good eye fixed on the road ahead. He did not turn his head as he passed the cart. But as he went by the stable door, his hand lifted a little way from the reins and fell again, and Orryn could not have said whether it was a greeting, or the shaking of an old man's palsy, or only a man brushing away a fly.

"Grassfield by dusk," Wat said. "If the mule holds."

What does Orryn do?

1. Ride beside the cart and tell Wat, in his own words, what Ser Wystan said about the burned letter, so that Wat hears it from him first.

2. Follow Ser Wystan's mule at a distance on the road, and ask him nothing, only so that the old man may see he can be trusted with silence.

3. Ask Wat about the blanket, and whether the book has a line for a thing given freely.

4. Something else of your own.
