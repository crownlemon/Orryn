Highgarden, 208 AC

The chamber across the passage was empty by the time the afternoon bells rang.

Matthos, Lord Alaric’s son and heir, with whom Orryn had shared a chamber at Grassfield all the year, was to be Ser Moryn Tyrell’s squire. Two of Moryn’s pages had come for his things while Orryn was fetching water, and they had taken everything: the good green cloak, the second pair of boots, the little box of thread and salves that Lady Jeyne had packed and Matthos had called nonsense and then carried up the stairs himself. What they left was a stripped bed, a hollow in the straw tick, and one stocking that had slipped down behind the chest. Orryn picked it up and stood in the doorway with it longer than a stocking deserved. Then he put it in his own bag, to give back.

He had carried Matthos’s message to Lord Alaric as soon as he came in from the stables, word for word. Tell Father I’ll see him at supper. Alaric had said, “At Moryn’s table, then,” and gone back to the letter he was writing, which was to Lady Jeyne. He wrote slowly. Twice he stopped with the pen lifted and looked at nothing, and the first sheet went into the cold hearth in four pieces. Orryn did not look at the pieces.

It was nearly the hour for supper when a page appeared at the door in the green and gold of the household, out of breath from the stairs.

“Lord Leo’s compliments to Lord Meadows, and would he come down to the long stables before the hall fills. His lordship asks that the Grandison boy come with him.”

Alaric laid down his pen. “The boy.”

“Yes, my lord. His lordship said you would know which.”

“I should hope so. I have been feeding him for a year.” He sanded the letter, looked at it, and folded it without sealing it. To Orryn he said, “Wash your hands. He will want you to hold something.”

“Hold what, my lord?”

“I have no idea. It is always something.”

*

The long stables lay below the practice yard, on the north side of the middle wall: two ranges of pale stone with a roofed walk between them and a lamp already lit at either end, though the sky above was still light. The heat of the day had gone into the stones and was coming out of them again. Flies hung in the doorways. Somewhere a groom was singing the same four lines of a song over and over, badly, to a horse that did not seem to mind.

Lord Leo Tyrell was in the third stall on the left, on one knee in the straw.

Orryn had seen him in the rose garden, and again across the great hall, always among cups and flatterers, and had taken him for the sort of lord who came into a room like a change in the weather: warm, bright, and a little too grand to be approached. He had not pictured him with his sleeves turned back and his silver head against the shoulder of a dark bay colt, running a thumb down a foreleg with the slow care of a man who had done it ten thousand times. Lord of Highgarden, sixty-six years old, overlord of all the Reach: men called him the Longthorn, and said it with an affection Orryn had never heard given to any lord at Grandview or Grassfield, as if he were something they had all agreed to be proud of. The groom at the colt’s head looked a good deal older than his master and a good deal more anxious.

“Cool?” Leo asked the leg.

“Cool as a spring, my lord. I’ve felt it twice.”

“Then feel it a third time, so the colt knows you mean it.” He got up out of the straw without putting a hand to the wall, which Orryn noticed, and Leo noticed him noticing, and looked pleased. “Alaric. And the stormboy. Come in, come in. He will not bite you. He is saving it for someone who deserves it.”

“This is the one that threw the knight?” Alaric said.

“Into a ditch, the day you came. In my hall last night it was a stream, and the knight was a better knight. I embroider. It is the only needlework I was ever any good at.” He slapped the colt’s neck. “This morning I had him ridden again, by the same boy, and the boy stayed on. Ser Luthor Risley wished to give the colt to a better rider. I told him a better rider would have stayed on the first time and learned nothing.” He turned. “Take his head, lad. Not so close. He dislikes a face. Yes. There.”

Orryn took the rope, and the colt put its nose against his sleeve and blew. It was a young horse’s breath, grassy and hot. The groom let go of the halter as though it had been burning him.

“Now,” said Leo, still looking at Orryn, though it was not to Orryn he spoke. “Alaric. You have been carrying something since the garden like a man carrying a full cup up a stair. Put it down.”

Alaric did not pretend to misunderstand. “I would sooner you heard it from me than from another, my lord. Ser Moryn has agreed to take Matthos into his household. Ser Olyvar offered him a place as well, this morning in the yard, before Moryn had said yes in front of the boy. I declined it. I did it as courteously as I knew how, and I should not like it thought that I set one of your sons against the other, or that—”

“Seven save us, Alaric.” Leo said it kindly. “I knew by noon.”

“By noon.”

“Olyvar told me himself when he came off the lower lists. He had the face he wears when he has been generous and wishes it noticed. Moryn told me over the midday cup, with the face he wears when he has been clever and hopes it has not been. I had the whole of it before the bread was cut.” He ran a hand under the colt’s jaw. “Moryn made certain the asking was done through his wife’s kin, so that no one could say he had asked at all. Olyvar offered second, and offered in front of Roxton, so that it would be a thing that had been seen. You said yes to the first and no to the second, and thanked them both before witnesses. I should have done the same, though I would have taken a day longer over it and cost myself more.”

“I did not know what you would think.”

“No. That is why you are standing as if you had swallowed a spear.”

Orryn kept his eyes on the colt. It had begun to lean, as horses did, its weight coming slowly over onto his left foot, and he found that he did not dare to move it.

“Do you know what the trouble is with my sons?” Leo said. He did not wait. “They are all three good at the same thing, and it is the thing I was good at. They gather powerful men about them. Garth has the Redwynes and every clerk in the Reach who can add. Moryn has half the old houses, Rowan and Peake and Ashford, who love him because he remembers their mothers’ names. Olyvar has the respect of Lord Roxton, who keeps my soldiers, and the Hewetts and the Tarlys, and every boy who would sooner be a knight than anything else under the sun. I did the same at their ages, and I had two hundred men who would have followed me into the sea before I was forty. It is how a man makes himself ready to be a lord. I do not blame them for it.” He gave the colt’s neck a last pat and let his hand fall. “But they have done it for years under my roof, and none of those men has sworn an oath to any of them. The oaths are mine. Rowan swore to me, and Redwyne, and Peake, and Tarly, and you. A man who gathers friends is doing nothing amiss. A man who gathers oaths is taking what was not put in his hand to take. They know it, my sons. It is why they are so patient.”

“Patient,” Alaric said.

“Within reason. Within very narrow reason.” Leo smiled. “So you need not be afraid of choosing among them. There is nothing to choose. A lord who sends his son to Moryn, and his roads to Roxton and Olyvar, and his accounts to Garth’s table to be picked over, has done the one thing that cannot be called a faction. He has put himself where the three roads meet. It is the safest place in the Reach, Alaric, and I will tell you for nothing that I do not often see a man arrive there on purpose.”

“I did not arrive there on purpose, my lord. I was asked twice.”

“That is generally how it is done.”

A step sounded in the passage, quick and a little uneven, and a clerk came round the corner of the stall with a writing board against his chest. He was a thin young man in a brown doublet, with an enamelled pin at his collar in the shape of a cluster of purple grapes, and he had been running; the board shook slightly in his hands. He bowed to the lord first and then, as an afterthought, to the groom, who ignored him.

“My lord. The Lord Seneschal’s compliments. The matter of the landing at the Mander ford wants your hand before the court sits tomorrow. Ser Arron says it is the same as the one you spoke of, and he would not trouble you, only the Justiciar requires a name above his own.”

“Arron would not trouble me for less than the whole river.” Orryn knew the name: Ser Arron Redwyne, the Lord Seneschal, who had sat at the head of the table that morning with grape leaves worked on his cuffs and had made everything Alaric said sound like a courtesy. Leo took the pen the clerk held out, set the board against the colt’s withers, and signed.

He did not read it. He did not turn the paper toward the lamp or ask what the landing was, or whose. The clerk had set the board where Orryn, holding the halter, could not help seeing it, and Orryn saw close lines of small writing, and the pen go down at the foot in one large flourish, Leo Tyrell, as long as a sword. A ribbon hung below it, waiting for its seal. The clerk blew on the ink. The colt flinched at the breath and Orryn spoke to it without thinking, low, as he would have spoken at Grassfield.

“There,” said Leo, handing back the pen. “Ten years ago I could not find my own seal in a day. Now Arron lays a paper in front of me and it is done before I have found my cup. He takes so much of my work from me, that young man, that I should be ashamed to say how much. I never had a better seneschal.”

“He is Lord Redwyne’s son,” Alaric said. The Redwynes held the Arbor, the wine island off the western coast, and kept more ships than any house in the Reach; Lord Theomar, who ruled it now, was Garth’s father-in-law.

“He is. And his sister is Garth’s wife, and I know very well what is said of that, and I say a man cannot help who his sister married any more than he can help his height. If Theomar had sent me a fool I should have sent him back.” He turned to the clerk. “Tell Ser Arron he has it, and tell him I said the soup at noon was cold.”

The clerk bowed again, to nobody in particular, and went away down the passage with the board held flat.

Orryn found that he was looking at Lord Alaric, and that Lord Alaric was looking at him. It lasted no longer than a breath. His lord’s face did not alter, and Orryn did not know what his own did; but he thought of how Alaric would not let the packhorse out of the yard at Bitterbridge until he had gone and looked at the chest himself, and then he looked at the colt’s ear, and Alaric looked at the stall post, and neither of them said anything at all.

*

“Walk with me,” said Leo, “while there is light. I like to see them all fed before I eat. Bring him, lad. He should see what he is missing.”

They went down the roofed walk between the two ranges, Leo a little ahead with his hands behind him, the old groom hurrying to keep at his elbow, Alaric beside them, and Orryn behind leading the colt. It was as good as a lesson in how to be followed. Leo stopped at every third stall to ask a groom some question about a hoof or a cough, and no sooner had he asked it than the answer seemed to come out of the dark of the box, and he went on, already speaking to Alaric about something else.

“The dry,” he was saying. “Tell me honestly how you are fed at Grassfield.”

“We shall have bread,” Alaric said. “Not so much as I would like. The Blueburn is low, and the millers are quarrelling over who has the water.”

“Everyone’s millers are quarrelling over the water. Rowan wrote me the same.” Leo grunted. “Theomar’s ships have been carrying Arbor grain up the Mander since the dry began, to every landing that asks, and he has not named me a price for one sack of it. There are men in the north of the Reach who would have gone hungry for a month without him. I have said so at my own table, and I will say it again to Rowan’s face.”

“And who carries it on from the landing?”

“The Arbor’s factors.”

“At whose price?”

“A fair price, I should think. They are Redwynes, Alaric, not pirates.”

“I do not say they are not fair, my lord. I say it is the Arbor’s factors who now know where every sack in the Reach lies, and which lord has none.”

Leo stopped. For a moment Orryn thought he had offended him, but the old lord only laughed, and put his head back to do it. “You have been listening to Rowan.”

“I have been listening to my own steward.”

“Rowan thinks generosity is a kind of conquest. It is only a kind of generosity. I have fed lords at my table for forty years, and not one of them has ever thought himself conquered by the bread.” He moved on. “Besides. A man who has carried your grain when you were hungry may be allowed some slight irritation at being asked to pay a toll upon the road.”

“Rowan would say the same, my lord, of the tolls.”

“Rowan would say that of the sunrise.” But he slowed, as a man does who has been hit in a place he has bruised before, and over his shoulder he said, “You came by the river road, lad. You have been silent as a post since you took the colt, which I like, but I promised you a question at supper and I cheated you of it; there was a horse in the way, and a very long story. So. Alaric told me at the garden that Bitterbridge was prosperous. Alaric is a courteous man. I said you might be brave enough to contradict him. What did you see?”

Orryn’s foot still ached where the colt had stood on it. “Of Bitterbridge, my lord?”

“Of my Reach. You had eyes the whole way.”

It was not a question he had rehearsed. He thought of the fields by the river, which were green, and the fields farther back, which were not; of the loaded barges, and the hard pears. He did not think that was what Lord Leo wanted.

“There was a little stone bridge, my lord, on the road in,” he said. “The toll man let us through without asking, because of Lord Alaric’s banner. Ser Bertram said it was because he had been told to. Two carts came behind us with sacks on them, and the man on the first one held up a paper, and the toll man shook his head at it. He would not take it from him.” He swallowed. “I only saw it from the road. But it had wax hanging from it. Green wax.”

“Green wax,” said Leo.

“There is a good deal of green wax at Highgarden, my lord. I do not know that it was yours.”

The old lord looked at him for a moment with an expression Orryn could not read, and then he laughed, not unkindly, and the groom ahead of them looked round, startled. “Then it very likely was mine. I have signed my name to more kindnesses than I could tell you, boy. A mill, a ford, a field that need not pay a toll to carry its own wheat to market. Some will have been foolish, and the foolish ones are the ones men remember. I do not regret the kindness.” He glanced at Alaric. “You did not tell me that.”

“I did not see it, my lord.”

“No. You were at the front, being looked at. That is the use of a squire.” He walked on, and added after a few paces, in a different voice, “Well. Now I know that a toll man on the Bitterbridge road will refuse my wax, and that a boy of the Stormlands was the one to notice it.”

Alaric said nothing. Orryn could not tell, from where he walked, whether his master was displeased, or merely putting something away to be looked at later.

It was hard not to like Lord Leo. Orryn had liked him in the garden, and liked him better now, with straw still on his knees, laughing at himself for a thing that other lords would have denied. He did not much like how easily the liking came. The unread paper and the green wax went along beside it, neither giving way, like two horses harnessed in a pair that did not suit.

It was Alaric who spoke first, after a while. “May I be plain, my lord?”

“You will be, whether I permit it or not. It is why I like you.”

“After the war,” Alaric said, “you made the peace. Houses that had gone to Daemon Blackfyre had to be hanged, or forgiven, or left to go sour; and you forgave most of them, and you paid for it. Not with gold. You had none to spare. With paper. A toll lifted here, a mill’s grinding right there, a bridge that need not be paid at by such and such a house for as long as the house should last.”

Orryn had been a baby in the war Lord Alaric meant. He knew it only as the reason some houses had fewer castles than they once had, and some lords fewer sons, and as the thing his elders still went quiet about when the wine was low.

“That is the case in outline.”

“And Ser Garth’s clerks are reading them.”

“Garth reads everything. It is the best thing about him.”

“They are reading them one at a time, my lord. Every one that cannot be found, or cannot be read the way it was meant, is a lord who learns what his pardon was worth. Lord Rowan’s grain carts are the first that I have heard of. I do not think they will be the last.”

Alaric’s hand, which had been trailing along the stall doors as they went, had stopped. Orryn saw it, and thought of the Alder Mill; but the Alder Mill was a quarrel with Dunstan Fallow, and it did not seem to him large enough to stop a man’s hand.

Leo had stopped walking. He stood in the lamplit aisle with his back to them for the space of three breaths, and then he turned, and the face that looked at Alaric was a great deal older than the face that had looked at the colt.

“Do not suppose I am a fool, Alaric,” he said. “I am old. It is not the same thing. I know very well what Garth is doing, and I know what happens to a pardon when it is read aloud in an office. I made that peace on paper because it was the only thing that could be made, and the Reach has lived on it eleven years. If I could have paid in gold I should have paid in gold, and no one would have needed to be afraid of a clerk.” He put out a hand to the post. “I will not have them unwritten while I live. After, Garth may read what he likes. He will anyway.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“You are not to say yes to me in that voice. It is the voice they use for dying men.”

“Forgive me.”

“I forgive everything. That is the difficulty.” And he was smiling again, and the groom let out his breath. “Come. There is a mare at the end I wish you to see, and I am told Olyvar has been to look at her, so naturally she is lame.”

*

The mare was not lame; she was sulking. Leo spoke to her in an undertone for a time, his forehead nearly touching hers, and Orryn, standing behind with the colt, heard him say fat old fool twice, tenderly, and could not tell whether he meant the mare. When he drew back he was talking again, as though he had never stopped.

“Lord Roxton has been at my ear since the dry began. He wants the Reach to stand in a muddy field and be counted. He wants a roll, with names on it, and a captain for each name who can tell him how many spears he will bring and where his horse is shod. I am sure he is right. Roxton is always right; it is what makes him so very hard to dine with.” He reached into his doublet, found nothing, and held out his hand to the groom, who put a withered apple in it. “And I give them a tilt. Four hundred knights came to my last, and not one of them was put on a roll. I could tell you the name of every man who came, and whom he brought, and whom he did not. Is that not a muster? It is a muster in good clothes. They come for the feast, and they stay for the lists, and when the horn blows they ride, because their lord is watching and so are a hundred ladies. Ask Roxton how many of the men who swear to him twenty spears would stand in the rain in a yard for a month, because he told them to.”

“None, I should think,” Alaric said.

“None. They would come for me.” Leo broke the apple in half with his thumbs and gave one part to the mare and the other, without looking, to the colt, which snatched it so close to Orryn’s ear that he started. “Even Olyvar has begun to say that Roxton is right, and he has won more of my tilts than any man living. He is the one of the three who is most like me. The other two have never forgiven him for it.” He wiped his hand on his hose. “He will want you to look at Roxton’s drill before you go. He has asked you already, I think.”

“I was invited, yes.”

“Go. Let them show you. They love to show, those two; it is almost the only thing they agree about. Let Roxton say what he wishes about your roads. And when he has said it, ask him who is to feed the men on them.” He looked sidelong at Alaric. “That is a question Garth would put much better.”

“I asked who would pay, in the yard this morning,” Alaric said. “Ser Olyvar told me I sounded like his brother.”

“Then you put it exactly as Garth would, and were thanked for it exactly as Garth is. There. Now you see why I keep three sons.”

The colt had dozed off on its feet, one of them planted on Orryn’s. His own foot had gone numb, and he did not dare to shift it.

“Armond,” said Leo, “will have talked to you of my grandson’s marriage. He talks of nothing else after the second cup.”

“He spoke of the Rowan girl,” Alaric said, “and the Blackbar girl.”

“He would have spoken of a third if there had been one, to give himself more to disagree about.” Leo leaned his shoulder against the stall. He spoke easily, and frankly, as though the groom were part of the stable, and the squire holding the colt part of the straw; Orryn understood after a moment that it was a compliment, of a kind, and an unnerving one. “Since you will not ask, I shall tell you what I think, because I would sooner say it to a man who will not repeat it to Armond. I am fond of Rycherd Rowan of Goldengrove. He reads every parchment twice and trusts neither, and I have never known him break his word, which is more than I can say for the men who read once. He should be honoured, and this year he has not been. A match with his house would honour him, and it would be a peace.”

“And the girl?” Alaric asked.

“Roslin. His daughter. Seventeen. I have seen her twice. Her mother’s mother was a Targaryen, as the Hightowers will tell you before you have sat down, and she has the look of it about the mouth. Both times I saw her she laughed at the wrong moment; once it was at a joke at her own father’s expense. And she did not apologise to him afterward, or to me.” He seemed to turn it over. “I liked her for it. I do not know that Harlen would.”

Orryn had not yet set eyes on Ser Harlen Tyrell. He knew only that Harlen was Garth’s eldest son, eighteen and a knight already, next in line after his father; and that his mother was Lady Alys Redwyne, Ser Arron’s sister.

“My grandson.” Leo’s voice altered, only a little. “He is a soft boy, Alaric. A sweet one, and I love him, and I have never once seen him win an argument, because he leaves the room before it begins. He will be lord of Highgarden if the Seven are kind and his father is not too long about it, and a lord cannot leave the room.”

“He is eighteen, my lord.”

“I was eighteen. I did not leave rooms.” He said it shortly, and then, as though he had heard himself, he smiled and went on.

He pushed himself off the stall. “The Blackbar girl, now. Meredyth, Lord Arstan’s daughter. Fifteen. Sweet and careful, curtsies as if she had been told once how and was determined never to be told again. Her mother is a Hightower, of the great house in Oldtown, and Alys favours the match, and the Arbor and the Hightowers behind Alys, and all of them are in a great hurry. I can see what Blackbar gets. I have yet to see what the Arbor and the Hightowers get from it, but I am too old to be suspicious of a man like Theomar, who has never asked me for anything he had not the ships to pay for.” He looked down at the apple core in his hand and gave it to the mare. “And Alys asks. Sweet Alys, who is like a daughter to me. I have had her at my table for twenty years, and I can refuse her nothing she asks twice. It is a weakness. I tell you so that you will not mistake it for a position.”

Orryn thought that it was a strange thing to hear a lord say about his own weakness, and a stranger thing to hear him say it so happily. He found that Alaric was not smiling.

“If you were to ask me which I lean to, and you have not, I should say the Rowan girl. Not for her blood, or her laugh. For the ships and the fields. Harlen’s mother is a Redwyne, so he has ships in his blood already, and fields in his name. Give him a Rowan wife and he has both at his own table, and I do not have to settle another toll.” He turned to Alaric. “What would you do, with two good doors and one grandson?”

It was not a question for which Alaric had been given time. He stood quite still, and answered slowly.

“I should ask the boy which of them he could bear across a table for forty years, my lord. And I should ask what each house would do if it were refused. Both, before I chose anything.”

“The boy would say whichever one was nearest. That is the whole of the trouble with him.” Leo shook his head. “And Garth thinks I shall decide it for him. He is half right. I shall decide it when it suits me, which is not yet, and I will not be hurried by a letter from Bitterbridge or a dinner at Goldengrove. Armond gave you his Jeyne, and that was a bargain for both houses, and I do not hear that either regrets it. You have a younger girl to place, I am told.”

“Elyce,” Alaric said. “She is twelve.”

“Twelve. And you have been making inquiries.” There was no reproach in it, only a faint pleasure at being the first to say so. “Do not look so alarmed. I hear whatever my lady wife has heard by supper, and she hears everything before it. She will want to see you about it. You had better go to her tomorrow, in the forenoon, and take the boy; she likes a squire in the room. She likes to be asked before I am. It is the one precedence she has never let me forget.”

“I had meant to ask for an audience.”

“Then ask it of her, and not of me, and she will be very gracious about it.” Leo put a hand lightly on the colt’s nose, and looked at Orryn over it. “Well held. You may let him off your foot now. He has been standing on it since the Rowan girl.”

Orryn opened his mouth, and shut it, and found that Lord Leo was already walking away, with the groom at his heels and the first of the supper bells beginning somewhere up the hill, deep and slow, then a second, higher and faster, then a third out of tune with both.

“Bring the horse back to his stall, Grandison,” Alaric said. “Then come.”

He did. When he came out again Alaric was waiting for him under the lamp at the end of the walk, with the dusk gone blue behind him and the swallows low over the yard.

“Is it broken?”

“No, my lord.”

“Walk on it.”

Orryn walked. It hurt, a little, in a way he thought he could put up with.

“Good,” said Alaric. He began to climb the path toward the hall.

Orryn went after him, favouring the foot. “My lord, about the bridge—”

“Not tonight.” It was not sharp. It was the way a man declines a second cup. “I should like my supper before I think about anything else.”

The bells were still sounding as they came up into the yard. Lights were showing along the galleries, one after another, and a long line of servants went by under them with covered dishes. He thought of Matthos, who would be at Ser Moryn’s table by now, among squires he had never sat beside, being told where to put his elbows. The stocking was still in Orryn’s bag, and he supposed he would have to carry it there like a petitioner.
