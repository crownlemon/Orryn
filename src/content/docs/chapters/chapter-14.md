All the breath went out of him at once.

It was the oddest thing. A moment before he had been a rider on the crest of a rise, wind in his teeth, shouting at the sky; and now he was a boy of fourteen standing on cobbles in a torn sleeve with a man's eyes on him, and every word he had rehearsed had gone out of his head like water from a split bucket. He opened his mouth. What came out was a sort of scrape.

"It is—" he said. "My lord. It is—"

Lord Alaric waited. He did not frown, and he did not help. He simply stood, with the steward at his elbow still holding the tally-stick, and the grey-haired woman with the goose gone quiet behind the well, and the yard noise sliding farther and farther off, until it seemed to Orryn that the whole of Grassfield was holding its breath to hear a stammer.

He looked at his boots. They were scuffed, and there was dried mud on the left one, shaped like the Reach. He made himself lift his chin.

"I should begin at the beginning, my lord. If I may."

"Begin," said Lord Alaric. "In order. And do not apologise for it. I find apologies take up the space where the facts would go."

That helped, a little, in the way a cold hand helps a burn. Orryn took a breath that shook, and began.

He told it in a thin, careful voice, to a spot just beside the lord's left shoulder, because he could not look at the face. The cart. The silent ride west with Wat and Pate. The ford at Sedgeford, with the rope across the road and the toll-house with the heron on its flag. Ser Osmund Sedgley, who had said the toll was two silver stags and three pence for carts in Reach livery, and who had said it was not the money, that was why he wanted it. His voice cracked on stags, and went up, and he heard it go up, and his ears burned. He went on.

"I said that I was to be your squire, my lord. I named you. I said that if the ford was owed anything, you should be told of it." He swallowed. "It did nothing. He said it was paid at the rope, not by letter. So I asked Wat to pay it from your purse. By my word. And Wat said he would tell you that, and the clerk wrote it in the toll-book, by order of the boy."

Lord Alaric's face did not change. But the steward, beside him, looked quickly at the cobbles, and a muscle moved in his plump cheek.

"Two stags and three pence," Alaric said.

"Yes, my lord."

"Paid from my purse, at your say-so, to a man who had not been owed it by any writ."

"Yes." It was barely a word. "I thought that it was the only thing that would end it."

"Did it end it?"

"It— yes, my lord. It did."

"Then the price was two stags and three pence for the road." Alaric said it as one reads a figure from a column. "Not an unreasonable price. Go on."

Orryn blinked. He had been braced for something, he did not know what. Not that. He went on, faster now, with the words tumbling out in a rush because he did not trust himself to slow them. Ser Jon Penrose, who had ridden across the ford in crimson silk and called him Master Counter. The horse that Ser Jon had offered, from Ser Osmund's stable, by a Penrose order. The dun gelding, Rook, seven years old. He had accepted it. He had sent thanks to Lady Alysanne by Ser Jon's hand. He had kept the slip.

"The slip," Lord Alaric said.

He dug it out. His fingers were clumsy; the paper had been folded behind Alaric's own letter and had come out creased and a little damp from sweat and pear. He held it out at arm's length without looking, as one offers a dish to a stranger, and felt it taken. For a few moments there was only the sound of the lord reading, a faint breathing of paper.

"Lent by Penrose order," Alaric read aloud, flat and exact. "Harl, clerk. No term of return. Witnessed Ser Jon Penrose." He paused over the last name, and the pause had an edge in it like a knife laid on a table. "A Penrose signature on a paper in my squire's coat."

"I did not ask for it, my lord. He offered it. Lady Alysanne had—"

"I can see who offered it." He folded the slip once along its crease, and again, and put it inside his own coat. "Do you know what a horse lent with no term of return is?"

Orryn's heart was going very fast. He had thought it through on the road, at least a little; he had come as far as an answer. "A gift, my lord," he said, "if no one asks for it back. And a debt if someone does."

For the first time, something moved in the lord's face. It was gone before Orryn could be sure of it.

"Yes," Alaric said. "That is exactly what it is." He turned the slip over in his coat with one finger, thoughtfully, like a man testing a loose coin. "And you are standing in my yard on it. And you came ahead of the cart, so that I should hear this from you first."

"Yes, my lord."

"Why?"

It was the question Orryn had no answer ready for, because the honest one was that he had wanted to ride, and the second honest one was that he had wanted to be the first to say it. He looked at the cobbles. He looked at the green smear on his knee. "Because I thought it would be worse if you heard it from the book," he said, very low. "I thought if I said it first, you would know that I did not mean to hide it."

There was a silence. A long one. Behind the well, someone's bucket clanked down on stone.

"Verrall," said Lord Alaric.

The steward straightened: Lyman Verrall, forty-odd, a younger son of some landed knight, whom Orryn had been told was the best-humoured man in the keep. He had a good open face, round and sunburnt, with laugh-lines at the corners of his eyes that did not quite reach his eyes now. "My lord?"

"Two stags and three pence from the household purse, entered against the squire's keep, to be reckoned when I say. The dun to the lower stables, and fed. A Penrose slip is not to be mentioned at table." His eyes came back to Orryn. They were hazel, flecked, and they went over his face as they had gone over the yard, one line at a time. "You have been a long day on the road. You have lost your hat, I see, and a part of your sleeve. You may wash. After that you will sit with a clerk and write down, in order, what you have just said to me. All of it. I will have it before supper."

"All of it, my lord?"

"All of it," said Lord Alaric. "Wat will have his book. I shall read the two against each other." He said it neither kindly nor otherwise. "A man who tells the same story twice, in two hands, has told the truth, or has told it very well. I find I can usually tell which."

It was only then, standing very still in the cooling yard, that Orryn thought of the old knight on the bench, and the thing he had not said.

He had told the ford. He had told the toll, and the dun, and the slip. He had not told Ser Wystan. He had not told the nine days, or the forty men, or the letter that burned and came in. He had told all of that to Wat on the road, in his own words, in the open, and Wat had said I'll write it as it was told. And at that moment, through the gate behind him, came the slow tread of a mule and the long creak of an axle, and he turned his head and saw the cart.

Wat sat on the board with the reins slack in his hands, the little book open on his knee. He did not look at Orryn. He looked at the lord, as a man looks at weather.

"My lord," Wat said.

"Wat," said Lord Alaric, without turning his head. "You have a book."

What does Orryn do?

1. Say at once, before Wat can read, that there is more: the old knight at the inn, and what he told, and that he told it to Wat on the road.

2. Keep still, and let Wat read the book, then answer whatever he is asked about the old knight.

3. Ask to write his account tonight and give it with Wat's, so Lord Alaric has both side by side, and say nothing more now.

4. Something else of your own.
