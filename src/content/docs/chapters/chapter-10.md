"Which one burned?" Orryn said.

He had not known he would say it until it was out, and then it lay on the bench between them like a dropped coin, and neither of them reached for it. The Pear and Plough went on about its evening. A boy ran across the yard with a bucket that slopped at every step. A woman in the kitchen door was shouting at someone about onions. Behind the stable the dun gelding blew and shifted, and Orryn heard it, and thought, absurdly, he is not mine either.

Ser Wystan did not answer. He turned his cup a quarter turn on the board, and then another quarter, as if the cup were a lock and he meant to find the way of it. His clouded eye stared at nothing. The good one, small and pale and wet at the rim, was on Orryn's face.

"I said I would tell it when I was dead," the old knight said at last.

"You did, ser." Orryn's mouth was dry. He thought of the sword on the bench beside the old man's hip, and of how the old man had said he had had his hand on it when Orryn rode in. "I do not wish you dead. I would take it very ill if you died. But you are eighty-one, and there will be someone who tells it after you, and they will tell it wrong. I would sooner have it from the man who carried it."

"Would you." Ser Wystan's lip lifted off his remaining teeth. It was not quite a smile. "Listen to him. Sooner have it from the man who carried it. You talk like a ledger, boy. Is that what they teach you at Grandview now, to ask a question so that it can only be answered one way?"

"I do not know what they teach," Orryn said. "I only know I have been asked all day what Grandview is owed, and not once what it did."

That was truer than he had meant, and the old man heard it. The eye narrowed.

"Sit," Ser Wystan said.

Orryn sat. The bench was warm from the old man's weight and smelled of mule and sour wine. Up close Ser Wystan was bigger than he had looked from the road, a great ruin of a man, with a scar like a split seam running from his scalp down to the corner of his bad eye, and hands that had been very large once and had since been worn down to knuckles and cords. They did not shake. That surprised Orryn more than anything.

"Who sent you to ask me?" Ser Wystan said. "That Meadows lad who keeps you?"

"No one sent me, ser."

"Then what do you mean to do with it? You will want to carry it somewhere. Boys always do. To Storm's End, to clear the name. To Penrose, to show him he was wrong. To your Alaric Meadows, to show him you can find things out." He leaned closer, and the wine on his breath was a thing you could have cut. "I have buried three lords who knew things, and a good many more who didn't. I never saw that the knowing did the first sort any good."

"I mean to know it," Orryn said.

"That is the dearest thing a poor boy can buy."

It was said without malice, as one might say that bread was dear in a bad year, and it went in all the same. Orryn thought of the coins he did not have, and of the purse in Wat's coat that was not his, and of the dun horse standing in a stranger's stable on a stranger's word, and did not answer, because there was no answer. The old man watched him not answering. Something in the weathered face shifted, a very little.

"I will give you a thing," Ser Wystan said. "One. And you will not ask for a second tonight, nor tomorrow, nor on any day you cannot pay for it."

"I understand."

"You do not, but you will." He turned the cup once more. "The one that burned did not go out of Grandview. It came in."

Orryn sat very still.

He had thought, all through the long afternoon on the road, that there were two letters and that both had gone west. He had built a small neat story on it: two letters sent, no answer returned, one thrown on the fire in shame, one kept in a coffer against the day it was needed. It was a good story. It had a place for everything. And now the old man had put one word into it, in, and the whole shape turned over like a carter's board tipped on a stone.

"Someone answered," he said slowly. "From Storm's End. There was an answer."

"I said a letter burned."

"A letter that came in. That is an answer."

"I said a letter burned, boy." But the great scarred head had turned, and the good eye was no longer on Orryn at all. It was on the gate.

The cart had come in. Orryn had not heard it over his own pulse. It stood in the yard with the mule steaming in the shafts, and Pate was already down and walking the animal's head round, slowly, with his big hands gentle on the bridle. Wat had not got down. He sat on the board with the reins slack in his fingers and his soft Reach face turned a little away, the way a man looks at the weather, and he looked at nothing, and he had been looking at nothing for the length of time it takes to hear a good deal.

"Your man has long ears," Ser Wystan said, very quietly.

"He is not my man. He is Lord Alaric's."

"That is worse." The old man drained his cup and set it down with a click, and in that moment he looked every one of his eighty-one years, and something more, a tiredness that was older than the wine. "That is all, then. That is the one thing. Do not come asking for the second in front of a groom, nor in front of anyone who will write it down."

"Ser Wystan—"

"Go and see to your cart, boy."

It was not unkind, and it was not a request. Orryn rose. His knees were not quite steady. He had gained something and he did not know yet what it weighed, only that it was heavier than it looked, and that he had spent a good part of what small credit he had to get it. He crossed the yard. Pate did not look up. Wat climbed down from the board, one careful leg after the other, and brushed his coat.

"Master Orryn," Wat said.

"Wat." He wanted to ask how much he had heard. He did not ask. Wat would not lie, he thought; Wat would simply choose what came first. "You made good time."

"Aye. The mule's sound." Wat looked at the dun gelding being led from the stable by a stable-boy who had plainly been told to wait for the quality. He looked at the old knight on the bench. He looked, last of all, at Orryn, and the corner of his mouth moved the little that it always moved. "His lordship will want to know who you sat with."

"An old knight who served at Grandview. We spoke of Grandview."

"Aye. I'll tell him that."

The innkeeper came out then. Orryn had seen her when he rode in without learning her name, a stout woman in a floury apron with forearms like a baker's and a way of standing in a doorway that said the door was hers, and had been her mother's, and would be her daughter's. She had a slate in one hand. She did not look at Orryn; she looked, with great courtesy, at Wat.

"Good evening to you, goodman. That's a Meadows cart, I see by the harness. Two men, a mule, a horse, and the young master's supper, and a bed apiece, I'd say, and the oats. It comes to four silver stags and a few pence. I'll not make it hard for his lordship."

Wat did not reach for the purse. He took his time. He thought it over like a man weighing a cheese.

"I'll pay the mule and the men," he said at length. "That's his lordship's order, for the road. The horse is not his lordship's." He did not look at the dun. "The young master's supper and his bed, I've no word on. I paid the ford at his say-so, and that's in the book. I'll not pay the inn at it, not without I'm told."

There was a small silence in the yard. Orryn felt the stable-boy looking at his boots, and Pate looking at nothing, and the innkeeper looking, now, at him, with an expression that was neither hard nor soft, only very patient, the look of a woman who has seen a great many young gentlemen discover what an evening costs.

Behind him, on the bench, he heard Ser Wystan's cup tapped once on the board, and did not turn to see whether it was a signal or a joke.

Four silver stags. The ford that morning had cost him less. He thought of the ledger in Alaric's hands, and the line that would be written there, and under it the other line, the one about a boy who had learned something from a drunk old man in a yard and then could not pay for his own bed.

"Well, young ser?" the innkeeper said.

What does Orryn do?

1. Say plainly that he cannot pay tonight, and ask the innkeeper what work or pledge she would take in place of coin, as honestly as he can.

2. Ask Wat to put the supper and the bed in the book as a debt in Orryn's own name, to be repaid, and have it written out before the innkeeper.

3. Sleep in the stable with the horse, and ask for nothing but water, so that nothing is owed by anyone.

4. Something else of your own.
