The bench was higher than it had looked from below. Orryn climbed onto it with the mazer held in both hands and was at once a great deal farther from the floor than he liked, with the black beams close over his head and the draught at his ankles and five faces tilted up at him like plates along a dresser. Far off at the middle board a knight roared at a joke. Nobody there turned round.

He had been afraid of this moment since the stair, and had not known why. Now that it was on him he found that it was only a question of what to put in his hands. There were things he carried that could not be said in any hall: the nine days, and the letter that had come in and been burned, and the old man on the inn bench with his one good eye. He set them aside one by one, as he might have laid away knives, and was surprised how light it left him. There was little in him that was not either dangerous or ordinary, and the ordinary part he could give.

"I climbed the tower," he said.

His voice came out smaller than he had meant it to, and the beams took it. Perkin, below him, said, "What tower?" and Alyn said, "Hush, it's his turn," in the voice of a man settling down to enjoy himself.

"At Grandview. The old watch-tower, the one we're forbidden. The stairs are rotten past the second landing, so I went up the outside, by the window-slits and the ivy." He swallowed. "You can see three keeps from the top. I was sick afterwards, behind the kennels. I'd do it again. I'm proud of that. That's the first."

"To the tower," said Alyn, and raised his cup, and the others raised theirs. Orryn drank from the mazer. The clove-sweet wine went down warm, and he felt it arrive in his chest like a small hand.

"Nobody climbs the outside of a tower," said Piers Fallow.

He had not moved. He sat with his chin up and his green sleeve on the board, watching Orryn from under his dark curls, and the words had come out flat and quick, the way a boy throws a stone at a bird he does not expect to hit. "The stone sweats and the ivy comes away. How many windows?"

"Eleven," said Orryn, and was surprised to find that he knew. "I counted them going up, so as not to look down. There should have been twelve. One was bricked over."

A silence. Orryn heard the fire in the hearth, and the fat spitting. Piers looked at him a long moment. "Eleven," he said, as a man repeats a price he has not decided to pay.

He did not say that he believed it. But he did not say that he did not, and something in his mouth gave a little, as if he had been handed back a thing he had lent and not expected to see again. "I'd have been sick at the first window," he said, to his cup.

"You'd have been sick at the ivy," said Perkin, and Alyn said, "Leave him his tower, Fallow," and nobody pressed it further.

"Go on, Grandview," said Perkin. "The sorry one."

That was harder. He had not chosen it in advance, and found that it had chosen itself, as such things do, from somewhere near the bottom. He looked at the grain of the table instead of at their faces.

"At Grandview, at Harvest Home, there was a stable boy who sang in the sept. He had the high part in the Mother's hymn. His voice broke in the middle of it. It just went, all at once, like a cart through ice. And I laughed." He stopped. "I was beside my lord uncle in the front, and everyone could hear. Then the whole sept was laughing, because I had started it. He never sang again after that. He went back to the stables and he doesn't look at me when I pass." His mouth had gone dry. "I'm sorry for it. That's the second."

Nobody drank at once. The silence this time was a different thing. Perkin had put down his bread. Baldric's watchful face had lost, for a moment, its watching. And from the head of the table Osric Dunmere said, quietly and precisely, as a man lays down a coin, "The Mother asks mercy of us all. That is a true penitence."

He touched the star. It was almost kind. Orryn, who had been braced for something else, did not know where to put it.

"Everyone's done worse," said Alyn at last, and for once the lazy voice was lower than usual. "I put a boy's head in the horse-trough when I was twelve and called it a christening. He didn't speak to me for a year. Drink."

They drank to the stable boy. It was a grave small toast, and no one jested over it, and when it was done Orryn found that his hands were shaking less.

"The third," said Perkin, beside himself. "The one you never told. And it's got to be a good one, mine was the chicken—"

"We know about the chicken."

"It was a sad chicken."

Orryn took a breath. He thought of the other things. They went past him like riders on a road, and he let them go by, one after another, and kept his seat. What was left was so small and so foolish that he almost could not say it.

"I am afraid of geese," he said.

There was a moment of perfect stillness. Then Perkin put his face down on the board and made a noise like a kettle.

"A gander got me when I was six. At the pond behind the kennels. It had me by the seat of my breeches and it would not let go, and I screamed for my mother, and she did not come, because the whole of Grandview thought it was funny. I've never forgiven it." He drew himself up. "I still go the long way round a yard if there is one in it. I have never told anyone that."

Alyn was gasping. Baldric, quite openly, had a hand over his mouth. Even Piers had turned his head away and was shaking, and Osric, at the head, was looking very hard at the ceiling, and the corners of his thin mouth were pressed together so tightly that they had gone white.

"To the geese!" Alyn cried, and lifted his cup so high that the wine went over his wrist. "To the whole bloody gaggle of them!"

"To the geese!" they shouted, and Orryn drank, and the wine went down the wrong way, and he coughed and wheezed and nearly lost his footing on the bench. Hands steadied him on every side. Somebody caught his belt, somebody took the mazer, and he was down on the boards again with Perkin pounding his back and saying "Welcome, welcome, you absolute goose," and he was laughing so hard he could not see.

When it was over Alyn rose, and the others after him, and the tall boy put out a hand and took Orryn's forearm in the grip that knights use, and held it. His palm was warm and dry. "Board's yours, Grandview," he said. "Nobody asks again. I'd not trust Perkin on the matter of geese, though. He'll have told the kitchen by morning."

"There'll be one in your bed by Thursday," said Perkin, with great satisfaction.

"Not a live one," Baldric said, from Orryn's other side, so quietly that only he heard. He had slipped round the end of the bench while the others shouted. "You have the best bed, you know. The one by the window. The wall does not sweat there. And a boy who counts windows with his life hanging from them is a boy Lyman Verrall can use. Tell him so when you've a mind, not the lord. Verrall can carry it up, and the lord will think it his own idea." His brown eyes were steady and friendly and gave nothing at all away. "It would be useful. To you, and to him."

The word sat in Orryn's ear a little too neatly. But the boy had given him a bed and a name, and the fire was warm, and he was a good deal drunk, so he only nodded, and did not say whether he would.

They climbed the stair together, five boys and the noise they made. The brindled hound on the landing lifted its head at the din, thought better of it, and lowered it again. A serving girl met them at the turn with a steaming pitcher; the cook, it seemed, had relented. Alyn took it from her with a bow, said something that made her colour and not look at him, and carried it up himself.

Later, in the long low chamber, in the dark, with the shutters closed and the tallow out by Osric's hand at the third bell, Orryn lay on the bed by the window in Lem's too-large shirt with the sleeves unrolled. Down the room, Perkin was telling somebody in a whisper that the best of it had been the gander, the breeches, the seat of the breeches. Someone told him to shut up. Someone else laughed into a pillow. A pallet creaked, and a chest lid knocked, and the breathing of five boys began to settle, uneven and quick and then slow.

He had come through two days and a river and a lord's study and a kneeling and a washing to reach this place, and he had expected it to be the lord's keep. It had turned out to be this: a room of boys in the dark, one of whom would put a goose in his bed. He did not know what any of them would cost him. He lay and listened to them breathe.

What does Orryn do?

1. In the morning, ask Lyman Verrall for work with the household accounts, as Baldric suggested, and begin to work off what his keep costs Grassfield with his habit of counting everything.

2. Spend the first days with the squires in the yard under Ser Everard Blythe, the master-at-arms, learning the sword and the horses, so that the board takes him for one of themselves before anyone else takes his measure.

3. Write the letter he owes Lady Alysanne, tonight or tomorrow, from the squires' chamber, with five boys able to see over his shoulder.

4. Something else of your own.
