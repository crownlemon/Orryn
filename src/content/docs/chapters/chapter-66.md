Goldengrove, the Reach, 208 AC

Lem was waiting under the gate with his thumbs in his belt and the look of a man who had been well fed.

He was one of Lord Alaric’s men-at-arms, long and loose in the joints, with a nose the sun had peeled raw, and he had left Grassfield half a day before the rest of them, on the fastest horse in the stable, so that Lord Rowan should not be surprised. Somewhere between Grassy Vale and the Mander he had stopped being a rider and become a guest. He pulled his cap off as Lord Alaric came under the arch.

“Horses go by the river wall, my lord. It’s cool there.” He dropped his voice, as though it were a thing that ought not to be spread about. “And there’s water. Troughs of it. Nobody measures.”

“Then don’t tell Ser Wyman,” said Lord Alaric, and swung down, and stood a moment with his hand on the saddle, as if the yard were moving.

Orryn knew how he felt. They had been three days on the road, through country so dry that the rooks walked about the stubble with their beaks hanging open, and the flagstones of Goldengrove would not keep still under him either. He was fourteen, a Grandison of Grandview in the Stormlands, and for the better part of a year he had been squire to Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep, in Grassy Vale, three hard days to the south. He took his lord’s reins and Rook’s together and stood holding both while a strange castle went about its business around him.

The yard was paved with the same pale stone as the walls, and everything, the stone and the stables and the very dust, smelled of apples, sweet and a little gone, the way a press-house smells when the cider is running.

He looked along the stable doors for a green cloak and chestnut hair, did not find them, and was angry with himself for having looked.

Lord Rowan received them in his hall, on his feet.

It was a long hall and a high one. On the wall behind the dais someone long dead had carved the golden tree of Rowan in oak and gilded every leaf, so that its branches spread over the high table like a roof. Candles burned along the boards, and the moths had found them. Orryn had a confused impression of green and gold and white, of a great many faces turned the same way, and of the heat that came off all of them.

Lord Rycherd Rowan was Lord of Goldengrove and hereditary Marshal of the Northmarch, an office his house held by blood and not by the gift of Lord Leo Tyrell, who ruled the Reach from Highgarden; Ser Lucan had made very sure the squires knew it. He was fifty-three, broad through the chest and grey at the temples, with a heavy face the weather had been working at for a long time. Orryn had seen him at Grassfield when Lord Alaric’s daughter Leonette was married, clasping hands with Lord Armond Caswell of Bitterbridge, Lord Alaric’s goodfather, in the little chamber off the hall. He did not look any gladder to see them now than he had looked then.

Lady Clarice brought the bread and salt herself. She was taller than her lord, in a dark gown with silver at the throat and wrists: a Hightower of Oldtown by birth and the daughter of a Targaryen princess, Ser Lucan had told them, and neither thing was to be mentioned. The bread was still warm, and the salt came in a silver dish with a lid made like an apple.

Lord Alaric broke the bread and dipped it and ate, and Lord Rowan watched him chew.

“There,” said Lord Rowan. His voice was slow and grey, like a cart on a long road. “You’ve eaten under my roof. Whatever you came to ask, you may ask it safe.” He did not say now. He turned to his steward. “Open the Grassfield cask. Here, at the high table. A man who carries one cask three days in this heat means it to be drunk.”

Orryn could not tell whether that was a courtesy or a joke. He looked to his lord’s face for help, and could not tell whether Lord Alaric knew either.

They gave Lord Alaric the seat at Lord Rowan’s right hand, and Ser Lucan Marwick, the Grassfield household knight who had ridden with them, a place among Lord Rowan’s knights below the dais, where he sat as straight as a lance and looked at nobody. Orryn stood behind his lord’s chair with the flagon, and nobody looked at him, which suited him very well.

Ser Triston Rowan sat at his mother’s left: the heir to Goldengrove, three-and-twenty, fair-haired and handsome, with his father’s breadth and none of his weight, who asked after the wells of Grassy Vale before he asked after anything else. Beside him sat the girl he was to marry.

Lady Joy Penrose was sixteen or near it, and Orryn would have known her for a princess’s daughter without being told. Her hair was so fair it was nearly silver, and she wore it loose down her back where it could be seen, over a gown of rose silk made for some cooler place. Her father was Lord Ronnel Penrose, who sat on the king’s council and kept the king’s purse; her mother was Princess Elaena, the king’s own kinswoman. She had come up from King’s Landing to be shown the house she would have when she married, and by Ser Wendell’s account she had been shown it twice.

She was being shown it still. When a serving man carried the Grassfield cask up the steps, she told him where to set it down, and then, before he had done so, somewhere else.

“It’s Father’s table, Joy,” said the girl across from her.

That was Lady Roslin, Lord Rowan’s daughter, seventeen, with a long neck and a face that usually gave nothing away. It was giving something away now. Two spots of colour sat high on her cheeks.

“It will be Triston’s,” said Lady Joy, quite pleasantly.

“Not while Father’s sitting at it.”

“Roslin,” said Lady Clarice, and that was the end of it; though Lady Joy smiled into her cup, and Lady Roslin did not.

Past Lady Roslin, a young man of twenty or so was already drinking as though the hall were on fire and the wine the only water in it. That was Robin Penrose, Lady Joy’s brother and heir to Parchments, with her pale hair and a soft pink face that did not look as if it had ever been out in weather. Parchments was a Stormlands castle, sworn to Storm’s End as Grandview was, and Orryn had somehow expected its heir to look like a Stormlander. Robin Penrose looked like the court, so far as Orryn had any notion what the court looked like.

Where the high table ran out sat Ser Wendell Webber, the short bald knight who had brought them up the hill talking of swans, and his lady. Lady Mina Webber was Lord Rowan’s sister. The first thing anyone noticed about her was that she stood a head taller than her husband; the second, that she had her brother’s heavy face, drawn long and narrow on her as though someone had pulled it. She had not looked at Lord Alaric once since the bread and salt. Ser Wendell had looked at little else.

The cask was broached. The cupbearer filled Lord Rowan’s cup first, and Lord Rowan drank, and set it down.

“Pears,” he said.

“Perry, my lord,” said Lord Alaric. “From the old trees by the mill. The year before last, when there was a year.”

“I thought only smallfolk drank perry,” said Lady Joy.

Lord Rowan looked down the table at her, not for long. “Then the smallfolk drink well,” he said, and held out his cup.

Ser Alyn came up from the knights’ benches while the first dishes were going round.

Orryn saw him before he was meant to, which was the trouble with Alyn: there was never any not seeing him. He was eighteen, the second son of Lord Merryweather of Longtable, and a year ago he had been senior squire at Grassfield, who stole flasks and sang filthy songs at the short table and had taught Orryn which end of a horse to stand at. Lord Alaric had knighted him in the sept and sent him away the next morning, and Orryn had been told never to say why. He wore Rowan’s green and gold now, and wore them well.

He went to one knee at the foot of the dais. “My lord.”

“Ser Alyn.” Lord Alaric did not rise. “You look well. Lord Rowan tells me you are useful.”

“I try to be, my lord.”

“Good,” said Lord Alaric, and went back to his trencher.

That was all, and it was perfectly courteous. Alyn’s smile stayed exactly where it was, and only someone who had watched that smile for a year would have seen it go empty for a moment, like a window when the candle has been carried out of the room. As he rose, his eye found Orryn behind the chair.

“Grandview,” he said, low. “They’ve let you out.”

Orryn’s face went hot to the ears, and he said nothing. You will be courteous to him, Lady Jeyne had told her husband at Grassfield. You will not be kind. Nor will Master Grandison. Orryn had not known then which thing she meant him not to be kind about. He held the flagon very straight, and Alyn grinned at him as if he had said something clever, and went back down to the benches.

“The boy,” said Lord Alaric a little later, without turning his head. “Below us. The first table.”

Orryn had found him already.

Lord Harys Oakheart sat at the head of the first table below the dais, as near to the high table as anyone could sit and still not be at it. He was Lord of Old Oak, and had been since before he could walk. His father had died in the rebellion, the year Daemon Blackfyre rose against the king, while the Oakhearts stood divided; afterward Lord Leo had taken the boy from his mother and given him to Lord Rowan to raise, so that Old Oak should behave itself. Lord Leo’s lady, Lady Arlanna, was the boy’s grandmother. She had chosen him for Lord Alaric’s daughter Elyce, and sent word to Grassfield by her own daughter, and asked neither Lady Jeyne nor Lord Rowan first.

He was as thin as a lath, as Ser Cleyton Caswell had told it at Grassfield, and as stiff as if he had swallowed a poker. Three green oak leaves on gold were worked on the breast of his doublet, very new and very bright, and he sat with both hands flat on the board, as though he were holding it down. Next to him, where Ser Wendell had almost certainly put her, sat a girl.

“There’s a trout come up,” said Lord Alaric. “With almonds. Take it down to Lord Harys, with my compliments.”

It was a whole fish, brown with butter and scattered with almonds, and it smelled so good that Orryn’s belly turned over; he had eaten nothing since a heel of bread at noon. He carried it down the steps and along the end of the first table, past knights who did not look at him and squires who did, and set it before the Lord of Old Oak.

“Lord Alaric Meadows sends you this, my lord, with his compliments.”

Lord Harys looked at the dish first. Then he looked at Orryn’s boots, which were grey with three days of road, and up from them, slowly, to his face. His own face was narrow and freckled, with brown hair cropped close as a soldier’s and a sharp nose he had not grown into, and his eyes were a pale clear green that did not blink as often as other people’s. Orryn saw his hands. The nails were bitten to the quick, all ten, and the skin beside two of them was raw.

Look at his hands, Lady Jeyne had said. She had not said it to Orryn. He had looked anyway.

“Who are you?”

“Orryn Grandison, my lord. Lord Alaric’s squire.”

“Grandison.” The boy said it as though he were putting it somewhere, to be dealt with later. “And whose man are you?”

“Lord Alaric’s, my lord.”

“And what does your house pay you?”

Ser Cleyton had told this at Grassfield as a joke against himself. It was a different thing to be asked it. Orryn found he had no answer ready but the true one.

“Nothing, my lord. I’m a squire. It goes the other way.”

“Then you’re kept.” Lord Harys looked at him a moment longer. “And whose is Lord Alaric?”

“His own, my lord.”

He did not know where it had come from, and then he did: the long table at Bitterbridge, and Ser Denys Tarly calling his lord Ser Moryn Tyrell’s man across it, and Lord Alaric answering, My own. It is a small side. Orryn had liked that very much at the time. It did not sound so well in his own mouth.

“Nobody is his own,” said Lord Harys.

“Except you, Harys,” said the girl beside him. “You belong to my uncle.”

She did not look up to say it. She was fifteen or so and tall even sitting, with Lady Mina’s long face and heavy-lidded eyes that made her look half asleep, and golden-brown hair pulled back so tightly that it seemed it must hurt. Her mouth had the look of a mouth that was bored by everything and expected to be bored by whatever came next. While Lord Harys went red to the roots of his cropped hair, she reached across him, pulled a strip of the trout loose with her fingers, and ate it.

“It’s good,” she said. “Eat it, before somebody does.”

The boy did not look at her. He looked at Orryn. “You may tell Lord Meadows that I thank him.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Go, then.”

Orryn went. Halfway up the steps he looked back. The girl was picking the almonds off the fish one at a time, and the Lord of Old Oak sat with his bitten hands flat on the board, looking at nothing whatever.

The supper went on a long while.

There were swans after all, dressed again in their own feathers, and Ser Wendell had a good deal of one. Orryn poured and carried and stood. His back ached from the saddle, and his belly, which had smelled the trout, would not stop telling him about it.

Lord Alaric drank what he was given, and each time Orryn filled the cup he heard Lady Jeyne on the clothes chest at Grassfield: You always do. Orryn did not think his lord was drunk. He was laughing at something Ser Triston had said about a mare, with his elbow on the board, more easily than Orryn had heard him laugh in a moon.

“Great friends,” Ser Wendell called up the table, waving a swan’s leg toward the first table. “My Myranda and the young lord. She has the place beside him every night.”

“So I saw,” said Lord Alaric.

“And I have one of eleven, my lord, who would do very well for that son of yours—”

“Wendell,” said Lady Mina, and he stopped as though she had pulled a rein.

It was Lady Clarice who spoke to Orryn, which he had not looked for. He was filling her cup when she said without turning, “You are Ser Steffon Grandison’s boy. The youngest.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“My sister writes that a Grandison boy promised her a letter. She is still waiting for it.” Now she did turn, and her eyes were grey and quite steady. “Would that be you?”

Her sister was Lady Alysanne Penrose, who kept Parchments while her goodbrother Lord Ronnel sat at court, and whose son Ser Addam had married Orryn’s cousin Elenei in the sept at Grandview a year ago. The letter in Orryn’s boot was Elenei’s, not Lady Alysanne’s, but he felt it all the same, folded small under his heel like a stone.

“Yes, my lady.”

“And Lady Elenei? Is she content?”

“She says so, my lady. She wrote that Parchments is drier than she thought, and that Ser Addam is kind.” He ought to have stopped there. He heard himself go on, the way a horse goes on after the rider has stopped asking. “And that Lady Alysanne is more tolerable than she feared.”

There was a short silence at that end of the table. Orryn wished very much to be under it.

Then Lady Clarice laughed. It was not a long laugh, nor a warm one, but it was real. “Tolerable,” she said. “My sister will be gratified. It is more than anyone ever said of her in Oldtown.” She turned back to her plate. “I shall tell her you are well, Master Grandison, and have not written.”

“Grandison,” said Lady Joy, who had been listening. “Is that a lordship?”

“Grandview is,” said Lady Clarice.

“And he is—?”

“A nephew.”

“Oh,” said Lady Joy, and that was all the notice Orryn had of her for the rest of the night. She was not done talking, though. When the swans were gone and the fried apples had come round, he heard her say to Lady Roslin, not much lower than she said anything else, “Poor Ser Wendell’s girl. She had quite set her heart on Old Oak, they say. Ser Wendell told me so himself.”

“Hush,” said Lady Roslin.

“Why? Everyone knows.”

Orryn could not help it; he looked down at the first table. Myranda Webber had pushed the trout away and sat half turned on the bench with her chin in her hand, watching a hound beg at the next table, while the boy beside her said something stiff to the air. Orryn had seen Leonette look at ribbons that way, ribbons she meant never to wear. If Myranda Webber had set her heart on anything, Orryn did not think it was the Lord of Old Oak. He did not think anyone else had noticed. Lady Joy certainly had not.

By then Robin Penrose had gone down with his cup to sit among the Goldengrove knights, who made room for him the way men make room for someone who is paying. He talked, and they let him. Ser Triston went down after him and stood at his shoulder, and Orryn, sent down with a jug for Ser Lucan, heard most of it from there.

It was the burnings, of course; it was always the burnings, that year. Riders had come down out of the hills on the Dornish border and fired a steading two days south of Horn Hill, killed two people and carried off a boy of ten: forty horse, the Tarly sergeant had told them at Grassfield; a hundred Dornishmen, by the tale Ser Cleyton had brought back from this very hall. Half the Reach had been riding south since, and the other half arguing about it.

“—so the Hand’s taken it into his own hands,” Robin was saying. “Prince Baelor himself, the king’s own son. He’s written to the Prince of Dorne to ask whether they happen to know of any burning.” He laughed, and slopped his cup. “As if they’d say yes! Why, my lord Hand, now you mention it. And to ask them very nicely to keep to their own side of the mountains, if it’s no trouble. And he’s sent a knight of his own down to look at the hoofprints.” He wiped his mouth. “He never so much as wrote to Lord Leo, you know. Nor to Storm’s End either. Father said so at supper and Mother told him to hush, so it must be true.”

At Grandview, all his life, Orryn had heard the name of Lord Edric Baratheon, who held Storm’s End and to whom his uncle was sworn, spoken the way a septon speaks of the Father. Something small and cold turned over inside him, and he could not have said why it should matter what a prince had not written.

“Nobody at court minds,” said Robin. “Princess Jena says—she’s the Hand’s wife, a Dondarrion, so of course the Dondarrions are everybody’s darlings now—Princess Jena says we’re all one realm these days. Dornishmen and all.” He raised his cup. “One realm!”

They laughed harder at that. Orryn did not, and did not quite know why. The Tarly sergeant had thought the riders were hungry men a long way from home, with no banner because they had none to fly, and it had seemed to Orryn the saddest thing he had ever heard, and somehow the most hopeful. If it was hungry men it was not Dorne, and if it was not Dorne, perhaps a princess could be right. He kept it to himself. He was a squire with a jug, and the knights were laughing.

“And no lord’s to take his levies over the mountains without the king’s leave,” said Robin. “Anyone who rides without it rides against the Crown. They’ll hang.”

The laughter stopped as though someone had shut a door on it.

“That isn’t what you said before,” said Ser Triston, quite courteously. “Before, you said they rode against the king’s wish.”

“It comes to the same.”

“It does not come to the same.”

“Our brother,” said Lady Mina Webber from the dais, very clearly, “is a knight of the realm, boy, and not a horse thief.”

Ser Nyles Rowan had ridden south with Ser Olyvar Tyrell and the knights who chose to follow him, with no summons from anyone. Lord Armond had told it at Grassfield: Lord Rowan had watched his brother go from the gate, and said only that he was a grown man.

Lord Rowan said nothing now either. He sat with his knife in his hand and his eyes on the Penrose boy, and the whole hall seemed to wait on what he would do; and what he did, after a long moment, was cut himself another piece of swan.

Robin Penrose laughed again, uncertainly, and found that his cup was empty. At the high table his sister was telling Lady Clarice about the sleeves they were wearing at court.

Lord Rowan’s closet was at the top of a tower above the river, up a stair so narrow that Orryn had to carry the jug before him like a candle. “Your boy can pour,” Lord Rowan had said at the foot of it. “Mine talks.” He had sent for the rest of the Grassfield perry to be brought up, and Orryn could not tell whether that was a courtesy either.

It was a small square room with one window, open for what air there was, and through it came the smell of the Mander and the sound of it going by in the dark. An old hound lay on the cold hearth. On the table, in a wooden bowl, were apples, small and smooth and yellow as butter: the first golden apples Orryn had seen since they came into Rowan’s country. Perkin Fenwick, back at Grassfield, had sworn there would be one on every pillow.

Lord Rowan sat. Lord Alaric went to the window and stood with one hand on the frame, looking down at the river as though he would like to take some of it home.

“You were told to come and ask,” said Lord Rowan. “Ask.”

Lord Alaric turned from the window. “On what terms do you keep the boy, and when do they end?”

“Leo gave him to me when he was still at the breast. His father was dead and his house was half one thing and half the other, and Leo wanted Old Oak to behave. It has. His mother holds it for him and does as she’s bid, because I have her son.” Lord Rowan said it the way another man might have said what he paid for oats. “He stays with me until he’s a man grown and knighted.”

“A boy’s a man at sixteen.”

“In law. A knight’s a knight when a knight makes him one, and I’ll not knight that boy for his nameday. I’ll knight him when he can hold a lance and hold his tongue, both.” He drank. “Seventeen, if he mends. Eighteen. Nineteen, if he goes on as he is.”

Orryn, with the jug, was counting before he could stop himself. Elyce was twelve. If the boy mended she would be sixteen, and if he went on as he was—

“And his marriage?” said Lord Alaric.

“Is Leo’s to give. Until I give the boy back, Leo has the giving of him, and nobody else. Not his mother. Not his grandmother, whatever she’s told Bitterbridge.”

“She told my wife’s stepmother, who told my wife’s father, who told me.”

“And nobody told me.” Lord Rowan set his cup down. “I heard it from Armond Caswell at my own board, over my own wine. I’ll tell you what I told him, Meadows. Your girl is the better match. I’m not blind. Meadows is an old house and a sound one, her mother’s a Caswell, and her mother’s stepmother is Lady Arlanna’s own daughter. If Leo had asked me, I’d have told him the girl would do.”

“Then where is the quarrel?”

“A guardian who is not asked is no guardian. He’s a gaoler.” The heavy face did not change. “I’ve fed that boy twelve years. I put the first sword in his hand. I put him on his first horse and picked him up when it threw him. And the Lady of Highgarden disposes of him from her embroidery frame and sends word to Bitterbridge, as if I were her steward.”

“My wife has the same complaint.”

“So Armond told me.” Lord Rowan took one of the golden apples from the bowl and turned it over in his thick fingers without eating it. “There were hopes in this house,” he said. “They were mine to disappoint.”

He did not say whose hopes. Lord Alaric did not ask. Orryn thought of Lady Mina, who had not looked at his lord once since the bread and salt, and of a girl picking the almonds off a fish, and he poured more perry, very carefully, so that nobody would have to look at him.

“I told Lord Armond,” said Lord Alaric, “that there would be no betrothal while the boy was a hostage. And that I would hear it from Lord Leo’s own mouth before I heard it from anyone else’s.”

“Then we agree on more than I looked for.” Lord Rowan put the apple back. “Now I’ll ask a thing of you, since you came so far to ask one of me. Before the year turns I mean to ride to Highgarden and say this in Leo’s hall, to his face, with his lady at her frame to hear it: that Goldengrove keeps the boy, and Goldengrove is asked.” His eyes came up. “I’d have you standing beside me when I say it.”

“Why me?”

“Because it’s your daughter. Because one lord with a complaint is a man in a temper, and two are a thing Leo has to answer. And because a Moryn man would be no use to me, and a Garth man worse.”

Ser Garth was Lord Leo’s heir and Ser Moryn his second son, and half the Reach had already chosen between them.

“And whose man would I be, standing there?”

“Mine, they’d say.” Lord Rowan moved his big shoulders. “They say you’re Moryn’s already. Let them say something new.”

Lord Alaric was quiet. Below the window the river went on talking to itself.

“I promised my wife,” he said at last, “that I would promise nothing here.”

“Then you’ve kept your promise. Is that a no?”

“It’s neither.”

“Armond tells me you once took a day to choose a king.”

Orryn’s hand went still on the jug.

“The better part of a day,” said Lord Alaric.

“I’ll give you longer. Until the year turns.” Lord Rowan leaned back. “As for the Penrose boy, most of what he says is true, and the worst of it is wrong. The men who’ve gone south without leave won’t hang. Knights with names don’t hang. They’ll not be thanked, that’s all, and the king will know who rode.” He laid his hand flat on the table. “My brother is one of them.”

“And Leo was never written to.”

“That part I believe. The boy hasn’t the wit to make it up.” Lord Rowan drank off his perry. “It’s a fine thing to watch Highgarden passed over. It would be finer if I didn’t know how it’s done.”

“How is it done?” said Lord Alaric.

“The way it was done to me. You go round a man. You don’t tell him. You let him hear it from somebody’s drunken son.” He stood, and the old hound lifted its head at last. “Go to bed, Meadows. You’ve had my bread and my news. The rest is Leo’s.”

There was no golden apple on Lord Alaric’s pillow. Orryn looked, and was obscurely disappointed in Perkin.

His lord pulled off his own boot before Orryn could get to it, and sat on the edge of the bed holding it.

“Nineteen,” he said. He did not seem to be saying it to Orryn. After a while he set the boot down beside its fellow, very neatly, as if that mattered. “Jeyne will say I rode three days to be told to wait.”

Orryn thought that Lady Jeyne would say a good deal more than that, and did not say so.

He woke before it was light, to the sound of a lance breaking.

Lord Alaric slept on his back with an arm across his eyes. Orryn found his boots, and the letter in the left one, and went down. On the stair he passed Osric Dunmere, Ser Lucan’s squire, on his knees at a window with the silver star of the Seven in his fist, who did not look round. He meant to see to Rook, whose heel Ser Wyman, Grassfield’s master of horse, had said might hold another moon. But the stables ran along the tilting yard, and he got no further than the rail.

At the far end of the lists a knight in plain steel sat a big chestnut with his lance up. At the near end, picking himself out of the dirt, was a thickset youth in Rowan green who had lost his helm, his lance, his horse and very nearly his breeches. That was Bryce Webber, Ser Wendell’s second son, sixteen and built like a keg, with his mother’s long chin set on his father’s short neck.

“Thirty-one!” called someone on the rail, where four or five squires in Rowan green sat eating apples. “Webber’s kissed the ground more times than he’s kissed a girl.”

“He kissed the cook’s girl.”

“She charged him double, and he paid in coppers.”

Bryce Webber spat dust. “Shut your mouths, or I’ll shut them.” Nobody did, and he didn’t.

The knight on the chestnut pulled off his helm, and of course it was Alyn. His hair was dark with sweat, and he looked down at Bryce Webber from the saddle with great kindness. “Up you get, Webber. Your father’s at a window somewhere, and he’ll want to know what it cost him.”

The squires howled. Bryce Webber went the colour of a brick and limped off after his horse. Alyn saw Orryn, and grinned.

“Grandview! Did you come three days to watch me knock Webbers down?”

“I came with Lord Alaric, ser.”

“I know who you came with.” He swung down and tossed his reins to the nearest squire without looking to see whether they were caught. They were. He put his elbows on the rail beside Orryn, and Orryn smelled leather and horse and sweat, and moved a little way along, and was angry with himself for that as well.

He had thought on the road about how he would do it. He did it now, before he could think about it any more.

“Ser Androw Ashford was at Grassfield, ser. He asked me to tell you that your lady mother has written to you four times since the new moon, and your sister twice, and you’ve answered neither. He said that Lady Wylla says a knight may write a letter without loss of honour. She says she has seen it done.”

Alyn laughed. Then he stopped, and looked out at the empty lists, where the dust was settling.

“What would I write her?” he said. “Dear Mother, Lord Alaric knighted me one day and sent me off the next, and you’ll never guess why?” He turned his head. “You know why.”

Orryn said nothing. He looked at the rail, at the grain of the wood worn silver by a hundred years of elbows, and said nothing at all.

“Good lad,” said Alyn.

It was what you said to a dog that had come when it was called. Orryn knew that, and went warm to the ears anyway, and could not have said which of the two things made him want to hit somebody.

“Perkin sends his love,” he said, too fast. “He says the yard’s gone very dull.”

“His love! Seven hells. Tell Perkin I’ve had better offers from Webber’s sisters.” Alyn looked at him sidelong. “You’ve gone red, Grandview.”

“It’s hot.”

“I’ll wager Perkin said you would. Go red and forget.”

“Yes, ser.”

“And you didn’t forget.” Alyn looked at him a moment longer than he needed to. “They have made you proper.”

A Rowan knight called him from the stables. He pushed off the rail, and said over his shoulder, “You’ve met the little lord? He’ll have asked what your house pays you. He asks everybody. He asked me the first day, and I told him a horse and a sword and all the Webbers I can knock down.” He grinned. “If you want to see something, go down to the river meadow. He rides there before anyone’s up. It’s the only hour in the day he isn’t a little shit.” He took an apple from a squire on the rail, a small golden one the boy had not yet bitten, and tossed it underhand to Orryn, who nearly dropped it. “For Perkin. Tell him there’s one on every pillow.”

Orryn put the apple inside his shirt and walked Rook down to the river.

The meadow lay between the castle hill and the Mander, under willows the river had kept alive, and the grass there was still green, the only green grass he had seen in a moon. An old groom sat on a fence with a bridle across his knees. Out on the grass, with nobody else to watch him, a boy on a long-legged grey was cantering in wide circles.

He rode with his heels down and his hands low and quiet, and the grey went for him as though it had been asked very nicely. At the end of each circle he leaned forward and said something into its ear, and once, when it shied at a heron getting up out of the reeds, he sat it without seeming to notice and laughed out loud. Then he brought it back to a walk, rode to the fence and swung down, and crouched beside the old groom to run his bitten fingers down the grey’s off foreleg, talking the whole while, not to the horse now but to the man, as one talks to someone whose answer one wants.

Like someone who likes horses, Elyce had said, with her hand flat on Rook’s neck.

The Lord of Old Oak looked up and saw Orryn.

It happened all at once. He stood, and his back went straight, and whatever had been in his face a moment before was put away somewhere Orryn could not see it. He took the reins from the groom without a word to him, mounted, and sat the grey as a lord sits, chin up and reins short, and rode across the meadow at a walk.

“Grandison.”

“My lord.”

He looked at Rook, all of him, the way he had looked at Orryn’s boots. “Whose horse is that?”

“House Penrose’s, my lord. He’s lent.”

“Then he isn’t yours either.” The boy looked away down the river, at the cracked mud banks and the dead reeds and the brown water moving between them. When he spoke again it was in a different voice, quick and low, as though he did not mean to be heard asking. “Does she ride?”

He did not say who. Orryn did not need him to.

“Yes, my lord. She has a colt that’s been throwing her for a moon.” He thought of Elyce on her upturned pail in the long stable, with her chin on the bay’s neck. “She keeps getting back on.”

Lord Harys considered that for a while, sitting very straight.

“Good,” he said. He turned the grey and rode away along the river. Orryn watched him go. He was halfway to the willows before his back loosened, and he leaned forward to say something to the horse.

Orryn led Rook back up the hill with the apple inside his shirt, where it would not bruise. He had gone red, as Perkin said he would. He had not forgotten.

Elyce would want to know. He would tell her that the Lord of Old Oak rode like someone who liked horses, because it was true, and because it was what she had asked. Then she would look at him with great severity, the way she did, and he would tell her the rest.
