"Yes," said Orryn.

He had not known what he would say until it was out, and then it seemed to have been there all along, waiting behind his teeth. "If I hear anything, I'll tell you. Before anyone."

The mere lapped at the wall. Osric was silent for so long that Orryn thought he had gone to sleep, or been offended.

"I did not ask for a promise," he said at last.

"I know."

"Lord Alaric might not like it."

"No." Orryn thought about that, as honestly as a boy can who has made up his mind. It was not the same as carrying tales, he told himself. Osric had asked only after his own matter, and had asked it fairly, and had told Orryn the worst of himself first. A man who did that was owed something. "But I don't see why he shouldn't knight you. Soon. You're the senior squire. Ser Everard has never once had to tell you a thing twice. You're Ser Marlon's son, and nobody could say you weren't fit."

He heard Osric draw breath, as a man does who is choosing among several sentences.

"You speak as a Grandison," said Osric.

"What does that mean?"

"It means you believe that a knighthood is a thing one is, and must only wait to be told. For most of us it is a thing that must be asked for, and paid for." The voice was neither unkind nor warm; it was exact. "A destrier. Harness and sword. The vigil, and the feast after, and a gift for every man who stands witness. And a lord willing to lay a hand on my shoulder before his whole household, and to lose me from his stirrup. Lord Alaric does not spend his favour lightly. I have a small purse, Orryn, and a name that opens nothing. You have a name that opens every door in the Stormlands and, I think, no purse at all. I should not like you to mistake the one for the other."

Orryn's face burned in the dark. He had been told, he understood, that he was kind and that he was a fool, in the same breath, and could not find a seam between them to take hold of.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Do not be. It does you credit that you do not know it." A pause, and a rustle of straw as Osric turned on his side. "I will remember that you said it. Goodnight."

Orryn lay and looked at the rafters. It was a long while before he noticed that Piers had stopped snoring.

*

In the morning there were oatcakes and honey in the hall, and Lady Edda sent each of them off with a twist of honey cakes in a cloth, and told Orryn that he was too thin and that she should speak to Lord Alaric's cook. Ser Cedric clapped his brother on the back and Lady Willa gave Orryn a small, private nod. Merel stood on the causeway wall and said that she should ask about the darn again when he came back, and that she meant to be properly polite about it next time.

Ser Marlon walked them to the gate. "Tell his lordship," he said to Orryn, "that Dunmere is his, to command." It was said as a man says a thing he has said often, and he looked at Osric a moment longer than he looked at the others, and said nothing to him at all.

They rode back through the white heat with the dust rising behind them, and passed the Fallow house without stopping, its painted shutters closed against the glare. Osric rode in front again. For a mile or more Piers kept behind, and then, without any change in his face, brought his cob up beside Orryn's.

"You heard it all, then," said Orryn.

"Aye. Every word." Piers did not look at him. "I was not meant to, I suppose."

"No."

"He's right, you know. About the price." He picked at the cob's mane. "My father paid for a favour to put me on that bench. He has never told me what it was. I could ask Lord Alaric and I'd be told, but then I should have to hear it." He gave a short laugh with no mirth in it. "You can promise a thing like that because it costs you nothing. Everyone's got a price, Grandview. Yours only falls to somebody else."

"That isn't fair."

"No. But it's the truth, which is what Osric would tell you, and the truth is the thing he tells everyone." Piers did turn his head then, and his look was not unfriendly. "I'm not saying he's false. I'm saying he's honest, and that's worse for a man who has made him a promise. Don't you wonder what he'll say when his aunt asks him what you told him?"

Orryn had not wondered. He wondered now, all the way down the river road, with the plank bridge booming under him and the tanneries stinking, and the honey cakes sticky in his saddlebag. Septa Lenora asked, and Osric answered, because it would be a lie to be silent. He had said so himself, in the dark. Orryn had heard it, and had gone on to promise him something anyway.

"And I don't want telling about me," said Piers, as they came into the town. "My father will know before the lord has finished saying it."

*

They came up the hill to Grassfield a little before the noon bell, with the dust lying thick on the cobbles of the gate. Lord Alaric was crossing the yard with Master Verrall and a ledger under the steward's arm, and he stopped when he saw them, and sent the steward on with a nod, and stood quite still with his hands behind him. Osric dismounted and bowed and gave his thanks, exactly, and went off to the barracks with his back very straight. Piers bowed, and followed. Orryn was left holding his bridle in the middle of the yard.

"Well, Orryn," said Lord Alaric. The hazel eyes were mild. "Two tables. What did they ask you?"
