The dawn drill had been Lord Alaric's rule since Osric's fall at the beech, and for ten days the squires had come down to the yard in the grey to hit one another with blunted steel in air that was very nearly cool. By the time the sun cleared the stable roof it was not cool at all. Ser Everard Blythe, who taught only in the mornings because by noon he could no longer be trusted with a sword or a pupil, had moved his stool twice to keep ahead of the shade, and the dust the boys raised hung over the yard like flour over a mill floor.

Orryn had been given Perkin, which was kind of Ser Everard and bad for both of them. Perkin Fenwick was fifteen, short and wiry and gap-toothed, and he hit with the cheerful inaccuracy of a boy who expects to be hit back. Orryn was taller and slower and had spent a moon learning how little that signified. His shield arm shook. The grip Baldric had taught him at the quintain was still a thing he thought about rather than a thing he did, and each time he remembered it Perkin's blade was already somewhere else.

"Hold," said Ser Everard. He did not shout. The master-at-arms never shouted, which Orryn had at first taken for gentleness and had come to think was a matter of saving breath. "Hold, all of you. We have company."

Ser Androw Ashford had come in at the yard gate on foot, his sleeves rolled to the elbow and the old glove on his sword hand, with Lord Alaric beside him talking with both hands, as he did when he had thought of something lately and liked it. Orryn had seen the Ashford knight across a supper table, on the bridge, and over a farrier's anvil, but this was the first time he had seen him with nothing between him and the open sky. He looked larger out of doors. It was the length of the arms, Orryn decided, and the habit of standing with his weight a little back on his heels.

"Ser Androw tells me he has endured two days of civility and would like to be hit by somebody," said Alaric. "I told him there would be a line."

"There is no line," said Ser Everard, with dignity, and looked about for a volunteer.

Ser Lucan Marwick had already come out of the armoury with a shield on his arm. He was Osric's master and the stiffest man in Grassfield, a household knight of thirty-eight from a small landed house of the Reach, and he had a way of arriving at a thing a moment before it was asked for, as though the asking were a formality he had been too courteous to wait upon. He bowed to the guest from the neck. Ser Androw took a shield from the rack, tried the strap, and shifted it half a finger up his forearm.

They fought with rebated swords for about as long as it takes to say the long grace. Ser Lucan fought as he did everything, correctly. He opened high, then low, then high again, each stroke exactly where the books put it, and Orryn, who had watched him drill the squires through those same strokes on a dozen mornings, found he could have named them before they came. Ser Androw seemed able to do the same. He gave ground without hurry and took what came on the shield, and once on the blade, and went on waiting until the third high stroke, when he stepped inside it and rapped the flat of his sword across Ser Lucan's thigh. Ser Lucan said, "One." Some while later he said, "Two," and lowered his shield.

"You were taught by a Reach master."

"By three, ser. The first two did not agree."

"Well fought." That was all, but from Ser Lucan, Orryn had begun to understand, it was very nearly an embrace.

"My turn," said Lord Alaric, and was out of his doublet before anyone could answer him.

It was a different thing to watch. Alaric was lean and restless and fast, and for the first dozen strokes he got through twice, once to the shoulder and once to the hip, and the squires along the rail made the noise squires make when their own lord is winning. Ser Androw gave ground. He gave a great deal of it, back across the yard toward the well-house with his shield high and his face quite still, and by the time the sun had reached the north wall the lord of Grassfield was breathing through his mouth and swinging wider than the books allowed. Orryn saw it before anyone. The shoulder came round a little farther each time, and the weight went forward onto the front foot. He wanted to say something, though he did not know to whom, and Ser Androw stepped inside the next stroke exactly as he had stepped inside Ser Lucan's and rapped Alaric once across the ribs, not hard.

Alaric dropped his shield arm and stood bent over his knees, laughing and sucking at the air. "You were waiting."

"My father's court is full of men who talk until the other man leaves," Androw said. "It teaches patience if it teaches nothing else."

"Seven save me, I hate it," said Alaric, with great feeling, and Ser Androw laughed as well.

On the rail beside Orryn, Alyn Merryweather, who served Lord Alaric as his other squire and was the eldest and easiest of the boys, had hooked both elbows over the top bar and let his weight hang. His shoulder was against Orryn's. It had been there since Ser Lucan's second stroke, and Orryn had stood very still ever since, as one stands with a bird on the sleeve.

"Look at his feet," Alyn said, low. "He never moves them more than he must. He does everything exactly as hard as it wants doing and not a hair harder." He considered that. "I should like to know how it feels."

Baldric Haye stood on Orryn's other side. He was sixteen, stocky and watchful, Ser Bertram Corliss's squire, and his father held the Alder Mill on paper under an award that no one at Grassfield seemed to have read. "Verrall said he'd take me down after the guests," he murmured, in the tone of a boy remarking on the weather. "To the chest. When they've gone."

"He said that about before they came."

"Yes," said Baldric, and watched Ser Androw wash his face at the well.

"Hawks this evening, when it's cooler," Alaric called across the yard, still blowing. "Wyman will have the birds out. Grandison, you'll carry mine."

Orryn said, "Yes, my lord," and only afterward remembered that he had held a falcon exactly twice in his life.

*

The falcon weighed less than he had expected, and a good deal more than his arm was willing to agree to.

Ser Wyman Latham had found him a spare glove, a man's, cracked at the thumb and too big in the fingers, so that Orryn had to keep them curled to hold it on. The falcon was grey-backed and barred below, with a plain brown hood and a pair of small bells on her legs, and Ser Wyman called her Tansy. She shifted her feet on the leather every time Rook moved, and each time her claws came through the glove's thick palm like pins through felt. Rook disliked the bells. Orryn had been told at Grandview by his father's falconer, on one of the two occasions, that he would do very well with a bird if he never moved, and it had seemed a poor thing to be praised for at the time. It seemed a great deal harder now.

"You're holding her like a wet cat," said Piers Fallow, riding up on his other side with the lure-bag at his saddle. Piers served Ser Wyman and had been in the mews since before the sun was up. His voice had no particular expression. "She isn't afraid of you. She's afraid of your sleeve. Drop your wrist, let the hand ride with the horse, and don't look at her."

"If I don't look at her I can't tell what she's doing."

"She's doing what she did the last time you looked."

Orryn dropped his wrist. Tansy shifted once more and was still.

They flew the first birds at the head of the long hayfield below the lower mill, where the grass had been left standing because there was no use in cutting it. It had gone the colour of old rope. The partridge had come down out of the burnt uplands to the last pools in the brook, and Ser Wyman, who was master of horse and hawks and a knight whose family had sold its land and whose debts the squires discussed in whispers, had walked the field the evening before with a boy and two spaniels and declared that a man could not hope for better in a dry year.

"Better than what?" said Lady Odessa.

"Than a wet one, my lady. In a wet year they scatter."

She received this as though it had been offered to her on a tray and she had not asked for it.

Half the household had come out. Orryn, riding at Alaric's left with the falcon on his fist, could see nearly all of them strung along the edge of the stubble in the low brass light. Lady Odessa Merryweather of Longtable, Alyn's mother, rode a grey palfrey in a dark green habit with a narrow gold chain at her throat, and Alyn rode at her stirrup because she had said so. Lady Jeyne and her cousin Marya Caswell kept to the shade at her other side, Marya on a mule with a flask across her saddle. Leonette, Lord Alaric's eldest daughter, rode with Lady Odessa's daughter Wylla, who was betrothed to Ser Androw. Wylla had changed her gown again, and was carrying a merlin on a glove that fitted her exactly; its hood was worked with seed pearls and she held the bird a little away from her, as though it might do something to the sleeves, which were green. Matthos, the Meadows heir, had been given a merlin of his own and a sullen look. Elyce, the youngest of the Meadows children, had been left behind at the keep with her puppy, which she regarded as an outrage, and had said so at length from the nursery window.

Ser Androw rode the bay with the new stitching at the girth. His mother, Lady Ashford, rode a plain black horse and carried no bird at all, and Orryn noticed that in the first hour she had not looked at one.

When a quail got up on the far side of the field, Matthos cast his merlin too soon. The little hawk went wide and fluttered down in the stubble, and Matthos threw his glove after it and swore in a voice that carried to the willows. Ser Wyman fetched the bird without a word. Lady Ashford, who had not turned her head for any flight until then, watched the whole of that one.

She rode up on Alaric's right a little afterward, with Ser Androw a length behind and to her far side.

"Lord Alaric, I mean to be plain with you. I saw at supper that you do not care for the other way."

"I like it well enough from people who mean it."

"Then you will find me very dull." She said it without apology. Her voice was dry and level, a voice for reading out the names in a roll. "My son spoke at table of rolls and clerks as if they were weather, something that comes over a man's land and goes again. They are not weather. They are Ser Garth Tyrell's clerks. Lord Leo is sixty-six, and he feasts and rides and forgives people, and he will not give his eldest son the keys of his own house. So Garth does the counting. He does it through men he trusts, and the men he trusts are the Redwynes." She did not look at Alaric as she said it. She looked at the stubble. "Ser Garth's wife is Lord Redwyne's daughter. You will know that Lord Redwyne sits on the king's council as Master of Ships."

"I had heard."

"Then you know how it stands. The father has the king's ear, and the daughter has Highgarden's heir, and between them they have more gold than Highgarden itself. Whatever is written in Ser Garth's rolls, Redwyne silver paid for the parchment."

Ahead, the spaniels had begun to quarter the grass with their tails going. Ser Wyman raised a hand and the whole line of riders slowed.

"And Ser Moryn doesn't care for it," said Alaric.

"Ser Moryn does not care for it, nor does Lord Rowan at Goldengrove, nor Lord Peake at Starpike, nor any lord of the Reach who holds his rights by his grandfather's word and a cup of wine. None of them has a quarrel with Ser Garth. Their quarrel is with the Redwynes becoming the Reach." She spoke as she might have read a list of linen. "Ser Moryn will not fight his brother. He would like friends. Not an army. Friends, lords and knights who will answer his summons first when he sends one and a clerk's last."

"Give her here," Alaric said, and held out his fist.

It took Orryn a moment to understand he was the one being spoken to. He brought his own fist beside the lord's and Tansy stepped across, bells ringing, with a grip on the leather that Orryn felt in his own wrist as it left. Alaric slipped the hood. The falcon turned her head once, left and right, and looked at the whole bright field as if she had been shown a price. He lifted his arm, and she was gone.

She climbed in wide slow turns, until Orryn had to put a hand over his eyes. The spaniels went into the grass and stopped.

"It is the rolls that matter, my lord," said Ser Androw, from his mother's far side. "What a clerk writes becomes what is owed. A rent that nobody can show paper for is no longer a rent. A service agreed in a hall over wine is no service at all. The lords who go to Ser Moryn are mostly the ones who have been asked for paper they do not have."

Alaric did not look down from the falcon. "That sounds like a threat."

"It is a description. At Ashford the court has begun asking for the oldest grants before it hears a man's complaint. It is the same everywhere. The only difference is whose clerk it is."

Orryn thought, without wishing to, of the chest in the undercroft with its three locks, and of the award nobody had read in ten years, and of the words as of old. He glanced at Alaric. Alaric was watching the falcon. She hung very small and high above the field, with the sun behind her, and it seemed to Orryn that she had been there a long time.

Then the spaniels flushed the covey, and the partridge went up in a scatter of brown, and Tansy came down out of the sky with her wings folded. The sound she made as she struck was the sound of a wet cloth dropped on stone.

"Piers," said Ser Wyman, quietly, and Piers was already riding with the lure.

Alaric was grinning like a boy. He seemed to have forgotten everything that had been said to him in the last quarter of an hour, and Orryn thought it was likely that he had not.

"I will say what I came to say," said Lady Ashford, when the bird was taken up and the kill was in Piers's bag. "Ser Moryn has told me twice that he would take a Meadows boy into his household as a squire when the boy is of an age. He keeps none who are cousins to the Redwynes. Your Matthos is thirteen."

"I know how old my son is."

"Then you know it is nearly time." She looked out across the field, where Matthos had gone to pick his glove out of the stubble and was standing over it with his back to them. Her gaze stayed on the boy. "He is not a patient child."

"He's thirteen," said Alaric.

"I did not say it against him. And for Lady Odessa's son, when he has his spurs, a knight's place in Ser Moryn's household. I told Odessa so yesterday. She did not answer. She knows what it would mean."

"Moryn Tyrell has never set eyes on my son."

"He is your lady's stepmother's brother," said Lady Ashford. "In Highgarden that is a nearer kinship than most."

Alaric made a short sound that was not quite a laugh. For some time he rode with the reins slack in one hand and Tansy, hooded again, on the other, and Orryn rode behind him and tried not to look as if he were listening.

"I hold from Lord Leo, my lady, as your husband does. I swore to him. I don't remember swearing to his clerks, or to his sons' friends. I don't like clerks, I like Redwyne gold less, and I like least of all being counted for the profit of whichever Tyrell is doing the counting."

"No one has asked you to forswear anything. Lord Leo is sixty-six." She let that lie a moment between the horses. "Your oath will be to Garth or to Moryn before many years are out, my lord. I have watched Highgarden for thirty years from a window in Ashford, and I came into the Reach from Nightsong as a girl, so I do not say it for the pleasure of the saying. The only question is which of them you will have been seen to prefer when it happens."

"Ser Moryn asks little and asks often," said Ser Androw. "It is his way. Nobody is asking you for more than a boy's service this year."

Lady Ashford turned her head and looked at her son for the length of a breath. Androw did not finish what he had been about to say.

Orryn had expected that politics would be harder to follow. He had followed perhaps two parts in three. The Redwynes had ships and gold, and Ser Garth's clerks counted, and Ser Moryn wanted friends among the lords the counting frightened, and the price of being a friend was a boy. It did not sound like a quarrel. What he could not follow was why she had said it so plainly to a lord she had known two days, with a squire riding at his elbow. Perhaps she had judged that a squire did not count. Perhaps she had meant him to hear it, though he could not think what use that would be to her.

"Say nothing of that at the squires' board," Alaric said, without turning his head.

"No, my lord."

"I don't mean that you would."

That was all, and then he put his heels to his horse and rode ahead to Ser Wyman with his falcon on his fist.

*

Below the mill the brook had shrunk to a string of pools joined by a trickle you could cover with a hand. A line of willows had kept their leaves along the bank by some accident of the roots, and the grooms spread cloths in their shade and handed round the water skins. Lady Jeyne had had mint put in the water. Wylla tasted it and said nothing at all, which Orryn thought must have cost her.

Alyn had taken the horses downstream with Piers. From the willows Orryn could see them, knee-deep at the edge of the farthest pool, leaning on their saddles and shouting at each other in the way that is not quite quarrelling. Alyn had pulled off his boots. A moment later he cupped a handful of water and poured it down the back of Piers's collar, and Piers said something, and Alyn laughed with his whole face.

"Take her," said Alaric, and gave Orryn the falcon. "Stay by me."

He walked along the bank to where Lady Odessa sat on a camp stool a groom had carried down, with a cup in her hand and Wylla beyond her complaining softly of the flies. Orryn followed a pace behind, the bird on his fist, as a hawk-bearer does. It was not a thing anyone would call listening. No one had asked him to go away.

"Lady Ashford tells me she has spoken to you of Alyn," said Alaric.

"Lady Ashford has spoken to me of a great many things." Lady Odessa did not look up from her cup. "She says Ser Moryn would take my son as a knight when he has his spurs. She says it very warmly. I think she says everything warmly, once she has decided."

"He has done well with me this year."

"He is eighteen, Lord Alaric. He has been a squire long enough to do well somewhere."

"Aye." Alaric let himself down onto one knee on the dry bank, as though it had been a thing he meant to do all along. "I'll lose him. I'd rather lose him to a place I can ride to in a day than to Highgarden. I have a daughter who is twelve."

For a moment Lady Odessa said nothing. A fly settled on the rim of her cup and she moved it with a finger.

"Lady Elyce," she said.

"In two years she'll be fourteen. I'd sooner she married where I could see it than where I had to send a letter."

"Lady Elyce is a sweet child. I have heard it said." She turned the cup in her hand. "She is twelve, and my son is eighteen, and a younger son of Longtable has his name and a place at some man's table and a little more besides. I do not say it unkindly. Lord Merryweather would not write before he had seen what Meadows meant to put beside her, and it has been a dry year."

"It is dry at Longtable too, I hear."

"Longtable has the river." She said it pleasantly, as one remarks upon the weather. "I will write to my lord husband. It is a kind thought, Lord Alaric, and I would not wish to seem unkind about it."

"I wouldn't have said it if I thought you would be."

"You have said it to me first," said Lady Odessa. "I take that kindly."

It was over in less time than it had taken Tansy to strike. Orryn had expected a longer answer, or a better one. He stood with his arm beginning to shake under the falcon and put his other hand beneath the wrist to steady her. Down at the pool, Alyn was kneeling on a flat stone with his sleeves rolled, letting a horse drink and saying something into its ear, and he had no more idea what had been said by the willows than the horse had.

*

They rode back in the last of the light. The dust was red, and the horses were tired, and for most of the way there was only the sound of hooves and the small chiming of the bells on Tansy's legs. Alaric rode ahead with Ser Androw and talked about sluices. Orryn kept Rook a length behind him and did not look at Alyn, who was riding with Leonette and Wylla and being told that his hair, which had been cut that week, ought to be cut again.

In the yard the grooms ran out with torches and Lady Jeyne's palfrey was brought to the block. She came down without a hand, and handed her reins to a boy, and said, "A word, my lord, before the others come in."

Alaric swung down from his horse. Orryn, who had dismounted a moment behind him, found that he had a horse in each hand and nowhere to be that he could name. The two of them went three or four paces along the mews wall, which was not far enough, and stopped.

"Odessa told me," said Lady Jeyne. She had not raised her voice and did not now. "She told me when she gave me back her cup. She said it was kind of you to think of Elyce, and that she did so hope I had been consulted. She said it very kindly."

"I said Elyce was twelve. I said I'd sooner she married near."

"Under a willow, with half the household at the water and Lady Ashford thirty paces off. It will be in Highgarden before it is in a letter to Longtable."

"It'll be in Highgarden whatever I do. They have clerks for it."

"Two years ago," said Lady Jeyne, "I wrote to Odessa for Wylla, for our Matthos. I wrote twice. I waited a moon for an answer, and when it came it said that Lord Merryweather had other thoughts. Meaning Ashford. Meaning a knight of twenty-nine for a girl of fifteen, rather than ours." She stopped. Orryn, with a horse's breath on each shoulder, thought it was the first time he had heard her stop in the middle of a sentence. "And now you offer her our daughter for the second son she doesn't want, in front of the woman she preferred."

"It was not an offer."

"Alyn Merryweather drinks. He chases the girls in my kitchen, and you know it, because you have laughed about it."

"He's eighteen."

"He is the second son of a house that would not have Matthos. Elyce is not a thing to be put on the table to buy back a boy who is useful to you."

"That isn't why." Alaric's voice had gone lower, and for once it was not calm. "If Moryn Tyrell has Alyn in Highgarden at nineteen, then in five years every Merryweather and every Ashford between here and the river belongs to Moryn, and Longtable is two days from my gate."

"Then you should have told me that this morning."

"I thought of it on the way down to the pools."

Lady Jeyne said nothing. She stood very straight in the torchlight with her dark hair coming out of its net, and Orryn saw her look at her husband in the way he had seen her look at a tablet of figures. Then her eyes went past Alaric, and found him.

"Master Grandison. That horse has stood a long time. Perhaps you would walk him."

"Yes, my lady."

He walked both horses toward the stable, and neither of them said anything more that he could hear. She had not asked him to keep what he had heard to himself. He understood that she had not needed to, and that it had gone into the ledger already, under his name.

Matthos, he thought. She had asked for Wylla for Matthos, and been put off for a moon, and been refused. He had not known. Nobody had said. He saw Wylla at the cart with the cup in her hand, asking after lemon, and he saw her sitting in Leonette's little bed explaining about two sheets, and he tried to see what anyone had wanted of her, Lady Jeyne or Lady Odessa or Ashford, and could not do it. He could see a round plain face and a voice that went on about sleeves. He remembered the gold horn on white in Maester Perrin's roll of arms, and he had heard somewhere that a Merryweather woman had once been thought of as a bride for a king. At Grandview they told it as a Grandison story, how Lord Lorent had sat on the council that ruled for the boy king Aegon, and it had always been Lorent in the telling and never the Merryweather who sat beside him. He supposed that it was the horn they wanted. He could not see that anyone could want the girl.

At the stable door he looked back across the yard. The torches had been set in the brackets and the grooms were leading the horses in, and Alyn had come up from the gate with his boots in one hand and his hair still wet, laughing at something Leonette had said. He saw Orryn and lifted the boots in greeting.

"Grandview! Did Tansy kill anything for his lordship?"

"One," said Orryn. "A partridge."

"Only one? It's a poor falcon, then." He was gone toward the hall, barefoot over the stones.

In the nursery window above the hall steps a small face looked out, framed by a lamp, with a puppy tucked in the crook of its arm. Lady Elyce Meadows was watching the yard, and she was watching Alyn. Orryn could not tell, at that distance, whether she had been watching long, or what she knew, or whether she knew anything at all.

He took the horses into the dark of the stable, and found a nail for the falcon's glove.
