Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

The trouble began with the horses.

There were not enough stalls for them, and scarcely enough hay for the horses already at Grassfield. Ser Wyman Latham had told Lady Jeyne as much, and Lady Jeyne had told him that her daughter's wedding was not to be postponed because the horses were hungry.

"My lady," Ser Wyman had said, "they will be hungry whether we postpone it or not."

"Then you may tell them so."

This was repeated throughout the stable yard, and by the third repetition Lady Jeyne was said to have ordered Ser Wyman to feed the horses upon the wedding guests. Orryn had been present for the actual exchange and thought the invention an improvement.

He was not foolish enough to say so.

It was a fortnight after his return from Highgarden, and Grassfield had begun to resemble a place preparing for a siege. Carts arrived with flour and candles, a cooper had been summoned from Grassy Vale to mend three leaking barrels, and Lyman Verrall spent his days walking from the kitchens to the storehouses with a tablet in his hand and the expression of a man calculating how much money could be lost before anyone began calling it a catastrophe.

The Footlys were expected with eighteen horses, perhaps twenty. Lord Armond Caswell would bring at least a dozen, Lady Jeyne's father being incapable of travelling anywhere without the greater part of his household. The Merryweathers had written that they would attend. Ser Androw Ashford and his new wife were coming as well.

The numbers changed every day.

Orryn was given the stables to help prepare. He preferred it to being made to carry benches, which had occupied much of the preceding week. He preferred almost anything to the practice yard, where Ser Everard still corrected his feet and where Piers Fallow had begun to win more often than he lost.

Orryn had not won once against him since returning.

He thought of this while he helped Jory Marsh carry a bundle of dried reeds into the empty stalls. The reeds had been cut beside the lower mill, where the water had retreated so far that the mud beneath them had cracked. They made poor bedding, but poor bedding was better than none.

"Mind the pitchfork," Jory told him.

"I can see it."

"You put your foot on it yesterday."

"That was Wat."

"No, it wasn't."

Orryn said nothing.

At least Jory did not ask him to demonstrate the proper way to stand with a sword.

From the gate came the sound of hooves. A rider had entered at a brisk pace, his horse dark with sweat beneath the saddle. He wore a travelling cloak over green and carried a small leather wallet.

Orryn recognised neither horse nor rider.

Ser Wyman came out of the long stable.

"Highgarden?"

"Aye."

"Then you'll have no hay until the horse is cool."

"I'll have water."

"You may."

The rider dismounted. Orryn took the reins while he loosened his saddle, and saw a small green seal upon the wallet.

"Where is Lord Meadows?" the man asked.

"In the keep, ser."

"I'm no ser. Take me to him."

Orryn led him across the yard.

Lady Jeyne had commandeered the lower solar for the wedding arrangements. Every flat surface was covered with something: folded linen, two different samples of blue silk, a bundle of candles, a bowl of pins and a little wooden board upon which names had been written in careful columns.

Alaric stood beside the window, listening to his wife explain why a knight of House Footly could not be seated below a knight of House Bushy merely because the latter had been born sooner.

"It is not the age of the man," she was saying. "It is the honour of the house."

"He will be older if we wait another day."

"He will be no more honourable."

The messenger was announced.

Alaric looked relieved.

"From Highgarden?"

"Yes, my lord."

"Good. Something that has nothing to do with chairs."

Lady Jeyne held out her hand for the letter.

The messenger hesitated.

"It is addressed to Lord Meadows, my lady."

"And I am his wife."

"Give it here," said Alaric.

He broke the seal.

Orryn stood beside the door, holding the messenger's gloves. Lady Jeyne had not dismissed him, and he had learned that standing quietly was sometimes the surest way to avoid being given another task.

Alaric read the letter once.

Then he read the last part again.

"Who brought this to the clerk?" he asked.

"Lord Osmund's office, my lord."

"Lord Osmund himself?"

"I cannot say."

Alaric folded the letter.

"And Lord Rowan?"

The messenger looked at Lady Jeyne.

"Lord Rowan has gone, my lord."

"Gone where?"

"Goldengrove, they say."

"When?"

"Three days before I left."

"Three days." Alaric frowned. "That was a short visit."

"It was not meant to be."

Lady Jeyne put down the seating list.

"What has happened?"

The messenger shifted his weight.

"I was not in the hall, my lady."

"Then tell us what was said outside it."

He looked grateful for the permission.

"Lord Rowan had words with Ser Garth. Over the judgment at Cress Hill. Lord Osmund was there, and Lord Leo."

"What words?" Alaric asked.

"Hard ones, my lord. Lord Rowan demanded to know by whose leave the Justiciar had heard a quarrel involving his people. Ser Garth said the judgment was lawful and Lord Osmund had acted within his warrant."

"Did he?"

"I wasn't there, my lord."

"Did he say it was lawful, I mean?"

"That is what the men in the hall say."

Alaric rubbed his mouth.

"And my lord of Highgarden?"

"He asked them to leave the matter until supper. There was to be a private hearing afterward."

"And?"

"Lord Rowan did not stay."

The room was quiet.

Orryn remembered Cress Hill. He remembered the sweating knight, Ser Tom Costen, standing before the Justiciar; the widow's bailiff with his arm bleeding; Septon Emmon kneeling on the flags to bind the wound. Lord Osmund's judgment had concerned a spring that belonged to Lady Ormesby, whose lands lay under Rowan's authority. A trench had been dug by night to take water from her farm for a village of two hundred souls.

Leygood had ordered the trench filled and Ser Tom fined.

Orryn had thought the judgment fair.

He had also heard Alaric say that a lord could not surrender his own court without surrendering something of himself.

Now Lord Rowan had said much the same thing, or so the messenger claimed, and Highgarden had found no satisfactory answer.

"Was Rowan dismissed?" Lady Jeyne asked.

"No, my lady."

"Did he ask leave?"

"He gave Lord Leo his respects before departing. That's what I heard."

"Then he did not ask leave," Alaric said.

The messenger looked at him.

"No, my lord."

Alaric laid the letter on the table.

"Who went with him?"

"His own men. Lord Florent's people were seen saddling the same afternoon. And I heard that Lord Gormon Peake departed before dawn, but whether for Starpike or elsewhere I cannot say."

Lady Jeyne regarded him for a moment.

"And Lord Jon Hightower?"

Orryn had not expected that name.

The messenger's face changed a little.

"Lord Jon was seen speaking with Rowan in the eastern gallery, my lady."

"When?"

"Before Lord Rowan departed."

"Who saw them?"

"Half the household, by the telling."

"Half the household would make a poor witness," Lady Jeyne said.

The man smiled uncertainly.

"Of what they said, I know nothing."

"Then that is what you must remember."

Alaric gave the letter to his wife.

"Lord Osmund asks whether I will provide grain and two men to guard a cart through the vale. I said I would."

"And will you?"

"Of course."

"Despite all this?"

"The grain is no less grain because Rowan is angry."

Lady Jeyne read the letter.

"And if Lord Rowan asks whose seal is upon it?"

"Mine, if it comes through Grassfield."

She looked at her husband.

"You're going to find yourself standing in a very small place, Alaric."

"It has room for a cart."

He smiled.

She did not.

*

By the following morning everyone at Grassfield had heard that Lord Rowan had stormed out of Highgarden.

By the next evening the tale had become a quarrel in which Ser Garth threatened to dispossess Goldengrove, Lord Rowan called the heir to Highgarden a tyrant, and Lord Leo wept before his assembled court.

Orryn believed none of the last three particulars.

Perkin believed all of them, or at least told them with great pleasure.

"Lord Rowan drew his sword," he said over breakfast.

"He did not," said Osric.

"How would you know?"

"Because he would have been arrested."

"Not if he drew it very quickly."

"That makes no sense."

"It would make sense to Lord Rowan."

Baldric reached for the bread.

"My father says Lord Rowan has more men under him than half the lords in the Reach."

"Then why did he need a sword?" Piers asked.

"To point with," said Perkin.

Orryn smiled.

He had been allowed to stand behind Alaric's chair during the messenger's account, and so knew rather more than the others. He took a certain satisfaction in this.

He also knew that there had been no sword.

He did not correct Perkin.

At the high table Leonette was arguing with her mother about a necklace. Orryn could hear the words silver and Tumbleton from where he sat.

At last Lady Jeyne said, in a voice that reached half the hall, "Your father has paid for a wedding, not a treasury."

Leonette's face grew red.

She turned her head away, and caught Orryn looking.

He dropped his eyes.

She had turned fifteen since his return, and had become, if anything, more conscious of the importance of that event than she had been of her fourteenth name day. She was to marry Ser Garlan Footly, eleven years older than herself, a kindly knight whose family held Tumbleton in the northeastern Reach.

In a few days she would be Lady Leonette Footly.

Orryn wondered whether this would make her less tiresome.

He doubted it.

The Footlys arrived two days before the wedding, and with them came the first cart of fresh flowers that Orryn had seen since the dry began.

They had been brought from gardens near Tumbleton, wrapped in damp cloth and packed in shallow wooden trays. Most were white or pale yellow, with a few blue blossoms whose names Orryn did not know. The leaves had begun to curl in the heat.

Lady Jeyne inspected them personally.

"They will do," she said.

The Footly steward, who had spent three days bringing them, bowed as though she had declared him a great man.

Ser Garlan Footly came behind his father, Lord Bennard, riding a handsome chestnut gelding with a white blaze. He wore a pale blue cloak fastened with silver. His face was broad and pleasant, his brown hair neatly combed, and he smiled so readily at everyone that Orryn wondered how he managed to remember whom he had greeted.

When a servant dropped a bundle of blankets in the yard, Garlan dismounted and helped him gather them.

Lord Bennard looked away, apparently deciding that he had not seen it.

Orryn thought this was a peculiar sort of kindness in a man who would soon marry Leonette.

She would have told the servant to pick them up himself.

*

The Caswells came the following afternoon.

Lord Armond rode at the head of his party beneath the arms of Bitterbridge, accompanied by Lady Brenna Tyrell, his second wife, and several knights. Behind them came two covered wagons and a handful of household servants. Orryn counted eleven horses before losing his place.

Joffrey Caswell rode near the rear.

Orryn saw him before Joffrey saw Orryn, which gave him a moment to compose his face.

It was not enough.

"Grandison!" Joffrey called, smiling.

Orryn raised a hand.

Joffrey was fifteen, a year older than Orryn, and already had the settled voice of a man. He wore a dark blue riding coat and carried a long leather case strapped beside his saddle.

His lute, Orryn supposed.

At Bitterbridge they had sat together in a narrow gallery overlooking the river, and Joffrey had played for him. Orryn remembered the music imperfectly. He remembered the warmth of Joffrey's shoulder against his own much more clearly.

"Did you have a good journey?" Orryn asked.

"Long and hot. My father complained of the roads, my stepmother complained of the dust, and I complained of both until they told me to be quiet."

"Then you must have had a pleasant time."

"For everyone else."

Joffrey dismounted and gave his reins to a groom.

"You came home safely."

"Yes."

"I wondered whether you would."

"Why shouldn't I?"

"You were travelling with my father."

Orryn laughed.

Joffrey looked pleased.

Then Lord Armond called for him, and Joffrey went.

Orryn watched him cross the yard.

At the mounting block, Lord Armond embraced Alaric with the familiarity of a man who had known him since he was a boy.

The embrace lasted only a moment.

Then Armond said something Orryn could not hear, and Alaric's expression changed.

Lady Brenna came down from her horse without waiting for assistance. She was younger than her husband and wore her Tyrell ancestry with the confidence of someone who had never needed to explain its value.

She kissed Lady Jeyne upon both cheeks.

"My dear girl. You look well."

"I am well."

"And growing."

"I am with child, not with a tumour."

Brenna laughed.

Jeyne did not.

Orryn took Lady Brenna's reins and led her horse away.

By the time he returned, the men had gone inside.

*

The Merryweathers and Ashfords arrived together.

Lord Merryweather was a genial, round-shouldered man who laughed at almost everything said to him and sometimes before it was said. His wife, Lady Odessa, was much more exacting. She inspected the arrangements for their accommodation with the courtesy of a woman who expected every request to be obeyed.

With them came Lady Wylla Merryweather and Ser Androw Ashford.

Orryn knew them both.

Wylla had visited Grassfield with her family in the spring, a lively girl with blue eyes and a fondness for fine clothes. Ser Androw was a handsome, capable knight approaching thirty, heir to Ashford on the Cockleswent, whose skill with a sword Orryn had admired during his visit.

They had been betrothed then.

Now they were married.

Orryn noticed the ring upon Wylla's finger when she held out her hand to Lady Jeyne.

"Lady Ashford," Jeyne said, smiling.

Wylla looked delighted.

"It sounds very grand, doesn't it?"

"It is grand enough."

"Androw says I ought not grow too fond of it."

"Because I intend to live a long while," her husband said. "My father is in good health, and I see no need to hurry him toward the grave."

"Nor I," Wylla answered. "I should much rather have a husband than a lord."

Ser Androw laughed and kissed her hand.

Orryn thought they seemed pleased with one another.

He was surprised to learn that neither Alaric nor Lady Jeyne had attended their wedding. It had taken place at Ashford a few weeks earlier, with a gathering of the two families and their closer neighbours.

"We had invitations enough," Lord Merryweather said. "And half the roads in the Reach are dust. I told Odessa we ought to wait until the autumn, but she said a girl should not be kept waiting once the groom had made up his mind."

Lady Odessa smiled.

"And he had made up his mind."

"Had he?" Lord Merryweather asked, looking about as though the answer might be found among the servants.

They laughed.

There was nothing remarkable in it, Orryn supposed. Marriages were arranged and celebrated throughout the Reach, and no lord could attend every wedding to which he was invited.

Yet Wylla was Alyn's sister.

Orryn had thought that if anyone in the family married, he would hear of it.

He wondered whether Alyn had been there.

He did not ask.

*

Lord Rowan arrived late.

It was already evening when the watchman called down from the gate tower that a party bearing the golden tree of Goldengrove was approaching along the north road.

Orryn was in the stable yard with Ser Wyman.

He had been helping to separate the remaining hay into measured bundles, and the arrival of another lord with another dozen horses was the last thing either of them desired.

Ser Wyman looked at the gate.

"Seven save us."

"Is he expected?"

"Every lord in the Reach is expected, according to Lady Jeyne. Only some have had the decency to send word."

They went out to meet the party.

At its head rode Lord Rycherd Rowan, Lord of Goldengrove and hereditary Marshal of the Northmarch. He was fifty-three, broad in the chest and greying at the temples, with a heavy face that had been weathered by long years outdoors. His cloak bore the golden tree of his house.

Beside him rode Lady Clarice Rowan, born Hightower, a tall woman whose dark gown and silver ornaments made her seem older and more severe than Orryn remembered from Highgarden.

A younger knight accompanied them, handsome and fair-haired, with the Rowan colours upon his surcoat. Orryn supposed him to be Ser Triston, Lord Rowan's heir.

A girl rode beside Lady Clarice. She had a long neck, a composed face and hair arranged beneath a pale veil.

Lady Roslin, perhaps.

Then Orryn saw the knight riding behind them.

He had a green cloak, dusty at the hem, and a sword hanging from a belt that Orryn recognised before he recognised the man.

Alyn Merryweather.

For a moment Orryn did not move.

Alyn was wearing the same careless smile he had worn at Grassfield. His chestnut hair had grown longer, and his riding coat was finer than the one he had taken away when Alaric knighted him.

He looked very much as he had always looked.

Except that there was a sword at his hip, and he rode among Lord Rowan's household knights.

Alyn saw him.

His smile widened.

"Grandview!"

Orryn felt his face grow warm.

"Ser Alyn."

"Listen to you. Have they made you proper since I left?"

"No, ser."

"Then there's hope for you yet."

Alyn swung down from his horse and handed Orryn the reins.

It was an entirely ordinary thing to do.

Orryn took them, and found that he could not think of a single word to say.

Alyn looked about the yard.

"Gods, it looks smaller."

"It hasn't changed."

"No? Then I suppose I have."

Orryn looked at him.

Alyn laughed.

"Don't look so solemn. I haven't grown taller."

A groom came forward to take the horse.

Alyn followed Orryn toward the stable, walking with the ease of someone who knew every stone beneath his feet.

"How is Everard?"

"Well."

"Still drinks?"

"After noon."

"Good. Some things ought not change."

"And Matthos?"

"At Highgarden."

"I know that. Moryn took him. He'll have a fine time telling everyone whose son he is."

Orryn smiled.

"He does."

Alyn glanced at him.

"And you? Still losing your sword?"

"Sometimes."

"Only sometimes? You've improved."

Orryn's smile vanished.

Alyn did not seem to notice.

"Lord Rowan's men train hard," he said. "I've been beaten black and blue since I entered his service."

"You're in his household?"

"Aye. Lady Melessa spoke for me."

"Ser Moryn's wife?"

"The very one. Her nephew Androw had told her I wanted a place. Lord Rowan needed knights who could ride and were not already owed three farms and a yearly purse."

"And he chose you?"

"Among others."

Alyn said it lightly, but Orryn thought he heard satisfaction beneath the words.

"Is it a good place?"

"Better than many. He keeps a serious household. The men know their work. And there are more roads to ride than I expected."

Orryn considered that.

"Will you stay?"

"If I'm useful."

He smiled again.

"That's what one does with a knight, Grandview. One finds him useful things to do."

From the gate came a call for the new arrivals to enter.

Alyn turned toward it.

"I'll be wanted."

"Yes, ser."

He took a step, then looked back.

"It's good to see you, boy."

Orryn could not answer quickly enough.

Alyn had already gone.

For a while Orryn stood beside the stable door, holding an empty rein in his hand.

He had missed Alyn for weeks.

Now Alyn was here, and Orryn was more miserable than he had been before.

*

Lord Rowan's arrival caused a greater commotion than any of the earlier guests.

Lady Jeyne had not expected him to attend in person. The invitation had been sent as a courtesy, along with others to houses too great or distant for anyone to suppose that their lords would come.

But Rowan had come.

He was too powerful to be lodged among the lesser guests, and his presence required several changes to the seating arrangements.

Lyman Verrall spent half the evening moving names from one board to another.

Lady Jeyne watched him.

"Lord Rowan must sit above Lord Merryweather."

"Of course, my lady."

"And Lady Clarice beside Lady Brenna."

"Yes."

"Not below Lady Odessa."

"No."

"And Ser Triston?"

"He is the heir to Goldengrove."

"I know who he is, Verrall."

"Then perhaps he ought to sit beside Ser Androw."

"Ser Androw is the heir to Ashford."

"Yes, my lady."

"Good."

Verrall looked relieved.

"That settles it, then."

"It settles nothing. Where is Lord Bennard?"

"At the head of the Footly party."

"It is his son's wedding."

"Yes."

"And my daughter's."

"Yes."

"Then the Footlys must be placed accordingly."

Verrall took up his tablet again.

Orryn was sent to fetch two more chairs.

When he returned, Lord Alaric was standing in the doorway.

"Has the whole Reach come to marry my daughter?"

"Not yet," Jeyne said. "There are still roads."

Alaric smiled.

She did not.

Then he said, "Lord Rowan wishes to speak with me before supper."

Jeyne stopped moving the wooden names.

"Does he?"

"He does."

"And Armond?"

"Armond is already with him."

There was a brief silence.

Then Lady Jeyne said, "You had better go."

*

Orryn was summoned to the smaller solar with wine.

The chamber held Lord Alaric, Lord Armond Caswell, Lord Rowan and Ser Bertram Corliss. Lady Brenna sat near the window, her hands folded in her lap.

There was a map upon the table.

Orryn recognised the Mander and the roads eastward toward Bitterbridge. There were little marks beside certain crossings, some in red ink and some in black.

He did not know who had made them.

Lord Rowan was speaking when Orryn entered.

"...and I said I had not come to dispute whether the widow owned the water. I came to dispute whether Lord Osmund had the right to hear the case."

"An old distinction," said Alaric.

"It was old before Highgarden was built."

Lord Armond gave a short laugh.

"And Ser Garth would make it young again by forgetting it."

Brenna turned her head.

"My husband."

"What?"

"You may say what you like of my brother-in-law. I am only reminding you that he is my brother-in-law."

"Then you may remind him that I am his father's son-in-law."

"I have done so before."

Rowan smiled faintly.

Orryn poured wine.

He had seen Lord Rowan at Highgarden, though only from a distance. There he had seemed another great man among many. At Grassfield he filled the room.

He spoke in a measured voice, without shouting or hurrying, and the others listened.

"Lord Osmund is an able judge," Rowan said. "I do not deny it."

"I thought his judgment fair," Alaric answered.

"So did I, when the facts were laid before me. That is not the matter."

"No."

"Then you understand me."

"I understand why you're angry."

"Ser Garth did not."

"He understood you perfectly," Armond said. "He merely would not yield."

Rowan looked at him.

"That is possible."

Alaric turned his cup.

"What did Lord Leo offer?"

"A private hearing," Rowan said. "A written clarification. A supper at which we were all to be friends again."

"And you refused."

"I refused to pretend the matter had been settled."

"Did you refuse the supper too?"

"I had been expected at Goldengrove."

Armond laughed.

Orryn thought Rowan might have smiled, but could not be certain.

Alaric leaned against the table.

"Then what is it you want?"

Rowan regarded him.

"That is a fair question."

He touched the map with one finger.

"I want the lords of the Reach to know which causes belong to their courts, which belong to Highgarden, and by what right one may take a cause from the other. I want a summons to mean a summons and a judgment to mean a judgment. I want the ancient rights of my house honoured, not merely remembered when it pleases Ser Garth."

"And if a lord refuses justice to his own people?" Alaric asked.

"Then there are appeals."

"To whom?"

"To his overlord, according to right."

"Which may bring us back to Lord Osmund."

"Then let us set down the right."

It was the first time Orryn had heard Rowan speak with any warmth.

The lord of Goldengrove drew back from the table.

"I will not have a man sent into my lands with Highgarden's seal and told afterward that the warrant was sufficient because the judgment happened to be sound."

Armond nodded.

"I told Ser Garth much the same."

"At Highgarden?" Alaric asked.

"Before Lord Leo himself."

"And?"

"He heard me. I cannot say he liked what he heard."

Brenna looked out the window.

Armond continued.

"I went there to speak of Cress Hill, and of the rights of Bitterbridge. Ser Garth told me that where Highgarden had lawful authority, no lord's displeasure could set it aside. I told him that I had been Lord Caswell long enough to know which court belonged to my father and which to the Tyrells."

"And Lord Leo?" Alaric asked.

"He asked me to remain."

"Did you?"

"I did. Unlike some."

Rowan accepted the remark without irritation.

"Lord Leo offered me no satisfaction," Armond said. "He offered to listen further. I told him that Lord Rowan had raised a question that ought to be answered before any more judgments were made under the same warrant."

"Before Lord Leo?" Alaric asked.

"Before him."

"And Ser Moryn?"

"Was present for part of it. He spoke sensibly."

"How fortunate."

Armond looked at him.

"Alaric, I have been to Highgarden. I have heard Garth tell me what my own father meant by his grants. I have watched Leo try to turn an argument into supper. I have spoken to Moryn, and he at least knows the difference between a lord and a steward."

Alaric was quiet.

Orryn remembered him saying much the same thing to Lord Osmund at Highgarden.

A lord who surrendered his court was only a steward.

But Alaric had also praised the Justiciar.

The two judgments sat uncomfortably together in Orryn's mind.

Rowan turned toward him.

"You were there, boy."

Orryn nearly dropped the jug.

"At Cress Hill, my lord?"

"Aye. You stood behind Lord Meadows."

"I did."

"Did you think Lord Osmund ruled fairly?"

Orryn felt the eyes of the room upon him.

"I thought the water belonged to the widow, my lord."

"And the village?"

"The village needed water."

"That was not my question."

"No, my lord."

Rowan studied him.

"Did you think the judgment fair?"

"Yes, my lord."

Alaric said nothing.

Rowan nodded once.

"So did I."

He turned back to Alaric.

"And still it was my right to hear the cause."

Orryn was not certain whether he had been corrected or agreed with.

The conversation moved on.

There were questions about grain, roads and the condition of the wells in Rowan's northern lands. Armond complained of the cost of feeding men who came to Bitterbridge with demands and no money. Ser Bertram spoke of the burden upon lesser landed knights.

Orryn refilled the cups.

He found himself wishing someone would speak of the wedding.

No one did.

*

On the morning of Leonette's marriage, a cloud appeared above the eastern hills.

It was the first large cloud Orryn had seen for days.

Perkin announced that it would rain.

Osric said the weather belonged to the Seven.

Baldric said that if the cloud brought rain before the ceremony, Lady Jeyne would have every boy at Grassfield beaten for permitting it.

The cloud passed without shedding a drop.

By noon the courtyard had filled with guests.

There were knights from nearby holdings, their wives and daughters, lesser relatives of Caswell and Merryweather, and retainers whose masters had sent them in place of appearing themselves. The Footly party had brought enough servants to make the western guest chambers seem full before the eastern guests were accommodated.

A pavilion stood below the walls, where Lord Rowan's younger knights slept. Several others had taken rooms in the town.

Orryn spent the morning carrying messages, fetching water and helping Ser Wyman keep order among the horses.

He saw Alyn twice.

Once Alyn was crossing the yard beside Ser Triston Rowan, and he did not seem to see Orryn.

The second time he stopped to speak with Ser Wyman, laughing over some difficulty with a chestnut mare.

Orryn stood nearby with a bucket.

He waited.

Alyn did not call him.

Orryn felt foolish for having expected it.

At last Lady Jeyne sent for him.

He found her in the upper hall, where Leonette stood upon a low stool while two women arranged the folds of her wedding gown.

The gown was pale blue, worked with silver thread at the sleeves and collar. A little chain of pearls lay against her throat. Her chestnut hair had been brushed until it shone and gathered beneath a veil so light that Orryn could see the shape of her face through it.

She looked beautiful.

He resented noticing.

"Master Grandison," Lady Jeyne said. "Take this to the sept."

She handed him a folded length of cloth.

It was Leonette's maiden cloak, bearing the colours and device of House Meadows.

"Give it to Septa Lenora. She will know where it belongs."

"Yes, my lady."

Leonette turned upon the stool.

"Has Lord Rowan arrived at the sept?"

"I don't know, my lady."

"Of course you don't."

Orryn looked at her.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You have a face for something."

"I was only wondering whether you wished me to find him."

"Why should I wish that?"

"You asked after him."

"I asked whether he had arrived. Everyone is asking after him."

Lady Jeyne tightened a pin.

"Leonette."

"What?"

"Stand still."

Leonette lifted her chin.

"I am standing still."

"You are turning."

"Then perhaps my gown ought to turn with me."

One of the women looked down to conceal a smile.

Lady Jeyne gave the last pin a firm push.

"There."

Leonette regarded herself in a polished silver mirror.

For a moment she said nothing.

Then she asked, quietly, "Is it too plain?"

"No," said her mother.

"Lady Wylla wore pearls in her hair."

"Lady Wylla is not my daughter."

"Her husband is the heir to Ashford."

"And yours is heir to Tumbleton."

"Yes."

The answer was very small.

Lady Jeyne placed a hand upon her shoulder.

"Ser Garlan is a good man."

"I know."

"He will treat you kindly."

"I know that too."

"Then what troubles you?"

Leonette looked at her reflection.

"Nothing, Mother."

Orryn lowered his eyes.

He thought she was being ridiculous.

She had the finest gown in Grassfield, a handsome husband, a marriage into a respected house and a hall full of people come to admire her.

There was no good reason to be unhappy.

Yet she looked unhappy.

Lady Jeyne noticed that Orryn was still there.

"The cloak, Grandison."

He bowed and went.

*

The sept at Grassfield was too small for all the guests.

The foremost nobles stood within, while lesser knights and household retainers gathered near the open doors. The air smelled of candle wax and flowers, and shafts of afternoon light entered through the high windows.

Orryn found a place beside one of the pillars, close enough to see the ceremony and far enough back that nobody would ask him to move.

Septa Lenora had taken the maiden cloak.

Near the altar stood Ser Garlan Footly in a dark blue cloak, looking both solemn and delighted. His father stood beside him, shifting his weight from one foot to the other.

Lord Rowan occupied a place near the front with Lady Clarice and Ser Triston. Lord Armond stood opposite him. The Merryweathers and Ashfords were nearby.

Orryn looked toward the rear.

Alyn stood among Rowan's household knights.

He was watching the altar.

Orryn looked away.

When the doors opened, Lord Alaric entered with Leonette upon his arm.

She walked slowly, holding her skirts just clear of the floor.

The blue of her gown seemed brighter in the pale light. The pearl chain lay still against her neck, and her face was composed beneath the veil.

Orryn had seen her laughing in the stable, arguing with her sister, teasing servants and demanding to be told how she looked.

He had never seen her so quiet.

Alaric led her forward.

For a moment he seemed reluctant to release her hand.

Then Ser Garlan took it.

The septon spoke of the Seven, of duty, fidelity and the joining of two houses.

Orryn listened only imperfectly.

He remembered his cousin's wedding at Parchments. He remembered the sound of the septon's voice and the heavy stillness of the guests. He remembered thinking then that marriage was a very solemn business for something that everyone spent so much time celebrating.

Now he understood a little better.

Leonette was leaving Grassfield.

The thought was unexpectedly strange.

Alaric removed her maiden cloak.

Garlan placed his own cloak around her shoulders.

The septon joined their hands.

Their vows were spoken clearly.

Leonette's voice did not tremble.

Garlan's did, very slightly.

Orryn noticed, and found himself liking the knight more for it.

When the septon declared them man and wife, Garlan kissed Leonette.

The guests murmured their blessings.

Somewhere outside the sept, a boy cheered too soon and was hushed.

Leonette smiled.

It was a brilliant smile, the one she had worn all her life when she knew she was being watched.

Orryn wondered whether she was happy.

Then he saw her glance toward the rear of the sept.

Toward the Rowan knights.

Toward Alyn.

It lasted no longer than a breath.

Orryn looked down.

He hated her a little for it.

He hated himself rather more for noticing.

*

The wedding feast began before sunset.

There were musicians, roasted fowl, pies, fresh bread saved for the occasion, bowls of vegetables and more wine than Orryn thought the household could reasonably afford. The cooks had contrived a dish of river fish with herbs, though the rivers had yielded little worth eating for weeks.

The hall was crowded.

The high table held the principal guests. Lesser knights occupied benches below the dais, while the squires and pages carried dishes and cups through the narrow spaces between them.

Orryn had been assigned to serve Lord Alaric and the most honoured guests.

It was a position he would ordinarily have coveted.

Tonight he wished he could sit.

He had scarcely eaten since morning.

Garlan and Leonette sat together beneath a hanging cloth bearing the colours of Meadows and Footly. Garlan spoke to her often and smiled each time she answered.

Leonette smiled back.

But her attention wandered.

At the far end of the high table Lord Rowan was speaking quietly with Lord Armond. Ser Triston sat beside his mother, and Lady Clarice occasionally joined the conversation.

Lord Merryweather leaned toward them with great interest.

Orryn caught a few words.

"...not merely Cress Hill."

"...Garth's officers..."

"...Lord Leo means well..."

Then laughter from the other end of the table drowned them out.

A servant brought a new jug of wine.

Orryn took it and moved behind the guests.

At first the conversation concerned the ceremony, the bride's gown and the journey to Tumbleton. Several people praised Lady Jeyne's arrangements. Lord Bennard Footly declared that he had never seen a finer feast, though Orryn knew that the man had been at far grander tables.

Then someone asked Lord Rowan whether he intended to return to Highgarden soon.

The question came from a knight whose name Orryn had not caught.

Rowan set down his cup.

"I have business in the north."

"Will Ser Garth receive you?"

"If he wishes to."

"Lord Leo will wish it."

Rowan considered.

"Then I shall be pleased to hear from him."

The answer was courteous.

It did not sound friendly.

A little farther down the table Lord Armond was explaining the affair to Lord Merryweather.

"Ser Garth would not yield an inch. Not for Rowan, not for me."

"His father is a gentler man," Merryweather said.

"His father is a better host."

"And a better lord?"

Armond glanced toward Rowan.

"That remains to be seen."

Lady Brenna put down her cup.

"Armond."

"What?"

"You're at a wedding."

"Then I shall drink to the bride."

He raised his cup toward Leonette.

"To Lady Footly!"

The nearest guests answered.

Leonette brightened.

She thanked him with a smile and leaned toward Garlan.

For a few minutes the talk returned to weddings.

Orryn began to hope it might remain there.

Then Lady Clarice Rowan said something to Lady Brenna that made her turn sharply.

"Triston?"

"Yes."

"And it is agreed?"

"The letters were sealed last week."

Brenna looked delighted.

"That is splendid news."

Orryn was filling her cup.

He heard the name before he understood its significance.

"Lady Joy Penrose," Lady Clarice said.

Brenna laughed.

"Then you must allow me to congratulate you."

Lord Rowan turned toward them.

"For what?" he asked.

"Your son's marriage."

"Betrothal," Lady Clarice corrected. "The ceremony will be arranged when the families have settled the journey."

"Then I congratulate you on the betrothal."

Ser Triston smiled politely.

Orryn looked from one face to the next.

Lady Joy Penrose.

He knew the name.

She was the daughter of Lord Ronnel Penrose, Master of Coin to King Daeron, and Princess Elaena Targaryen. At Parchments Orryn had heard her spoken of, though he had never known her well enough to claim acquaintance.

His cousin Elenei Grandison had married Ser Addam Penrose, whose mother was Lady Alysanne Hightower.

And Lady Alysanne was Lady Clarice Rowan's sister.

There were so many relationships between great houses that Orryn sometimes thought a man could travel from one end of the Seven Kingdoms to the other without ever escaping a cousin.

He was still considering this when the news spread.

"Triston Rowan to Joy Penrose?"

"Lord Ronnel's daughter?"

"The princess's girl?"

"Then Goldengrove has done very well."

The words passed along the table.

Lord Merryweather seemed delighted.

"A fine marriage. A very fine marriage."

"It is," said Lord Rowan.

"And with the Master of Coin!"

"With his daughter," Lady Clarice said.

"Of course. Naturally."

Lord Armond laughed.

"One might say you have made a friend at court, Rowan."

"One might," Rowan replied.

"And Lord Jon Hightower?"

Lady Clarice turned toward him.

"What of my kinsman?"

"Was he pleased?"

"I believe so."

"Then Oldtown has had a hand in it."

Lady Clarice smiled.

"Oldtown has hands in many places, Lord Armond."

That produced laughter, but Orryn noticed that Lord Rowan did not join it.

A knight farther down the table said that the betrothal had come at a very convenient time, so soon after Rowan's disagreement with Garth.

Another said it had been discussed for months.

A third insisted that Lord Jon Hightower had arranged the whole matter.

Orryn listened, trying to understand which was true.

It was Lady Brenna who supplied the plainest account.

"Lord Ronnel has been approached before," she said. "But the agreement was made after the quarrel at Highgarden. That is what Lady Clarice tells me."

"Indeed," Lady Clarice answered. "We had reason to wish the matter settled."

"Reason," Armond repeated, smiling.

"Marriage is full of reasons."

Lord Rowan looked at his wife.

She returned his look without changing her expression.

Orryn thought of Lady Jeyne's remark about Hightower.

Who saw them?

Half the household, the messenger had said.

Now Lord Rowan's heir was promised to the daughter of a royal minister and a Targaryen princess.

Perhaps Hightower had helped.

Perhaps he had done nothing.

Everyone seemed determined to credit him with something.

A knight of House Bushy, standing behind Orryn, leaned toward his companion.

"Rowan has made himself a greater friend than Tyrell."

"Lord Penrose serves the king."

"Aye. And Rowan now has his daughter."

"That does not make the king his friend."

"It makes the road shorter."

Orryn thought that was a strange way to speak of marriage.

Then he remembered how many people had already spoken of Leonette's.

*

By the time the second course was finished, Leonette had begun to look displeased.

The talk at the high table had returned again and again to Rowan, to Lord Penrose, to Highgarden and to the question of whether Garth had been wise to defend Leygood.

Lord Rowan remained courteous. He did not raise his voice, and he made no speech denouncing anyone.

But men kept turning toward him.

They asked his opinion on the rights of lords, on the harvest, on the condition of the northern roads. They asked whether Ser Moryn agreed with him and whether Lord Leo might summon another hearing.

Orryn wondered whether Rowan had expected so many questions.

Leonette had certainly not.

At last she turned to her husband.

"Do you suppose anyone remembers why they came?"

Garlan looked startled.

"To see us married, my lady."

"Then they have done so."

"Yes."

"And now they have found another marriage to discuss."

He glanced toward the other end of the table.

"Lord Rowan's son is to marry a Penrose."

"I heard."

"It is a very fine match."

"I heard that too."

Garlan considered.

"Would you like to dance?"

Leonette looked at him.

For a moment Orryn thought she might refuse.

Then she smiled.

"Yes. I should."

Garlan rose and offered his hand.

The musicians began another tune.

The bride and groom moved into the open space before the dais, and the guests turned to watch them.

Orryn thought that at last Leonette had achieved what she wanted.

He supposed she would need to dance the whole night if she wished to keep it.

She moved prettily. Garlan was not graceful, but he followed her carefully and never stepped upon her gown.

Orryn saw Lady Jeyne watching with an expression he could not read.

Then he saw Alyn standing beside the entrance to the hall.

He wore Rowan colours.

A sword hung at his hip.

He watched the dancers with an easy smile, one hand resting upon the hilt of his sword.

Orryn wondered whether Leonette saw him.

He wished she would not.

He wished Alyn would turn away.

He wished, suddenly and fiercely, that Alyn were still a squire at Grassfield, with a stolen flask beneath the bench and nothing to do with Lord Rowan.

Alyn glanced toward him.

Their eyes met.

Alyn raised his cup.

Orryn felt his face grow warm.

He raised his own hand in answer.

Then a servant called for him, and he was gone.

*

Later, when the feast had grown louder and the candles burned lower, Orryn was sent with a fresh jug to the small chamber beside the hall.

Lord Rowan and Lord Armond had withdrawn there with Lord Alaric, Ser Androw Ashford and two older knights whom Orryn did not know.

The door stood open.

Orryn entered quietly.

They were not seated.

Lord Rowan stood beside the hearth, speaking to Armond.

"...not against Lord Leo."

"I have said as much," Armond answered.

"Then say it again."

"To whom?"

"To anyone who chooses to forget it."

Armond laughed.

"That might keep me busy."

Rowan smiled.

"It is not a burden to be loyal to a lord who has earned it."

Alaric stood near the window.

"And what of his son?"

"Ser Garth will inherit Highgarden," Rowan said. "No man here disputes it."

"That is not what I asked."

"No."

The lord of Goldengrove looked toward the hall.

"His inheritance does not give him leave to unmake every right his father has sworn to uphold."

"And Ser Moryn?" Alaric asked.

Rowan considered.

"Ser Moryn understands that a lord is more than the keeper of another man's accounts."

"He has many friends."

"He does."

"And he has few lands."

"That may change."

The answer came so easily that Orryn wondered whether Rowan knew something he did not.

Alaric was quiet.

Lord Armond said, "I told Moryn I would support a proper settlement of the courts. I told Lord Leo the same. I told Garth too, though I fear he heard only the first half."

"And you will stand by it?" Rowan asked.

"I will."

"Even if Ser Garth dislikes it?"

"I have been disliked before."

Rowan held out his hand.

Armond took it.

It was a simple gesture, one lord to another.

Orryn had seen men clasp hands over horses, debts, wagers and promises of marriage. Sometimes it meant very little.

This time the room grew quiet.

Ser Androw looked toward Alaric.

Alaric did not move.

Rowan released Armond's hand.

"Then we shall speak again after the wedding."

"At Goldengrove?" Armond asked.

"If that suits you."

"It may."

Orryn filled the cups.

He wondered whether he had just witnessed a promise.

He had heard no oath.

He had heard no name of a faction.

But Lord Armond, who had gone to Highgarden intending to hear both Leo and Moryn, was now speaking of meeting Rowan again at Goldengrove.

Orryn thought of Lady Jeyne's prediction.

My father will be a Moryn man by the next moon.

Perhaps she had been right.

Perhaps it was not so simple.

When he turned to leave, Rowan spoke.

"Grandison."

Orryn stopped.

"My lord?"

"Your cousin is married into the Penroses, I believe."

"Yes, my lord. Lady Elenei Grandison married Ser Addam Penrose."

"And you have been to Parchments?"

"I have."

"Did you meet Lady Joy?"

"Not properly, my lord."

Rowan nodded.

"No matter."

He took his cup.

"An old house, the Penroses. Good people to know."

Orryn bowed.

He had the uncomfortable impression that Lord Rowan had asked a question to which he already knew the answer.

He was relieved when Alaric sent him back to the hall.

*

The feast ended late.

Orryn carried empty cups until his arms ached, helped the servants clear the tables and then went to the stables to see whether Rook had been watered.

He found Alyn there.

The knight stood beside his horse, loosening the girth while a groom held a lantern.

"Grandview."

Orryn stopped.

"Ser Alyn."

"You've been worked hard."

"There's a wedding."

"So I noticed."

Alyn smiled.

"How was the dancing?"

"Very fine."

"And the bride?"

"Very fine."

Alyn laughed.

"You're a poor liar."

Orryn looked down.

He remembered Leonette's face in the sept, the glance toward the rear and the bright smile she had worn when Garlan kissed her.

He remembered the herb garden.

He would never speak of it.

Alyn took the saddle from his horse and set it upon a stand.

"She's married well."

"Yes."

"Footly's a good house."

"Yes."

"And Garlan's a decent man, from what I hear."

"Yes."

Alyn looked at him.

"Have you learned any other words since I left?"

Orryn smiled despite himself.

"A few."

"Then save them. You'll need them."

He reached for a cloth.

Orryn took the other end of the saddle and helped him wipe the dust from its leather.

They worked without speaking for a while.

It was the sort of task Orryn had done beside Alyn a hundred times before.

Only now Alyn was a knight in another lord's service, and Orryn remained a squire at Grassfield.

At last Alyn said, "Lord Rowan means to ride early."

"Tomorrow?"

"Aye."

"Will you go with him?"

"Of course."

Orryn knew that.

He had known it before asking.

He wished there were another answer.

"Is Goldengrove far?"

"Far enough. Not so far as Highgarden, depending upon the road."

"I've never been."

"Perhaps you will."

Alyn said it without any particular weight, as one might speak of a market or a fair.

Orryn held on to the words nonetheless.

The groom finished with the horse.

Alyn took the lantern.

"Go to bed, Grandview. You'll be falling over your feet again tomorrow."

Orryn flushed.

"How did you know?"

"Perkin told me."

"Of course he did."

Alyn laughed.

"Don't mind him. You're young yet."

He clapped Orryn upon the shoulder.

It was a familiar gesture.

Orryn felt it long after Alyn had gone.

*

The next morning the Footlys prepared to leave for Tumbleton.

Leonette's baggage had been loaded upon two wagons, and the grey mare she had chosen for her dowry stood beside the mounting block, looking splendid and thoroughly unimpressed.

The yard filled with family and servants.

Lady Jeyne embraced her daughter for a long while.

Orryn was too far away to hear what she said.

Leonette's face was hidden against her mother's shoulder.

When they parted, her eyes were bright.

Alaric came forward next.

He kissed his daughter upon the forehead and said something that made her laugh.

Then he stepped back.

Garlan helped her mount.

The Footly household began to form upon the road.

Orryn watched as Leonette gathered her reins.

She looked up at the keep.

For a moment she seemed to be trying to remember something.

Then she turned to Garlan, who was waiting patiently beside her, and smiled.

They rode out beneath the gate.

Orryn stood with the other squires.

Perkin said, "Well. One less person to tell us what to do."

"She will write," Osric answered.

"Then we may burn the letters."

"Perkin."

"What?"

"Nothing."

Orryn smiled.

He had expected to be glad when Leonette left.

He was glad.

But he had not expected the yard to seem quite so empty afterward.

Lord Rowan departed not long after.

His household formed beneath the golden tree, the horses watered and the baggage secured. Ser Triston rode near his father. Lady Clarice travelled beneath a light canopy, with Lady Roslin beside her.

Alyn was among the knights.

He saw Orryn standing by the stable door.

For a moment Orryn thought he might come over.

Instead Alyn raised his hand.

Orryn raised his own.

Then the knights passed through the gate.

The golden tree disappeared beyond the bend in the road.

Orryn watched until the dust settled.

Behind him, Ser Wyman was calling for the remaining horses to be returned to their stalls.

There were still empty buckets to carry, a broken strap to mend and a stable to clean.

Orryn went to work.

By noon the keep was nearly itself again.

The wedding guests had gone. The musicians had packed their instruments. The long tables were being taken down, and the servants were scraping wax from the boards.

Leonette was on the road to Tumbleton.

Alyn was riding toward Goldengrove.

Lord Rowan had left Grassfield with Lord Armond Caswell's support and an agreement to meet him again.

Orryn did not know what would come of any of it.

He knew only that Alyn had been there, that he had spoken to him, and that he had gone away once more.

From the yard came Ser Everard's voice.

"Grandison!"

Orryn turned.

The master-at-arms stood beneath the beech with a wooden sword in his hand.

"Are you going to make me wait all morning?"

Orryn looked at the sword.

His feet already hurt.

"No, ser."

"Then come."

He went.

Behind him the gates stood open, and beyond them the road ran pale and dry between the fields.
