Orryn had not meant to speak. But Tull waited, and the wasps went on about their business among the windfalls, and it was plain that nobody on that hillside was going to spare him the silence.

"I would wait," he said, slowly, and heard Piers draw breath beside him. "A season. To see whether it spreads. Two trees last spring, you said, and four now, and the rust in six. It might not go so quick as that, it might—" He stopped. Two to four in a year. Four to eight, if it only doubled. Eight to sixteen. He counted it on the warm hillside like a sum he did not want, and the rows below Maud Pyke's ladder, which had looked so orderly from the gate, began to look like something drawn on a map in the wrong ink.

"No," he said. "That is Master Haverill's way, isn't it? It looks like deciding. It is only deciding later."

It was out before he had thought what it meant to say it of a man he had never met, to the lord's own servant, in the hearing of the lord's other squire. He felt the heat climb his neck. Tull said nothing, but his mouth did something at the corner that was not quite a smile, and it seemed to Orryn that the thing had been said many times on this hill, only never aloud, and never by a boy with a pear core in his fist. Piers made a noise through his nose that might have been a laugh.

"I would cut it. This autumn, when the leaves are down. And I would carry them, bread and rent, for the seven years." The words came out easily. It was not his bread. "And her son. Master Tull, you said he was lame, but a lame man isn't a helpless one. He could work elsewhere in the meantime and bring something in, so that she isn't living on the lord's hand alone, and the lord isn't carrying every bit of it."

Tull regarded him for a time with the pewter eyes, in no hurry whatever. "Col Pyke," he said. "Thirty-one years. He fell off the high ladder at sixteen and the leg knit crooked. He is the best grafter in this block, and I'll say so before the reeve or the lord or the Mother herself. Aye. He could work." The old man rubbed his jaw. "Where?"

"I—" Orryn said. "The picking. At harvest."

"Who picks?"

"The holders."

"And their kin, and their neighbours' sons who owe them a day. Every ladder in the vale is spoken for by midsummer. A lame stranger takes a place from a neighbour's boy, and the neighbour remembers it at the harvest board for ten years. Where else?"

"The town," said Orryn. "The mill—" and stopped, because Piers was looking at him.

"My father's mill is run by my mother's brother and his sons," said Piers. "The town takes its own. I've seen my father's book, Grandview. It is full of lame men who were going to find work." He said it as flatly as he had counted the trees. "They find it for a month at the picking. Then it is winter, and they come to the house with their caps in their hands, and he lends them a little, kindly."

"Seven winters," said Tull. "A pear takes seven years, and a lame man's work is the picking, and the picking is three weeks. That is what you don't see yet, young master, looking down from where you stand. Work in a vale is a gift, same as bread. It's given out by custom, and every place is somebody's. Only the lord has enough of it to hand to a man without taking it from his neighbour. So when you say let the son work elsewhere, you have said let the lord give him work, and the lord is carrying them after all, only by another door." He paused. "Which is not a foolish thing to say. It is the better door."

"Why the better?"

"Because of what it makes of them. A widow fed from the lord's bread is a charity. A holder's son paid a day's wage to grub her row and plant it again is a man with a trade, and she is a holder whose row is being made new. The same coin out of the same purse. The vale doesn't see it the same." The old hand lifted from the trunk. "Hire him by the day, a full wage, mind, no less than a stranger's, to grub and to plant, and to graft for other holders in the lean years, since there's none better. You carry the less, and he keeps his name. That is a good thought. I've heard worse from reeves." He shook his head. "It is not the whole. The wage runs a season, and the winters run seven. Something still falls on the lord, and he had best know how much before he promises it."

"How much?" Orryn said, and then tried to answer himself before anyone could, which was a thing he did and could not stop. "A row, in a good year?"

"Two hundred baskets, in this block."

"Then the lord's third is—"

"Call it seventy."

"Seventy, for seven years." He did it by tens, as he had been taught: seven sevens were forty-nine, so four hundred and ninety. "Four hundred and ninety baskets," he said, with some satisfaction, and Piers, who had opened his mouth, shut it again.

It was a small thing, seventy by seven, a child's sum. But it was the first of the day that he had reached before Piers, and he took it as a man takes a cup of water in a dry place, and was ashamed afterwards of how much he had wanted it.

"And the bread?" said Tull.

Orryn's satisfaction left him. "I didn't count the bread."

"No. Nobody does, the first time. Meal for two for seven years, and salt, and wood for the fire, and a roof that wants thatching. Ask Master Verrall. He keeps a price for every one of them." The old man took the billhook from his belt and looked at it, as if the figures were written along the blade. "Bring him that. Bring him the baskets, and the bread, and the wage, and lay it before his lordship plain, with what is lost on either side. It is not a clever answer. It's a lord's answer. Most of it is only sums, and someone to say them aloud."

Orryn found that his ears were hot. Tull had not scolded him. He had said that the idea was good, and told him in the same breath how much of it was missing, and the second half was the part that stayed. He thought of Ser Steffon at the foot of the stair, counting. He thought that his father would have found the bread in a breath. He thought that his father would never in his life have said it was a good thought.

The afternoon had worn on while they talked. The sun stood over the ridge now and lay heavy on the back of his neck, and the pear he had eaten sat sour and uncertain in his stomach, and his ribs ached with the standing. Below them the sheep had drifted down towards the brook in a slow grey tide. A ladder creaked, and somewhere a mallet knocked at a post, and the smell of the sweet rot of windfalls was everywhere, so thick that he could taste it at the back of his teeth. He thought that he would remember this smell. He thought that he would never be able to smell a pear again without remembering a sum he had not finished.

It was then that the man came up the row from the brook.

He had a bundle of bare twigs bound with bast on one shoulder, and he walked with a hitch, the left leg swinging stiffly from the hip, quick for all that, like a man who had long since stopped noticing it. He had Maud Pyke's height and Maud Pyke's hard hands and none of her laugh. He was brown and close-bearded, with a long wary face, and he looked at the two squires' good boots, and at Tull, and at the black sunken patch on the trunk beside Tull's hand.

Orryn watched him see it. He watched him understand it, and not be surprised. Col Pyke set his bundle down very carefully, as a man sets down something that might break.

"You've shown them, then," he said.

"I've shown them," said Tull.

"I've known since the spring." His voice was low. On the ladder, thirty feet off, his mother's tuneless singing had stopped, and then, after a moment, started again. "I cut out the worst of the four and burned it and put clay on the wounds. It wasn't enough. She doesn't know. I'd rather she didn't, not before the leaves fall." His eyes went to the black bark and came back. "What does the lord say?"

"The lord has not been told," said Tull. "Not by the reeve. Not yet."

Piers had gone the colour of tallow and was studying a wasp on his sleeve. Col Pyke looked at him for a moment, at the fine doublet, and Orryn saw him know whose son it was and say nothing. Then the grey-brown eyes moved on, and settled on Orryn, and stayed there.

"Then what are they for?" he said. "The lord's boys. What did you bring them to my mother's row for?"

He did not say it rudely. He waited, in the sun, with one hand on the bundle of twigs, and the wasps went on among the windfalls, and nobody came to Orryn's rescue.

What does Orryn do?

1. Tell Col plainly what he told Tull: cut the row, carry them for the seven years, and give Col a day's wage to grub and graft, adding that none of it is his to promise.

2. Say only that they are here to learn what a row asks, and ask Col to show them how he grafts, leaving the decision where it belongs.

3. Ask Col, apart from the others, what he would want if it were his mother's row and his own name, before a word is said to Lord Alaric.
