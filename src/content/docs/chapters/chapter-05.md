"Stop on the far side," said Orryn. "Please."

Wat did not turn his head. He drew the grey horse up on the gravel just past the water's edge, where the road climbed into a bank of willows, and set the brake. The wheels stopped dripping. Nobody spoke. Pate looked at the willows, and then at the sky, as if an extra hour in a day might be read off it.

Orryn had no good reason to give them. He had it in mind that a man who rode down a hill after you at that speed meant to be spoken to, and that a Grandison who drove off in a cart without turning round would be remembered as one who had. It was not much to build a halt on. He sat with his mother's bundle on his knees and said nothing, and the grey horse, being asked nothing, lowered its head and began to drink from the shallows with long, thoughtful pulls.

Across the ford the rider came down the slope at a canter. The chestnut courser took the last bend with its shoulders low and went splashing through the ruts of the approach, and the second horse, a bay, laboured behind it with a boy on its back who rode as if he had been told he would be sick if he looked up. They checked at the rope. It was still down. Ser Osmund Sedgley had come out onto the gravel with the cable coiled over one arm, and he lifted the other hand in a wide, theatrical greeting, and his voice came across the water in pieces, large and hearty and softened by the distance: "...Penrose! ...the house itself, ...come to look at my ford..."

Ser Jon did not dismount. He leaned down from the saddle and said something to Ser Osmund that made the big man throw back his head and laugh, and then he pointed, with his whole arm, across the water at the cart. Ser Osmund shaded his eyes. The thin clerk Harl, behind him, had opened the book.

It was a quiet minute. A fish rose somewhere among the willow roots, and Pate shifted on the bench. Orryn found, to his surprise, that his hands were not quite still on the bundle, and put them flat.

Then Ser Jon touched his heels to the courser and came through the water.

He came straight across, not at the ford's shallow line but wherever the horse chose, so that the spray flew up in sheets and the sun caught it, and the boy on the bay followed more cautiously, picking the shallows. On the far side Ser Jon pulled up beside the cart so close that the chestnut's wet flank brushed the wheel. He was still in the crimson silk coat of the night before, much creased now, with a smear of something on one cuff that might have been wine. His thick auburn hair stood up in the back. He had slept little, Orryn thought, and it did not show in his face at all. It showed only in the care with which he sat, a man at ease who has remembered to be.

"Master Counter," he said.

It was said with a grin. Orryn did not know whether to be flattered. "Ser Jon."

"I thought it was you. I said so to Willem. I said, that is a Grandison on a cart, with a face like a man who's been fined." He glanced back. "Willem Tarn, my squire. Willem, say good day to the boy who found my brother's wife a better room."

The boy on the bay inclined his head. He was sixteen or so, long-armed, sunburnt across the nose, with a sulky mouth and red-rimmed eyes. Orryn knew him now. It was the squire who had lost the ring-tilting to Arlan, and had been made to carry his lance back across the yard in front of the whole household. "Good day," said Willem, to a point above Orryn's head.

"He's a poor loser," said Ser Jon, comfortably. "It's why I keep him." He swung one leg across the saddle and sat sidesaddle, with a hand on the courser's neck, looking down at the three of them on the bench with undisguised interest. "Osmund's caught you, then. He told me he had. A silver stag a wheel-pair, is it? He has hung that rope for nine years and taken a mule and a pedlar. He will tell the story till harvest."

"Two stags," said Orryn. "And three pence."

"Two stags and three pence." Ser Jon sounded delighted. "From a Grandison. Oh, my lord uncle will hear of that." He did not say whether he would repeat it with sympathy or with relish, and he did not seem to feel that it mattered. "Never mind. It's a custom, and I'm told they're sacred. I'd not have the rope cut for the world. I'm too fond of Osmund."

That was all he said about the toll. He did not offer to return the coin, nor did he suggest that he could. Orryn noted that, without being sure what to make of it. Behind him on the bench Wat sat as if turned to stone.

"I came for another reason," Ser Jon went on. "Two, in fact. The first is Osmund's hounds. He keeps a pack for the Sedgeford boar, which nobody has seen in four years, and I mean to kill it or die of the attempt. You can't imagine how restful it is. The second is you."

"Me, Ser?"

"My mother woke me at sunrise." He said it with an easy rue, as one mentions the weather in a house that is always damp. "She is not a woman who wakes people. She sends for them. This morning she stood at the foot of my bed and said, The boy who keeps the rooms is going west in a cart. That was all. She said it the way she says a name when she has put it in a ledger." He looked at Orryn with his head a little on one side. "She hasn't spoken a word to your uncle since the door. Not one. She spoke to me for an hour. I'll tell you, Master Counter, I have never known her to say so much and mean so little by any of it."

Orryn said nothing. There seemed to be nothing in the sentence he could take hold of. He thought of the pale blue eyes in the torchlight, and the sentence she had left him with, and felt his face grow warm.

"She wants you mounted," said Ser Jon.

"Mounted?"

"On a horse. It's a thing the Penroses are rather particular about, being a house that writes things down. We don't lend our guests to the Reach in a hay cart." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and a shade rueful, and Orryn could not tell what was behind it, or whether anything was. "Osmund keeps four decent horses in his stable for Parchments. I know the one I mean. A dun gelding, seven years old, sound in wind and limb, ridden by half the household and bitten by none. You may have it to ride to Grassfield. Leave it with Lord Alaric's grooms when you're settled, and one of ours will fetch it home in the autumn. Or keep it, if the Reach suits you." He flicked a hand. "My mother's thought, not mine. But I'll put my name to it."

Across the water the tall figure of Ser Osmund stood with the cable on his arm, watching them. He had stopped smiling. He had the look of a man who has been told that a horse of his is to be given away, and is deciding how much of the fact he can afford to resent.

"It's a handsome offer," said Orryn, and heard the lameness of it.

"It's a courteous one. They are different things, my mother taught me, and it's the second that costs more." Ser Jon laughed, and for a moment he looked entirely like what he was: a rich and handsome young man of twenty-two, on a good horse, in the sun. "Don't mistake me. There is nothing in it. A horse for a boy who did the rooms, and was blamed for a thing that wasn't his, and bore it well. I thought, at the time, that it was handsomely done. Between ourselves I thought Alester had behaved like an ass." He lowered his voice, a little, though not enough. "But a gift is a gift. You know what we say at Parchments. We write it down."

Wat, beside Orryn, drew a very slow breath. Pate had turned to look at the gelding Ser Jon had mentioned, though it could not be seen from there. It stood, Orryn supposed, in a stall on the other bank, dun and sound and ridden by half of Parchments, waiting for a boy to say a word.

Ser Jon waited. He did not hurry. He sat his horse in the long light with his crimson sleeve crumpled and the river running behind him, and he seemed in no way troubled by the silence. It occurred to Orryn that this was a man who had never in his life been refused, and had therefore never learned to dread it, and that this was probably the most dangerous thing about him.

"Well, Master Counter?" he said. "What shall I tell my mother?"

What does Orryn do?

1. Accept the horse outright. Thank Ser Jon, take the dun gelding, and ride it to Grassfield. You arrive mounted, which is what Lord Alaric did not provide. You also arrive on a Penrose horse with the Penrose house's name on the loan.

2. Accept, on your own terms. Take it as a loan only, name when it will be returned, and ask Ser Jon to put that in the toll-book as a written note. This is careful and organised. It may also look like you doubt a Penrose gift.

3. Decline politely. Thank Ser Jon and say that Lord Alaric sent a cart and you will ride in it. You owe nothing, but Lady Alysanne's courtesy is turned down, and Ser Jon may report it in the worst light.

4. Ask Wat first. Turn to Wat and ask whether Lord Alaric would object. Wat answers in his own way, which may be useful, and which shows Ser Jon that you do not decide without your lord's men.

5. Ask a question before answering. Ask Ser Jon why his mother wants this. He may tell you more than you expect, or he may enjoy being asked. It delays your answer and shows you wonder at a gift.
