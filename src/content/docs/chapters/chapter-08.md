He let Rook out at the first long stretch of straight road, where the hedges fell back and the land lay open on both sides, and the dun gelding, who had walked all afternoon behind a cart with the patience of a monk, put down his ears and went.

It was not fast. Rook was not a fast horse; he was a steady one, a plain, deep-chested animal made for carrying a man all day, and what he gave Orryn was a long loping canter that ate the ground in even bites. But it was movement. After a morning on a bench with a pillow at his back and a sword rattling at his feet, it went to his head like wine. The wind came up past his ears. The road unrolled in front of him, pale and rutted, and the evening laid long gold bars across it from the western hedges. He leaned forward over the dun's neck. He heard himself laugh, once, quite loudly, and was glad that there was no one to hear.

He had not been alone since the door of the guard hall. He had not noticed that until now. There had been the hammering, and the kitchens, and the lists, and the whole of Grandview pressing in from every side with its needs; and then Alyssa on the wall, and Steffon at the trough, and his mother's wet face; and then the cart, with its two silent men. All day there had been someone's eyes on him, or the memory of them. Now there were only the hedges flicking past and the soft drum of hooves, and the sky, which was very large.

Behind him, the cart was slowly falling out of earshot. He knew he should have felt it. Wat had said nothing when he touched his heels to the dun; he had only turned his head, a little, and looked, and gone back to the road. Orryn had not asked leave. It had not occurred to him until he was a furlong gone that he ought to have, and by then it was too late to feel anything but the speed.

The road climbed. The hedges gave way to a long, bare back of heath, heather going brown in the heat, and the wind blew through it with a dry whisper; and on the crest of the ridge, where the road crossed a track running north and south, there was a stone.

He drew rein and sat looking at it.

It was a grey standing stone, taller than a man, leaning slightly, furred with lichen the colour of old brass. It stood a little off the road in a ring of cropped turf, and it did not look like a thing that anyone had put up. It looked as though the heath had grown it. There were marks on it. He rode closer, and Rook, who had no feeling for history, stopped to blow. On the face turned toward the east, toward Grandview, the weather had worn away almost everything, but he could still make out a stag, with its antlers raised, and above the stag the broken line of a crown. On the other face, the one turned west, a rose. That was almost smoothed away as well.

So that was the border. It was not a river or a wall. It was a rock on a heath, with two worn pictures. He had known for weeks that he would cross it, and he had pictured something larger, a gate, a custom, a change in the air. A moment passed. Rook shifted his weight. In the long grass at the stone's foot a grasshopper sang, one dry note, and was answered.

Behind him lay everything that was his. The stag was Storm's End, the stag of the Baratheons, the great lord on the sea who sat over his father's house and his uncle's and a hundred more; and the stag meant Grandview, the Old Lion with its sleeping lion on gold, and the hall hung with ribbon, and Meg's keys, and the garden chamber with its green hangings. It meant a language he could speak in his sleep. Ahead was the rose, the Tyrells of Highgarden, who held half the Reach and were said to hold the other half in their pockets, and he knew nearly nothing about them. Pears, said Elenei. Don't say it's pears. He smiled in spite of himself.

He did not dismount. He did not murmur a prayer to the Seven, as some men would; he was not sure what a prayer was for, at a stone. He touched the dun's flanks and rode, a few paces, between the stag and the rose, and the land on the other side of the line was exactly the same as the land on this.

Then the road tipped over the ridge and the Reach lay before him.

It was green. That was the first thing, and it surprised him, for he had been looking at brown land all summer. The Stormlands in high summer were scorched and hard and a little bitter, with the stones coming through. Here the slope fell away in long folds of orchard and meadow, hedged and ditched and sleek, and it went on and on, one soft hill after another into a blue haze, and every field seemed to have been combed. There were pear trees. He saw them at once and felt a stupid pleasure: whole rows of them, heavy with small hard fruit, standing in the evening light with their shadows lying long and blue between. Smoke rose from a farm somewhere below. A flock of geese went honking across the valley in a ragged line. The air smelled of cut hay and ripe fruit, and of woodsmoke, and, very faintly, of rain that had not fallen yet.

It looked like a place where nothing was ever late. He thought, with a small flicker of contempt that he recognised from his father's voice, that it would be a great deal of trouble to keep a place like that going. And then, with a flicker of honesty that was his own: that someone did.

He rode down.

It was a mile or a little more to the inn. He knew it from the smoke before he saw it, a plume going up straight in the still air from a low, whitewashed building at a crossroads, with a thatched roof and a great sprawling yard and a painted sign swinging on a post. As he came closer he made out a pear, and a plough beneath it, in bright, bad paint. The road ran past the yard gate, and there was a long water trough, and a rail where three or four horses were tied, and a good deal of noise: a dog barking, a woman's voice scolding somebody, the clatter of crockery.

He was halfway to the yard when he saw the mule.

It was tied at the end of the rail, with a bundle lashed behind the saddle and a quantity of dust on its legs: a thin, rough-coated brute with white socks, a bald patch on its rump, and a look of bitter, universal grievance. He knew it at once. It had spent a night in the old barn at Grandview with Ser Jon's hounds, and had expressed its opinion of them in a way that nobody had been able to ignore. It turned its long ears toward him as he came up, and showed him its yellow teeth.

"Seven save us," Orryn said to it, aloud.

On the bench beside the inn door, in the last of the sun, there sat an old man with a jug.

He was the oldest man Orryn knew. He might have been seventy-five, or ninety; he had the pared, brown, desiccated look of something that had been left out in all weathers and had refused to rot. His head was quite bald, mottled like an egg, with a fringe of white at the ears. One eye was clouded. A long scar ran from his temple down across his cheek into the white stubble of his beard, and he sat very straight, with a staff between his knees and his two hands folded on it, and a cup of something dark at his elbow. Beside him, on the boards, lay a battered sword in a battered scabbard, as one lays down a hat. He wore a faded green surcoat with something on the breast that might once have been a device.

Ser Wystan. He had come to the wedding in the crowd of Peasebury cousins, and had frightened a groom in the gate; he had been one of Lord Gawen's own sworn swords, they said, in the war. Alester called him by his first name and treated him with a fond, careful tolerance, as one treats a clock that is still ticking and has long since lost the hour. Orryn had been told his surname once and had lost it. Nobody ever used it. He had been Ser Wystan for fifty years.

The clouded eye turned toward him. The good one, a bright washed blue, followed it down to the horse.

"Hold," said Ser Wystan. His voice was a cracked bellow, startling in that small body. "Hold there, boy. That's Osmund Sedgley's dun."

Orryn drew rein. Rook, hearing his name, if he understood it, stood very still.

"I've seen that horse go over the Sedgeford water thirty times," said the old man. "Osmund won't lend it to his own mother. He'd sooner lend his wife." He picked up his cup, looked into it with disfavour, put it down again. "You're Steffon's brat. The little counter. I saw you at the gate. You gave me a room, and then you gave it away to a woman, and I never knew whether to thank you or hit you." The blue eye gleamed. "Now come here and tell me how you came by Osmund Sedgley's best horse. And be quick about it, boy. I'm eighty-one, and I mean to have the whole of the story before I die."

Orryn sat the dun in the evening light, with the wind in the pear trees and the mule glaring at him from the rail, and the road empty behind him as far as the stone. The cart was a mile off, perhaps more. He did not know how long he had before it came.

What does Orryn do?

1. Dismount and sit with Ser Wystan. Tell him the story of the ford, and listen. He is old, drunk, and one of Lord Gawen's men. If he is in the mood, he may tell you something about Grandview that nobody at the wedding would say.

2. Ask him about Lord Gawen and the war. Skip the horse and put it to him straight: why did Grandview wait? He is the person most likely to know, and the least likely to be polite about it. He may also take offence, or refuse to say.

3. Take care of the practical things first. See the innkeeper about rooms, stalls and supper for three, before the cart arrives. This is what a careful boy would do. But you have no purse. Wat holds it, and Alaric's money was meant for the road, not for an inn.

4. Wait at the gate for Wat and Pate. You rode ahead without leave, and the least you can do is be seen to be waiting for them. It is the safe choice. It leaves Ser Wystan to shout across the yard, and you answer from the saddle.

5. Answer briefly and ride back to meet the cart. Give Ser Wystan two lines, promise him the rest at supper, and go back along the road to join Wat. It shows you know you were wrong to leave them, and costs you some of the old man's good humour.
