"I would be glad of the horse, Ser," said Orryn. "Thank you. And thank Lady Alysanne."

That was the easy part, and it was out before he had time to dislike it. Ser Jon's smile widened. Orryn drew a breath and went on, because there was a sentence in him that wanted saying and if it was not said now it would not be said at all.

"Will you carry a message for me? To her. Not only that I am grateful. She'll have that from you already." He found his hands had gripped the bundle again. "Tell her I did not think that I would be remembered. Not by someone of her house. And that I will see the horse is kept as she would keep it herself. That I am her servant in it. That's all."

It sounded, once spoken, a good deal grander than he had intended and a good deal smaller than he had hoped, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man. He wanted to take it back and say it again plainly. But Ser Jon had put his head on one side and was listening with the same bright, unhurried attention he had given the willows, and when it was done he gave a short laugh, delighted rather than mocking.

"I did not think that I would be remembered," he repeated. "Oh, that's good. That is very good. That is the very thing she will want to have been said." He shook his head. "I'll give it word for word, Master Counter. I may tidy the stammers. She can't abide a stammer."

"I didn't stammer."

"Not once," said Ser Jon gravely. "I was thinking of the one before." And he winked, and Orryn felt the warmth go up the back of his neck again and was angry at it, as if it were a dog that had followed him from home and would not be sent back.

"Willem," said Ser Jon, without turning. "Go and tell Osmund I want the dun. The dun, mind, not the one he'll try to give you. And Harl is to write it down."

The boy on the bay said, "Yes, ser," in the voice of someone made to fetch a thing he did not care about for a person he cared about less, and turned his horse back into the water.

They waited. It was not long, though it felt it. Ser Jon talked easily, about the Sedgeford boar and the hounds that were said to be coming from Cafferen, and about a mare of his own that had taken a fence in the rain; Orryn answered when he was asked and found that he could say nothing more intelligent than yes and I see. Beside him Pate had stopped looking at the reins. He was looking, with an expression of careful attention, at the far bank, where Willem had dismounted and was waiting before the toll-house door with the air of a man delivering a summons.

Ser Osmund came out. Even at that distance his shoulders said all of it. There was an exchange, shorter than Orryn expected, with a great deal of arm in it on the Sedgeford side, and then the big knight turned and strode into the yard behind the toll-house and a minute later came out leading a horse by the cheekpiece, with Harl at his elbow and Willem trailing.

It came across the ford behind the bay, picking its way with a mild, steady step. A plain horse: dun-coated, black-legged, with a dark stripe down the spine and a thick, sensible neck, and the sort of face that has seen a good many people ride it and held no opinion about any of them. It was perhaps fifteen hands. It had been rubbed down. Someone, some stable-boy in a temper, had braided a ribbon into its forelock and then, on second thoughts, taken it out, and a few kinked hairs still showed where it had been.

Harl came with it, ankle-deep and aggrieved, holding the toll-book above the water in both hands like a priest with a relic. He stopped at the wheel, tore a leaf from the back of the book, and offered it up to Orryn without a word.

It was written small and upright, in the same hand as the toll. At Sedgeford, by order of Ser Jon Penrose, for Lady Alysanne Penrose of Parchments: a dun gelding, seven years, called Rook, sound, lent from the Sedgeford stable to Master Orryn Grandison of Grandview, to be ridden west. Entered in the afternoon. Witness: H. Clerk. Below that was a blot where the pen had hesitated, and a signature in a large, loose, handsome hand that Orryn had to look at twice before he realised it was Ser Jon's.

"I told you," said Ser Jon, pleased. "We write it down."

Orryn read it again. He found that his thumb had gone to the corner of the leaf and was smoothing it flat. It said nothing about return. It said nothing about price. It said that the horse was lent, and who had lent it, and why, in nine lines, and he found that he was grateful for every one. A thing written was a thing that could be shown to a man who liked paragraphs. He folded the leaf in three along the creases and put it in his doublet, behind the letter, and felt them lie there together, the one from the man he was going to and the one from the woman he had left.

"Thank you, Master Harl," he said.

The clerk looked startled, and then gratified, and then looked at his master across the water, and made himself blank again. "Sir," he said, and went back through the ford with the book held very high.

Pate got down first. He did it without a word, and walked round to the horse's head and stood a moment with a hand on its neck, letting it smell him. Then he bent and, one after another, lifted each of the dun's feet and looked into the hoof with the slow, intent face of a boy reading a letter in a language he knew. He ran his thumb round the frog. He set the fourth foot down.

"He's sound," he said. His deep voice was gentle, as if the words were for the horse. "Shod well. Not young, not old. He'll go all day."

It was the longest speech Orryn had heard from him. "Thank you," he said.

Pate shrugged. It might have been embarrassment. He gave the girth a tug and led the dun round to the side of the cart, and held the stirrup, and stood back.

Orryn climbed down off the bench with his legs stiff from the hours of sitting. He put his sword on properly at last, buckling the belt so that the scabbard hung where it ought, and had a moment's worry that Ser Jon would see him fumble. Ser Jon was looking at the river. The saddle was a plain one, with worn leather and a high cantle, and the stirrups had been left at a length for a taller man, and Pate set them up three holes without being asked. Orryn took the reins. The dun turned its head and breathed on his sleeve.

He swung up. The ground fell away, and then, as it always did, the world rearranged itself into a better shape. Everything was a little wider and lower and nearer. The road ran off between the willows with the evening on it, and the brown river lay behind, and the cart was beside him instead of under him, a thing he travelled with and not in. It was the first time since the gate that he had been able to see over the canvas. He drew a long breath and did not hide it.

"There," said Ser Jon. "That's better. You look like a person." He gathered the courser's reins. "Go well, Master Counter. Write to her, if you've a mind. My mother answers letters. It's the only vice I know of that she has."

"I will," said Orryn, before he had thought, and then thought: I have said it. And let it stand.

Ser Jon grinned, lifted a hand, and turned the chestnut with a touch of his heels. He splashed back through the ford with Willem sullenly behind him, and they went up the far bank to where Ser Osmund waited, arms folded, on the gravel. The crimson coat blazed in the last of the sun. Orryn watched it as far as the toll-house door, and a good deal longer than that, and then looked up and found Pate watching him, with that pale, patient look, and Pate looked at the horse's ears.

"His lordship'll ask whose horse," said Wat.

He had not spoken since the dun came. He sat with the reins slack in his hands and looked at the road, and he said it mildly, as one gives the time. There was no blame in it that Orryn could hear. He thought that there might be something else, and could not tell what.

"Then I'll tell him," said Orryn.

"Aye." Wat clicked to the grey. The cart creaked and began to roll. "Tell him all of it, young master. Every word. He'll have the book."

The evening came on slowly. The road climbed out of the river valley by easy stages, between hedgerows gone silver with dust, and the first swifts came out and cut the air above the stubble. Orryn rode a half-length behind the cart's wheel, not near enough to talk and not near enough to be left. Rook moved under him with a plain good will, like a man who had decided long ago to like his work. In the fold of his doublet the two papers rustled. He did not take them out. He knew what each of them said.

What does Orryn do?

1. Ride ahead. Let Rook out for a mile on the open road, alone. You enjoy the horse and the evening, and you see what is ahead before the cart reaches it. You also leave Wat and Pate behind, and Alaric's men may mind that.

2. Stay beside Wat and ask about Lord Alaric. Ride level with the cart and ask what kind of man he is. Wat may say little, but he is the only person on the road who knows. Whatever he does say is a fact you can use. It also shows him a boy who asks.

3. Write a note for Alaric tonight. At the next halt, set down what happened at the ford: the toll, the horse, the slip from Harl. You tell Alaric before Wat does. It is exact, and it is yours. It also puts into writing a story that is more flattering to you than any other version would be.

4. Ride with Pate and talk horses. Pate has spoken more about Rook than he has about anything else. A friend among Alaric's men is worth having. He is the quiet one, though, and may not want to be pushed.

5. Write to Lady Alysanne. Ser Jon said she answers letters. A first letter, from the road, carries your thanks further than a message does. It is also the beginning of something with a woman who keeps accounts of what she is owed.
