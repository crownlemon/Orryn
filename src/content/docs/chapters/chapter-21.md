The bell woke him before the light, a flat iron bell struck by someone with no love for it, and Osric Dunmere's voice followed it down the chamber before Orryn had found out which way was up. "Prayers, then porridge, then the yard. His lordship's word, by way of the steward: the new squire is to be at arms until noon, and in the solar after."

Orryn sat up. His legs ached from two days of cart and one of horse, and his ribs ached from laughing, and he looked, quickly, under the blanket and then under the pallet. Perkin watched from across the room with his chin in his hands and an expression of enormous innocence.

"Thursday," Perkin said, kindly.

Prayers were said in the small sept off the lower yard by a septon who yawned through the Warrior, and the porridge was thick and grey with a spoonful of stewed pear in the middle of it, and then they went out in a line, six boys with their breath smoking, to the place behind the stables where Grassfield taught its sons to kill.

It was a long rectangle of beaten earth gone dark with the night's damp. A gallery of old timber ran down one side, where the household knights stood when they stood at all, and at the far end a quintain leaned on its post, the crosspiece worn pale where a hundred lances had struck it. Along the wall hung the practice swords: oak, black with sweat and handling, one for each boy and a few over. Beyond the wall the pear trees stood in their rows, stripped of the last of the fruit, with the sun just getting into them.

One knight was there already, standing on the gallery steps with his arms folded. He was tall and square-jawed, with dark hair cropped close and a stiff, straight back, and his plain mail looked as though someone had ironed it. "Ser Lucan Marwick," Perkin breathed, at Orryn's ear. "Household knight. He doesn't care for Stormlanders. He doesn't care for much. But he's fair, so you'll hate it."

Ser Lucan looked at Orryn once, from the boots up, in the manner of a man reading the label on a cask, and then looked away at the quintain.

The master-at-arms came when the sun stood a hand above the wall, which the boys seemed to regard as early. Orryn knew him for what he was before anyone said it: a tall man who had once been handsome and was now running to fat, with silver hair tied back at the nape and a long nose broken over with fine red veins. His doublet was frayed at the cuffs and immaculate everywhere else, and he walked across the yard with great care, like a man carrying a full cup. When he reached them he put his hand on the pommel of his sword and left it there, and Orryn saw that the hand was trembling a little, and that the pommel was what he held it with.

"Gentlemen," said Ser Everard Blythe. His voice was lovely: low and unhurried, the voice of someone who had once been paid to recite. "The morning. Let us not waste it." His pale, pouched eyes travelled down the line and stopped. "A new face. Come here, child."

Orryn went. Someone put an oak sword in his hand; it was heavier than the ones at Grandview, and cold. "Show me how you stand."

He stood. He had stood so a hundred times in the yard at Grandview, with the master of arms there, a patient man with a bad knee. He put his left foot forward and bent his knees and held the sword out in front of him like a bar across a door.

Ser Everard walked all the way round him. He did it slowly, once, with his hands behind him. When he came to the front again he said, almost gently, "Someone was kind to you."

"Ser?"

"Whoever taught you. They were patient and they were fond of you, and you have been trading on it ever since." He tapped Orryn's heel with the toe of his boot, and the heel was where the weight was. "You stand on your heels because you are thinking. A boy who thinks in the lists is listening to his own funeral. And you swing like a Stormlander, as though the sword were a mallet and the other man a stake." The pouched eyes did not smile. They were very sharp. "You will unlearn it by midsummer, or you will be hit until you do. Thirty advances. Thirty retreats. Do not think."

The footwork was the hardest hour of his life, for it was so very dull. Forward, back, forward, back, up and down the packed earth with the sword held out and his thighs beginning to shake by the tenth, while the others paired off behind him and the oak clacked and the master-at-arms moved among them murmuring corrections in that beautiful voice, never once raising it. Orryn counted without meaning to. At eighteen he lost count and had to begin again. At twenty-five a woman with a basket crossed the far end of the yard and he looked at her and felt the heel of the sword-hand come up and the weight go back, and Ser Everard said, from nowhere, "There. That is how a man dies: looking at a basket."

He was on his third round of thirty, or perhaps his fourth, when a boy came over the rail.

He did not use the gate. He came over the top rail with one hand and landed running, as if the rail had been put there for the purpose, and every other boy in the yard stood straighter. He was thirteen, tall for it, with light brown hair, hazel-green eyes, and the sturdy, sunburnt build of a boy who lived out of doors. His tunic was good blue wool with a rent in it that he plainly liked. He was grinning.

"Master Matthos," said Ser Everard. "You are late."

"I'm early for what I came for, ser." He did not say it rudely. He said it the way a hound walks into a room. And then his eyes found Orryn, and the grin widened. "So that's the cousin."

"Matthos Meadows," Osric murmured to Orryn, quickly, without turning his head. "The lord's heir." Orryn knew the name. Lord Alaric's son, and so, by the long reckoning of cousins, his own second cousin, though nothing in the face before him suggested it was a tie to be remembered.

"I heard you're frightened of geese," Matthos said.

The yard went silent. Orryn felt it go: the clack of oak stopping pair by pair, the heads turning.

"My sister Elyce told me," said Matthos. "She had it from the cook, who had it from the cat." He looked down the line of boys, and Perkin's ears, which had begun to colour at the neck, reached a deep and wonderful red. "She says there was a gander the size of a bullock and you hid in a well. Is it true you hid in a well?"

"I only told the kitchen," said Perkin, in a small voice, to the ground. "I never said a well."

Orryn said nothing, since there was nothing he could say. He felt the whole yard look at him, and the old burn of it rose in his face and neck, and he held the sword and did not drop it.

"Master Matthos," said Ser Everard.

"I only want to see what a goose-boy can do." Matthos had already crossed to the rack and taken down a sword, and he threw another underhand across the yard, so that Orryn had to let go of his own to catch it. "Three touches. Nothing above the collar. Ser can call it."

Ser Everard looked at the heir for a long moment. Orryn saw the thought go across his face and saw it decide against itself. "Three touches," said the master-at-arms, and raised his unsteady hand. "On my word. Halt means halt. Begin."

It was over in less time than the footwork had taken. Matthos was stronger than he had any right to be at thirteen, and he moved like a boy who had never in his life been afraid of being hit. He drove in on the first beat, and Orryn's weight went back on his heels, exactly as Ser Everard had said it would, and the oak rapped hard across his ribs. One to Matthos. The second came faster, a feint high and a cut low, and Orryn went for the feint and lost his knee to the cut. Two.

By the third he had stopped thinking, which was mostly because he had nothing left to think with. His body, sore and furious, made up its own mind. When Matthos came in on the same feint, Orryn did not go for it. He stepped aside from the cut instead, with a clumsy half-turn that nobody had taught him, and his sword went out and tapped the heir of Grassfield on the hip.

"Touch," said Ser Everard, with something that might have been surprise.

Matthos looked down at his hip. His grin had not gone, but it had changed, and went on changing. "One to the goose," he said. "Again."

"Halt," said Ser Everard. "That is a touch, and the bout stands at—"

The oak came round before he had finished. It struck Orryn flat across the side of the ribs, the same ribs, already sore, and for a moment he saw nothing at all. He found that he was on one knee in the dirt with his sword gone and his breath gone, and above him someone was saying, loudly and not unkindly, "Slipped! Slipped, I'm sorry, cousin. Did you not hear? Three to one."

"It was after the halt," said Ser Lucan Marwick.

He had come down off the gallery without any of them noticing. He stood at the edge of the circle with his arms still folded, looking not at Orryn but at Matthos, and his voice was perfectly flat. "Ser Everard said halt. The blow was struck after it. That is a breach, my lord's heir or no."

"I slipped."

"Then you will slip less," said Ser Lucan. He turned his head and looked, at last, at Orryn, on his knee in the dirt, and the label on the cask was still the same. "As for you, Stormlander. In this house you keep your guard up until the master says otherwise, and not a breath before. Remember it. Nobody else is going to remember it for you."

Orryn got up. It took two tries. The yard was still watching him in the early light, and the pear trees beyond the wall had gone gold at the tops. Perkin was staring at the ground. Piers Fallow was looking at Matthos, not at Orryn, with an expression Orryn could not read. Ser Everard looked tired and very old and had gone a shade paler, and was holding the pommel of his sword with both hands.

Matthos was grinning again, the broad bright grin of a boy who has been caught and finds that he does not mind. "Same time tomorrow, Goose?" he said.

What does Orryn do?

1. Answer the nickname with a laugh and take it for his own, "Goose" or whatever they like, so that it is a thing he gave them and not a thing taken from him.

2. Ask Ser Lucan, in front of the yard, to stand judge at a second bout with Matthos, a fair one, with the rules said aloud first.

3. Say nothing to Matthos, and after the lesson find Ser Everard alone and ask what it would cost to be taught properly.

4. Something else of your own.
