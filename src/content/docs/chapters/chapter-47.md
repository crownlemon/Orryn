Grassfield Keep, 208 AC

Ser Garlan Fossoway had sent Leonette a pair of gloves.

They were soft white leather, stitched about the wrists with little red apples, and had come in a wooden box lined with yellow silk. Lady Jeyne thought them very handsome. Leonette wore them once, to the sept, and afterward left them on a bench in the upper hall, where Orryn found them beneath a cushion.

He brought them to her in the gallery.

“Your gloves, my lady.”

She looked at them with something less than pleasure.

“Did you know his arms are an apple?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“A red apple. On yellow. And his name is Fossoway, which anyone can tell you. Yet he sends me gloves to remind me.”

“They're good gloves.”

“Then you may have them.”

“They wouldn't fit me.”

“Not much does, Grandison.”

She took them, though, and put them through her girdle. When Alyn came up the stair a little later, she pulled one free and held it out for him to admire. He told her she ought to send Ser Garlan a pair with a horse embroidered upon them, so that he might remember what sort of animal he rode.

Leonette laughed. She had been laughing a great deal with Alyn.

It was well into 208 AC, and the heat had outlived the old year. The mornings were cooler than they had been during Lady Odessa's visit, but by noon the yard was white with dust and the stone steps burned through thin shoes. Rook's damaged hoof had been pared twice, and Piers said the crack was closing, though he would not say the horse was sound. Orryn had taken to riding a chestnut gelding for Lord Alaric's errands. It was an honest beast with a troublesome habit of stopping to piss whenever another horse came alongside.

Alyn liked to ask whether Orryn had taught it that.

There were other things about which he had grown less talkative. He no longer spoke so readily of leaving Grassfield, although Orryn remembered perfectly well what he had said beneath the willows and in the mews. Ser Androw Ashford had offered him a place in Ser Moryn Tyrell's household, but Alyn had not said whether anything had come of it. When Perkin asked one evening if he meant to go to Highgarden, Alyn said he meant to go wherever there was good wine, soft bedding and a lord who paid his knights. Then he took Perkin's cup and drank from it.

Orryn had wanted to ask him again. He had not.

Leonette, meanwhile, was sometimes at the squires' board after supper, leaning over Alyn's shoulder to see what game they were playing, or demanding that he teach her the words of some song he knew from Longtable. She had once made him sing three verses of a particularly stupid one about a miller's daughter, then complained that he had left out the best.

Lady Jeyne had sent her away.

Afterward Leonette had come less often. Yet Orryn saw her with Alyn in the orchard, at the well, and once coming down from the upper gallery when the other girls had already gone to their chambers.

He could not have said how often they met when he did not see them.

That afternoon he was carrying a broken girth buckle to the smithy when he found them.

The cart road through the outer bailey was blocked by three men trying to turn a wagon whose rear wheel had sunk beside the granary. Rather than wait for them, Orryn went through the inner court and along the narrow path behind the herb garden. It was a shorter way to the smithy, though Lady Jeyne disliked boys using it because the gardeners left their tools there and the squires had once broken a row of young rosemary.

The little wicket between the garden and the orchard was standing open.

Orryn heard Leonette before he saw her.

“You said you would.”

“I said I might.”

“You said it twice.”

“That doesn't make it better.”

“It makes it twice as certain.”

There was laughter, then the soft thud of something against the wooden gate.

Orryn stopped.

He had meant to go straight through. Instead he stood with the buckle in his hand and listened to Alyn say something too low to hear.

“You're a dreadful liar,” Leonette answered.

“I've been told.”

“I don't think you mind.”

“No.”

Orryn moved forward enough to see through the gap.

Alyn stood with his back toward the orchard, one hand against the gatepost. Leonette was facing him, her hair loose beneath a green ribbon. She had hold of the front of his doublet, not tightly, but as though she had taken it to keep him from going away. He said something. She shook her head.

Then she pulled him down and kissed him.

Orryn felt the buckle slip in his hand. He caught it before it struck the stone.

Alyn had put his arm around her. For a moment neither moved. Then he drew back a little and Leonette reached for him again.

Orryn looked at the path behind him.

There was no one there. He could turn about and go back by the granary. The men would have moved their wagon by now. He could say he had found the garden gate shut.

He did not move.

He had seen Alyn kiss a servant girl once, behind the kitchen after dark. It had not seemed a thing worth remembering at the time. Alyn was always finding girls to kiss. The difference was that Leonette was his lord's daughter, and promised to another man, and that she was holding him as if she had no intention of letting him go.

Alyn's hand had come up to the back of her head.

Orryn could see the long fingers in her hair.

He looked down at the buckle.

“Leonette?”

Lady Jeyne's voice came from the far end of the garden.

The two by the gate sprang apart.

Orryn turned. Lady Jeyne had come along the flagged walk from the keep, her skirts gathered in one hand. Marya Caswell was several paces behind her, carrying a basket of cut lavender.

“Leonette, I have been looking for you since the noon bells.”

She stopped.

For a heartbeat nobody said anything. Orryn stood in the middle of the path with his foolish piece of iron, Alyn beyond him, Leonette against the gatepost.

Lady Jeyne looked first at her daughter, then at Alyn.

“Master Grandison,” she said, “why are you here?”

“The smithy, my lady.”

“Then why have you stopped?”

Orryn opened his mouth. He had no answer that was true and did not make matters worse.

“I was going through the garden.”

Lady Jeyne looked at the buckle in his hand.

“Of course.”

Leonette had begun to smooth her hair. She stopped when her mother looked at her.

“We were only speaking, Mother.”

“Were you?”

“Yes.”

“Then you need not have stopped when I called.”

Alyn took a step forward.

“My lady, I ought to say—”

“You ought not to say anything just now, Master Merryweather.”

He stood still.

Lady Jeyne turned to Marya.

“Go back to the house. I shall be there presently.”

Marya hesitated no more than an instant before turning. The lavender basket knocked against her skirt as she went.

Orryn wondered how much she had seen. She had been behind Lady Jeyne, and the path was narrow, and perhaps she had been looking down at the basket.

His aunt waited until Marya was beyond the turn.

“Leonette. Come here.”

Leonette did not move.

“Mother—”

“Come here.”

She came, though she passed Alyn so closely that her sleeve brushed his arm.

Lady Jeyne looked at her face.

“Have you been alone with him before?”

Leonette's chin lifted.

“I am not alone. Orryn is here.”

“Do not make me ask you again.”

“Once or twice.”

“Where?”

“Here. In the garden. Sometimes the gallery.”

“Sometimes?”

Leonette said nothing.

Alyn shifted his feet.

“My lady, if I may—”

“You may go to the small solar and wait for Lord Alaric. You will not speak to anyone on the way.”

Alyn looked at Leonette. She was staring at her mother.

“My lady.”

He bowed and went past Orryn without touching him or speaking. Orryn caught a glimpse of his face. It had gone very pale beneath the sunburn.

“Master Grandison,” said Lady Jeyne.

Orryn looked up.

“You will come with us.”

“I have to take this to—”

“The smith will see it tomorrow.”

She took Leonette by the arm, not roughly, and led her toward the keep.

Orryn followed, the buckle hanging from his fingers. When they reached the courtyard, a groom called to ask whether he had found Piers.

Lady Jeyne answered before Orryn could.

“He is attending me.”

The groom bowed and went on with his work.

No one else took notice of them. The yard was busy, the wagon had finally been turned, and a kitchen boy was chasing a hen beneath the carts.

Orryn would have given a great deal to be the kitchen boy.

The small solar lay above the buttery, with a narrow window overlooking the inner bailey. Lady Jeyne used it when the great chamber was occupied, and its walls were hung with faded green cloth that had once belonged to her mother's household at Bitterbridge.

Alyn was standing beside the window when they entered.

He had removed his sword belt. It lay upon a chair beside a plate of untouched bread.

Orryn had never seen him stand so still.

Lady Jeyne closed the door herself.

“Sit, Leonette.”

“I would rather stand.”

“Then stand.”

She took the chair opposite the window and arranged her skirts. Orryn remained by the door. He had no idea whether he was supposed to sit, and there was no chair left that did not seem to belong to someone else.

They waited.

From below came the noise of men carrying something heavy across the buttery floor. A cup broke, followed by an oath and somebody laughing. The ordinary sounds of the keep seemed extraordinarily loud.

Leonette had begun to cry. She did it quietly, with her face turned toward the window.

Alyn looked at her once and then away.

Lady Jeyne did not offer her a handkerchief.

When Alaric arrived, he was wearing his riding boots and the old brown doublet with the torn cuff. He had evidently come straight from the stables. There was a dark smear of grease across one thumb.

He looked at the four of them.

“What has happened?”

Lady Jeyne rose.

“Your daughter has been meeting Master Merryweather in secret.”

Alaric looked at Alyn.

“What sort of meeting?”

Neither answered.

Alaric took two steps into the room.

“I'm asking you, Alyn.”

“We were together in the garden, my lord.”

“Doing what?”

“Speaking.”

“Speaking?”

Alyn looked at the floor.

“And kissing, my lord.”

There was a silence.

Alaric turned toward his daughter.

“Leonette?”

She lifted her face. Her eyes were red, but she seemed to have stopped crying.

“Yes, Father.”

“How often?”

“I don't know.”

“You don't know?”

“I didn't count.”

Lady Jeyne made a small sound through her nose.

“Do not be clever, Leonette.”

“I wasn't trying to be.”

Alaric pulled a chair from the wall, turned it about and sat with his arms across its back.

“How long has this been going on?”

“Since the visit,” Leonette said.

“Which visit?”

“The Merryweathers.”

Alyn looked up.

“It wasn't so, my lord. Not then.”

“Then when?”

“Afterward.”

Leonette turned toward him.

“It was before.”

“We were speaking before.”

“We were doing more than speaking.”

Alyn said nothing.

Orryn felt his face growing hot. He had never been so glad that nobody was looking at him.

Alaric rubbed his thumb against the worn wood of the chair.

“Were you meeting her in the garden before Lady Odessa left?”

“No, my lord,” Alyn said.

“Is that the truth?”

“Yes.”

Leonette was staring at him.

Alaric looked from one to the other.

“And have you been alone together elsewhere?”

“Father!”

“Answer me.”

“In the gallery. Sometimes the orchard. We've never gone anywhere outside the keep.”

“That was not what I asked.”

Leonette's cheeks coloured.

“We haven't done anything wicked.”

Lady Jeyne stood.

“You have been meeting a young man alone while you are promised to another.”

“I haven't promised him anything.”

“Your father has.”

“Well, I haven't.”

“Enough,” Alaric said.

Leonette shut her mouth.

He turned to Orryn.

“Did you see them?”

Orryn had been hoping the question would not come.

“Yes, my lord.”

“What did you see?”

He looked at Alyn.

Alyn was watching him now. Not angrily. That might have been easier.

“They were kissing, my lord.”

“And before that?”

“I heard them speaking. I didn't hear much.”

“How long were you there?”

“Not long.”

Leonette gave a short, disbelieving laugh.

“He stood there watching us.”

“I didn't mean to.”

“Then why did you?”

“Because I didn't know what to do.”

“That's enough,” said Alaric.

Orryn lowered his eyes. He had told the truth, and wished he had been allowed to keep it.

Lady Jeyne moved around the table.

“What you have witnessed today, Master Grandison, concerns the honour of my daughter and the honour of this house. You will understand that it is not a matter for conversation.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“I mean with anyone. Not the other squires, not the servants, not the men you meet in Grassy Vale. Nor your relations at Grandview.”

Orryn looked at her.

“Even my father?”

“Especially your father,” Alaric said. “Your father would have every right to ask what has happened in my household, and I have no wish to answer him.”

Lady Jeyne came a little closer.

“Leonette is your cousin, Orryn. You have eaten at our table and been received here as family. Her good name is part of your family's good name. What touches House Meadows may touch Grandview as well.”

He had never heard her speak of Grandview quite that way. At other times she spoke of his grandmother Ellyn as though she had rescued the place from its own bad habits.

“You will tell no one what you saw,” she continued. “Not now, and not when you leave this household. Do you understand?”

“Yes, my lady.”

Alaric watched him.

“If I learn that this has become a story at the squires' board, you will answer to me. I don't care how it reaches my ears.”

Orryn could feel the buckle digging into his palm. He had forgotten he was still holding it.

“And if someone asks me why Alyn is leaving?”

“Who said he was leaving?” Alyn asked.

Alaric turned on him.

“I did.”

Alyn stared.

“You're eighteen,” Alaric said. “You've asked to go often enough. Well, you shall go.”

“My lord, I—”

“You've served me well in the yard, and badly in my house. I trusted you with my horses and my weapons and the company of my children. I ought not to have needed to tell you which of those things you might take liberties with.”

Alyn looked down.

“No, my lord.”

“You will be knighted before you leave. I had thought to do it when the year's riding was finished, but it may as well be done now.”

Orryn looked up sharply.

Alyn seemed equally surprised.

“My lord?”

“You've the skill for it. Ser Lucan says so, and I have watched you fight. I shan't keep a grown man in service merely because he has been a fool.”

Lady Jeyne had returned to her chair.

“When?”

“Tomorrow or the day after. When Lorimer can have the sept ready.”

“And he leaves?”

“The next morning.”

Leonette came away from the window.

“You cannot.”

Alaric looked at her.

“I can.”

“He hasn't done anything that I haven't!”

“Then you should be grateful that I have no means of sending you back to your father.”

“I don't want him sent away.”

“That is plain enough.”

She turned toward her mother.

“Say something!”

Lady Jeyne folded her hands.

“I have been saying things all afternoon, Leonette. You haven't listened.”

“Because neither of you listens to me!”

Alaric stood.

“Then say what you want, and let us hear it.”

Leonette looked at Alyn.

It was the first time since Alaric had entered that she seemed uncertain what to say.

“I don't want to marry Garlan.”

Lady Jeyne closed her eyes briefly.

“We know.”

“I don't think you do.”

“You've told me often enough.”

“He's old.”

“He is five-and-twenty.”

“He's dull.”

“He is a good man.”

“He speaks to me as if I were Elyce.”

“Then perhaps you should stop behaving as she does.”

Leonette flinched.

Her mother went on.

“Ser Garlan is a knight of one of the principal houses of the Reach. He is honourable, well regarded, and possessed of sufficient means to maintain you in comfort. You will be treated with respect at Cider Hall.”

“I don't want to live at Cider Hall.”

“Then you ought to have told us before the agreement was made.”

“I did!”

“You told me you disliked his dancing.”

“Because I did!”

“You told me he was tedious.”

“He is!”

“You told me you did not like his beard, though the poor man had scarcely grown one. Every objection you have offered has been of that sort.”

Leonette's face twisted.

“Would you have listened if I'd said anything else?”

Lady Jeyne did not answer at once.

“Perhaps not,” she said. “But you might have tried.”

Leonette turned away.

Orryn thought of Wylla Merryweather and her expensive gowns, and Ser Androw bending from the saddle to speak to her. Wylla had seemed perfectly satisfied with the arrangement made for her. He had wondered what anyone could see in the girl.

Now Leonette was standing in front of him, beautiful and furious, and he could not quite understand why a marriage that seemed so fortunate to everyone else should make her so unhappy.

Yet he remembered the little twig she had worn on her finger during the hunt.

He remembered Alyn making it.

“I want Alyn,” Leonette said.

Nobody spoke.

Alaric looked at Alyn.

“And what do you want?”

Alyn's expression changed. He seemed to take some time considering his answer.

“I have great affection for Lady Leonette, my lord.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“I know.”

“Do you intend to ask for her hand?”

Alyn glanced toward Leonette.

Orryn saw her watching him.

“My lord, I have no lands. My father has not settled anything upon me, and my brother will have Longtable. I haven't even my spurs yet.”

“You will have them in two days.”

“Then I shall have my spurs, my lord.”

It was a sensible answer, Orryn thought. He could have given it himself.

Leonette did not seem to think so.

“You could ask,” she said.

Alyn looked at her.

“Leonette—”

“You could!”

“Your father has already given his word.”

“He might release me.”

Alyn looked toward Alaric.

The lord of Grassfield had gone very still.

“Do you propose that I break an agreement with House Fossoway because my daughter has been kissing my squire?”

“No, my lord.”

“Then I suggest you cease speaking of it.”

Lady Jeyne took Leonette's hand.

“You will come with me. We shall speak again when you are calmer.”

“I don't want to be calm.”

“That, too, is plain.”

Leonette pulled her hand free.

For a moment Orryn thought she might strike her mother. Instead she turned and went to the door.

She stopped before Orryn, who was standing in her way.

“Move.”

He moved.

She went out without looking back.

Lady Jeyne followed her, but paused in the doorway.

“Alaric.”

He looked up.

“Not a word beyond this room.”

“I know.”

“And Orryn?”

“He has heard me.”

“Then he should hear me once more.”

She looked directly at him.

“Your cousin's honour, Orryn. Remember that.”

The door closed behind her.

Alaric stood for some time looking at it.

Then he picked up Alyn's sword belt from the chair.

“Take this.”

Alyn obeyed.

“You will present yourself to Ser Lucan at dawn. He will see that your equipment is fit for the ceremony. Your own sword will do if the edge is sound.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And you will not seek out my daughter.”

“No, my lord.”

“Nor send her messages by anyone in this household.”

Alyn looked at Orryn.

“I won't.”

Alaric nodded once.

“Good. Now get out of my sight.”

Alyn bowed and went.

Orryn remained.

Alaric looked at him.

“What is it?”

“Nothing, my lord.”

“Then why are you still here?”

“You told me to stay.”

Alaric stared at him for a moment, then gave a short laugh without much pleasure in it.

“So I did.”

He took the broken buckle from Orryn's hand and examined it.

“What's wrong with this?”

“The pin's bent.”

“Then why haven't you taken it to the smith?”

Orryn looked at the door.

Alaric looked at it too.

“Seven hells. Go.”

By supper everyone knew that Alyn was to be knighted.

Nobody appeared to know why it had been decided so suddenly. Perkin said Lord Alaric had received some great favour from Longtable, and Baldric said that if it were a favour there would be a feast. Piers thought Alyn had been offered service at Highgarden and would need his spurs before taking it.

Alyn himself said very little.

He ate at the squires' board as usual, though there was a place for him at the high table if he chose to take it. He laughed when Perkin asked whether he would have to call him ser after the ceremony.

“You'll have to call me ser now.”

“You're not a knight yet.”

“Then practise.”

Perkin bowed so deeply that his forehead struck the table.

Osric said that a man ought to spend the night before his knighting in prayer.

“Then you may spend it for me,” Alyn answered.

“I should be pleased to pray for you.”

“Good. I'll sleep.”

Some of the boys laughed. Osric did not.

Orryn watched Alyn break bread and pass the larger piece to Perkin, who had dropped his own into his cup.

He had been trying not to look at him all evening.

Leonette did not come to supper. Lady Jeyne told Elyce that her sister was unwell, and Elyce asked whether she had the sweating sickness. Her mother said she had not. Elyce wanted to know how Lady Jeyne could be certain without asking Maester Perrin.

Lord Alaric told her to finish her meat.

Later, while the others were arguing about whether a knight could be made without a night-long vigil, Alyn leaned close to Orryn.

“You were there before her, weren't you?”

Orryn looked at him.

“Yes.”

“I thought so.”

Alyn took a drink.

“Why didn't you say anything?”

“I didn't know you were there until I heard you.”

“You could have called out.”

“I know.”

Alyn set down his cup.

“It's done now.”

Orryn looked at the table.

“Are you angry?”

“With you?”

He shook his head.

“No.”

That was worse than Orryn had expected. He had thought Alyn would blame him. He had almost prepared an answer.

Alyn leaned back and stretched his legs beneath the table.

“Don't look so miserable, Grandview. I'm getting my spurs.”

“But you're leaving.”

“I was leaving anyway.”

“I know.”

“So what's the trouble?”

Orryn could not answer.

Alyn looked at him for a moment, then reached across and took a piece of meat from his plate.

“You weren't eating that.”

Orryn had been.

He said nothing.

Leonette was allowed into the herb garden the following afternoon.

Orryn saw her from the stable yard. Marya Caswell walked beside her with a basket on her arm, talking about something that required a great deal of explanation. Leonette listened with the expression she wore when Septa Lenora lectured her on modesty.

At the corner of the garden Marya stopped to speak with one of the gardeners.

Leonette came toward Orryn.

“Master Grandison.”

He bowed.

“My lady.”

She looked over her shoulder.

“Are you going to tell me you're sorry?”

“I don't know what for.”

“For standing there.”

“I didn't mean to find you.”

“But you did.”

“Yes.”

“And now he's going.”

Orryn looked toward the stable. Rook was standing with his head over the lower door, watching a groom carry a bucket past.

“He was going to leave,” Orryn said.

Leonette looked at him sharply.

“What?”

“He told me he meant to go.”

“When?”

“A while ago.”

“How long?”

Orryn hesitated.

“Before your mother came.”

“Before she came to Grassfield?”

“Yes.”

Leonette stared at him.

“And did he say where?”

“He talked about Highgarden.”

“Highgarden.”

She repeated the word as though she disliked its taste.

“He never told me that.”

Orryn said nothing.

Leonette turned toward the garden.

“He told me he would like to see the sea.”

Orryn looked up.

“The sea?”

“He's never been. Not properly. He said he would take me one day.”

Orryn remembered Alyn speaking of the Arbor's ships and the great roads south of Highgarden, and the times he had made grand promises to Perkin about taking him hunting, or drinking, or riding. Some he had kept. Others had simply been forgotten.

“Did he say when?” Orryn asked.

Leonette gave him an angry look.

“No.”

He wished he had not asked.

She walked a few steps away, then came back.

“Do you think he loves me?”

Orryn felt his throat tighten.

“I don't know.”

“That's a foolish answer.”

“It's the truth.”

She looked at him.

“Have you ever loved anyone?”

Orryn's face grew warm.

“No.”

Leonette laughed, though there was little pleasure in it.

“Then why should I ask you?”

She turned toward the garden, but stopped once more.

“I know what they all think. Mother says Garlan is kind. Father says he is a good knight. Septa Lenora says I shall have a fine household and children of my own, as though every girl in the Reach ought to be grateful for the chance to sit in someone else's chair.”

She tugged at the ribbon on her wrist.

“Garlan talks about horses. He asks whether I have been riding, and when I say yes he asks whether I enjoyed it. He asks whether I have been reading, and when I say no he tells me I ought to. He doesn't know what to say to me.”

“Perhaps he will when you're married.”

“Perhaps I shall die before then. Wouldn't that be convenient?”

Orryn stared at her.

“Don't say that.”

“Why? Would you miss me?”

“Yes.”

She looked surprised.

Then she laughed.

“You hardly know me.”

“I've lived here nearly a year.”

“And spent most of it following Alyn about.”

Orryn looked away.

Leonette watched him for a moment.

“Don't be cross. I didn't mean it unkindly.”

He said nothing.

She pulled the ribbon free and began winding it about her finger.

“I don't hate Garlan,” she said. “I hate that everyone is so pleased with him. If he were cruel, or ugly, or an old man with no teeth, perhaps somebody would understand.”

“He's not old.”

“He might as well be. He does everything as his father did it, and means to do it until he dies.”

She looked toward the orchard.

“Alyn makes me laugh.”

Orryn thought of the pear Alyn had thrown him outside the stable, the water he had fetched for his bleeding tongue, and all the times he had made Orryn laugh when he had meant to be angry.

“Yes,” he said.

Leonette was quiet.

Then she asked, “Did Mother make you swear?”

“No.”

“She told you not to speak?”

“Yes.”

“And Father?”

“Him too.”

“Then I suppose I may trust you.”

Orryn looked at her.

She seemed to consider him for the first time that afternoon.

“I wish you hadn't been there,” she said.

“So do I.”

Marya called from the garden.

“Leonette!”

She turned.

“I must go.”

“My lady.”

Leonette took a step, then came back and caught his sleeve.

“If he speaks to you before he leaves, tell him I didn't mean what I said.”

“What did you say?”

She let go of his sleeve.

“Never mind.”

She went back toward Marya, walking quickly enough that the older woman had to turn and follow.

Orryn stood beside the stable door. He had been entrusted with something and had not been told what it was.

He hoped Alyn would not ask him about it.

The knighting took place in the sept at dawn.

There was no great feast, no tourney, and no crowd of visiting knights. Lord Alaric had called his household together, and Ser Lucan Marwick stood beside the altar with Septon Lorimer. The septon had put on his best robes, though the hem had been mended with thread that did not quite match the cloth.

Alyn wore a clean white tunic beneath his sword belt.

Orryn had helped him dress.

It had been an unpleasant business. Alyn's old belt was too worn to sit properly over the new tunic, and one of the buckles had to be changed. Piers had offered his own, but Alyn said it would make him look like a merchant's son. Piers told him that at least a merchant's son could afford a decent belt.

Alyn laughed and accepted it.

Now he stood before the altar with his hands folded.

Orryn had expected him to look proud. Instead he seemed very tired.

Lord Alaric wore his best dark green doublet and carried his sword without a scabbard. The household knights stood on one side of the sept, the squires on the other. Matthos was beside his father, wearing the new cloak Lady Jeyne had ordered for him.

Leonette was not present.

Lady Jeyne stood with Elyce and Marya near the rear wall. She watched the ceremony without expression.

Septon Lorimer spoke of the Seven, of the duty of a knight to defend the innocent, and of the honour that came with service. He spoke for some time. The sept was warm, and Orryn could hear a fly moving against the window.

Alyn knelt.

Lord Alaric drew his sword.

“Alyn of House Merryweather,” he said, “you have served me as squire and have been found worthy to bear the arms and duties of a knight. Will you defend those who cannot defend themselves, keep faith with your lord and king, and bear your honour as carefully as your sword?”

“I will, my lord.”

The sword touched his right shoulder.

“In the name of the Warrior, be brave.”

It touched the left.

“In the name of the Father, be just.”

Alaric spoke the remaining charges, invoking the Mother, the Maiden and the other faces of the Seven. His voice did not change. When he had finished, he lowered the sword.

“Rise, Ser Alyn Merryweather.”

Alyn rose.

For a moment nobody moved.

Then Ser Lucan stepped forward and embraced him, as knights did. Ser Tobin followed, then Ser Bertram. Alaric returned his sword to its scabbard and gave Alyn a short clasp of the arm.

“Well done, ser.”

Alyn bowed.

“My lord.”

The others came forward.

Perkin was first among the squires. He had been waiting with his hands clenched at his sides and now threw both arms around Alyn.

“Ser Alyn!”

“Mind the sword.”

“You've got one now!”

“I've had one for years, you fool.”

Perkin laughed.

Baldric bowed with great solemnity, though his ears were red. Piers offered his hand, and Alyn took it.

“Now you can pay for your own wine,” Piers said.

“Not if you keep buying it for me.”

Osric congratulated him and said he hoped the Seven would guide his service.

Alyn thanked him properly.

Orryn stood a little behind the others.

When Alyn reached him, he held out his hand.

“Well, Grandview?”

Orryn took it.

“Congratulations, ser.”

Alyn laughed.

“Seven save me. Not you as well.”

“You told Perkin to practise.”

“I don't remember telling you.”

He pulled Orryn into a brief embrace, one arm around his shoulders, and struck him lightly between the shoulder blades.

Orryn caught the smell of clean wool and the leather oil Alyn had rubbed into his belt the night before.

Then Alyn let him go.

“You'll have to get someone else to show you where the wine is kept,” he said.

“I know where it is.”

“Then you're ahead of half the knights in the Reach.”

He moved on.

The septon had begun speaking to Lady Jeyne. Lord Alaric was already at the door with Ser Lucan, discussing something about the horses.

The ceremony was over.

Orryn looked toward the place where Leonette might have stood.

There was no one there.

*

Alyn left the following morning.

His belongings filled two bags and a small chest. He had more clothes than Orryn expected and fewer useful things. There were two pairs of boots, one nearly new and one fit for little beyond a dung heap, a broken silver cup he said had belonged to his grandfather, three belts, several shirts and a pair of gloves that were not his.

He found the gloves at the bottom of the chest.

“Whose are these?”

Orryn looked.

They were soft brown leather, much too small for Alyn.

“I don't know.”

“Nor do I.”

Alyn tossed them onto the bed.

“Somebody will want them.”

He went back to packing.

Orryn sat on the edge of his own bed and watched.

The other squires had already gone down to the yard. Perkin was helping with the horses, and Baldric had promised to see that the girths were properly fastened. Osric had gone to the sept.

Alyn pulled a shirt from beneath the mattress.

“There's another one somewhere.”

“Under your pillow.”

Alyn looked.

“So there is.”

He held it up to the light.

“Gods, that's filthy.”

“It has been there for weeks.”

“Then it's had plenty of time to get clean.”

He threw it into the bag.

Orryn tried to laugh.

Alyn looked at him.

“You've been quiet.”

“I haven't anything to say.”

“That has never stopped Perkin.”

He sat beside Orryn.

For a while neither spoke.

Then Alyn said, “I didn't think he'd do it so quickly.”

“Knight you?”

“Send me away.”

“You said you wanted to go.”

“I did.”

He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees.

“I thought I'd have a choice of where.”

“Will you go to Highgarden?”

“Perhaps. Ser Androw's offer was a place for a man who might still have been a squire when he came. I'm a knight now, which is better in some ways and worse in others.”

“Why worse?”

“A knight ought to have a horse and a sword and someone willing to pay him. A squire can usually count on the last.”

“You have a horse.”

“My father has horses. I have one I'm taking home.”

He smiled faintly.

“Unless Mother decides it belongs in Longtable's stable.”

Orryn looked down at his hands.

“Are you going to marry Leonette?”

Alyn turned his head.

“Seven hells, Grandview.”

“I only asked.”

“Why?”

Orryn could not say why.

Alyn was quiet for a time.

“She's a pretty girl,” he said at last.

“Yes.”

“And she's good company.”

“Yes.”

“And she's promised to Fossoway.”

Orryn nodded.

“I don't know what she'll do.”

“Neither do I.”

Alyn reached for one of his boots and began rubbing a mark off the leather with his thumb.

“Her mother won't forgive me. Nor should she, I suppose.”

“You could have asked for her.”

Alyn looked up.

“Asked whom?”

“Leonette.”

“Of her father?”

“Yes.”

“I could have asked for the Starry Sept and half the Arbor. He'd have given me the same answer.”

“Perhaps.”

“No perhaps about it.”

He looked at the boot.

“Leonette thinks a great deal is possible if she wants it hard enough. That's one of the things I like about her.”

Orryn was silent.

Alyn put down the boot.

“She'll be all right.”

Orryn thought of Leonette winding the ribbon round her finger.

“How do you know?”

“I don't.”

Alyn got up.

“I hope she will be.”

He lifted the last bag.

“Come on. If I stay any longer Perkin will put something foul in my saddle.”

They went down together.

*

The whole household had gathered to see him off.

Lord Alaric stood beside the mounting block with Ser Lucan and Ser Tobin. Lady Jeyne was on the steps, Elyce beside her. Matthos had brought his wooden sword and was showing Piers a cut he said he had learned from Alyn.

Leonette was not there.

Alyn greeted everyone in turn. He thanked Ser Lucan for his teaching, shook Ser Tobin's hand and embraced Perkin, who was already crying. When Piers offered him a small leather purse for the road, Alyn protested that it was far too generous. Piers said it was less generous than the money Alyn had borrowed from him over the past year.

Alyn took it.

Lord Alaric came forward.

“You know the road to Longtable.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And you know what I said about the place at Highgarden.”

Alyn nodded.

“I do.”

“Then don't put my name to anything I haven't promised.”

“I won't.”

Alaric looked at him for a moment.

“You served me well in many things, Alyn.”

“Thank you, my lord.”

“I hope you serve your next master better in all of them.”

Alyn bowed.

“I shall try.”

Lady Jeyne stepped down from the stairs.

She offered her hand. Alyn knelt and kissed it.

“May the Seven keep you, Ser Alyn.”

“And you, my lady.”

“Give my regards to Lady Odessa.”

“I will.”

Her face remained composed.

“If your mother asks after us, tell her we are well.”

Alyn rose.

“Of course.”

Elyce wanted to know whether he would come back.

Alyn crouched so they were almost level.

“Perhaps one day.”

“Before my nameday?”

“I don't know when that is.”

“You do!”

“I've forgotten.”

She struck him lightly on the shoulder.

“You always forget.”

He laughed and kissed her forehead.

Matthos came forward.

“I'll be a better knight than you.”

“I expect you will.”

“And I'll have better armour.”

“You'll need it.”

Matthos frowned, unsure whether he had been insulted.

Alyn mounted.

He looked once toward the upper windows.

Orryn looked too.

There was no movement behind the shutters.

Then Alyn gathered the reins.

“Well, Grandview.”

Orryn stood beside the horse.

“Ser.”

“You'll have to keep them in order.”

“Who?”

“The idiots upstairs.”

“You were the worst.”

“Then it'll be easy.”

Alyn grinned.

Orryn wanted to ask whether he would write. He wanted to say that the squires' chamber would be different, that he would miss the noise Alyn made coming up the stairs, the wine under the bench, the foolish songs and the way Alyn could make a bad afternoon seem worth laughing about.

Instead he said, “Safe roads.”

Alyn leaned down and clasped his forearm.

“You too, Grandview.”

Then he rode through the gate.

Perkin ran after him as far as the bridge, calling something that made Alyn laugh. The sound carried back through the open arch.

Orryn watched until the horse disappeared beyond the bend.

He did not look up at Leonette's window again.

*

The next day Lord Alaric sent for him.

Orryn found him in the stable yard, standing beside the chestnut gelding and arguing with Piers about a saddle. The lord had the animal's head in one hand and was pulling at the girth with the other.

“It's too long.”

“It was made for the bay, my lord.”

“Then why is it on this one?”

“Ser Wyman said—”

“Ser Wyman can explain it when he comes.”

He saw Orryn.

“Grandison. Come here.”

Orryn obeyed.

“Have you ever been to Bitterbridge?”

“No, my lord.”

“Highgarden?”

“No.”

“Then you'll see both.”

Orryn looked at him.

“My lord?”

“We ride in a few days. Matthos comes with us. He is to be presented to Ser Moryn Tyrell, and I have business of my own with Lord Leo's household.”

Orryn had heard the journey discussed before, but had thought little of it. Alaric had spoken of taking Matthos and Alyn, with Verrall or one of the household knights if the road required them.

“Alyn was to go,” Orryn said.

“Alyn has gone.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And I still require a squire.”

Orryn felt something quick and bright in his chest.

“I'm to come with you?”

“Yes. Unless you have other plans.”

“No, my lord.”

“Good. You'll see that the horses are ready. Ask Ser Wyman which beasts can make the journey. I don't want to be halfway to Bitterbridge with a lame gelding and two boys who think the Seven will carry them.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And don't take Rook. Not until Piers is satisfied with that hoof.”

Orryn nodded.

Alaric released the horse and wiped his hand against his breeches.

“You'll need decent clothes. Speak to Marya. She'll know what Lady Jeyne means to send with us.”

He started away, then turned.

“One other thing.”

Orryn waited.

“What happened in my garden stays in my garden. You'll meet men at Highgarden who know Alyn's family. Some will ask after him, and perhaps after my daughters. You may tell them he was knighted and has gone home. Both are true.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Anything more is none of their concern.”

He looked hard at Orryn.

“You understand me?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He went toward the smithy.

Orryn stood beside the chestnut gelding. Piers was still adjusting the saddle, muttering about men who could tell a horse was poorly fitted without knowing how a buckle worked.

“Highgarden,” he said, when Orryn came to help him. “You'll see more gold in a day than we have in this whole keep.”

“Have you been?”

“Once. With my father.”

“What was it like?”

Piers considered.

“Very large. And very expensive.”

He pulled at the girth.

“Hold that.”

Orryn held it.

From above came the sound of a shutter being opened.

He looked up.

Leonette stood at her chamber window in a pale green gown. Her hair was loose, and for a moment he thought she might call down to him.

She did not.

She looked toward the road beyond the gate, then drew the shutter halfway closed and disappeared.

Piers pulled the girth tight.

“Now,” he said, “does that look better?”

Orryn looked at the saddle.

“Yes.”

“Then fetch the other strap.”

He went to fetch it.

In a few days he would ride to Bitterbridge and Highgarden, where great lords kept their courts and the sons of House Tyrell gathered men about them. He had never seen Highgarden, though he had heard of its walls and gardens all his life. He wondered what sort of horse Lord Leo Tyrell rode, and whether his knights really wore gilded armour, as Perkin claimed.

He would have liked to ask Alyn.

The thought came so readily that for a moment he turned toward the stable door, expecting to find him there.

But Alyn's horse was gone, and the place where his saddle had hung was empty.

Orryn took down the strap Piers wanted and carried it into the sunlight.
