He let the cart get a furlong ahead before he followed it.

Rook did not mind. The dun had a long, easy, rocking walk that ate the road without seeming to, and the morning was cool and clear, with a mist lying in the hollows of the orchards like milk in a saucer. The pear trees went by in their rows, heavy and bowed, and every so often a windfall burst under a hoof with a sweet ripe smell that made Orryn's empty stomach turn over. He thought of the bread. He put the thought away.

Then he touched his heels to the dun's sides and drew level with the cart.

Wat did not turn his head. The reins lay loose in his hands, and the mule plodded on as if it had made up its mind about the day. Pate sat on the board beside him, looking at the road between the mule's ears. Neither spoke. They had not spoken, Orryn thought, for the whole of a mile; it was how they were, these two, and he had grown used to it the way one grows used to the sound of a mill.

"Wat," he said. "I want to tell you something."

"Aye, Master Orryn."

"It is about what Ser Wystan said to me last night. You heard some of it. I do not know how much." He kept his eyes on the mule's ears too. It was easier. "I would rather you had it from me than made it up out of pieces."

For a moment there was only the creak of the axle. Then Wat said, in his soft level voice, "I'll not make it up. But I'd hear it."

So Orryn told him.

He told it as exactly as he could, which was how he told everything. That the old knight had been Gawen Grandison's sworn man. That Gawen had waited nine days at Grandview, after Roland Penrose rode in with forty men. That two letters had gone to Storm's End, and no answer had come back, so men said. That Alester had wanted to ride on the second day and had shouted so at the high table. That Steffon had stood at the foot of the stair and counted. And last, because it was the thing he had been given and the thing he did not understand, that one letter had been burned and one kept, and that the burned one had not gone out of Grandview, but come in.

When he had done, he found his mouth was dry. He had said more in the last quarter hour than in the whole of the day before, and it did not feel like a thing he had given away. It felt like a thing he had set down on a table between them, in the open, where either of them could look.

Wat drove for a while without speaking. A rook went over, flapping slowly, and Pate's eyes followed it.

"Forty men," Wat said at last.

"So he said."

"Nine days."

"Yes."

"Was he drunk when he said it?"

Orryn considered lying, and found that he could not make it fit. "Yes," he said. "He was. I do not know how much of it was the wine. I told you what he said. I do not say it is so."

Something happened at the corner of Wat's mouth. It was not a smile; it was only a thing that happened. "That's what his lordship will want," he said. "That last. I'll write it down as it was told, and that he was in drink, and that the young master gave it so." He glanced sideways, at last, a quick weighing look, the look of a man checking a coin for its ring. "You didn't have to."

"You would have heard it anyway."

"Aye. I'd have heard a bit of it." He clicked his tongue at the mule. "I'd have put down the bit, and that you'd said nothing of it. His lordship'd have seen that for himself. He sees what a man leaves off."

The cart rolled on. Orryn sat very still on the dun, feeling something loosen in his shoulders that he had not known was clenched.

"What is he like?" he said. It came out smaller than he meant. "Lord Alaric. I have a letter from him and a few lines in my father's hand about him, and Maris says he is cold and exact. That is all anyone has told me."

Wat was quiet so long that Orryn thought the question had been refused. The orchards thinned, and the road dipped toward a stream with a stone bridge, and a boy was driving geese across it with a stick.

"His lordship doesn't ask what a thing meant," Wat said. "He asks what it was. A man comes to him and says, my lord, the miller cheated me. He doesn't say how, or why would he. He says, how much did you give him, and how much did you get? And then he sits and thinks about the sum."

"That is how I would want to be asked."

"Aye, I thought it might be." There was no flattery in it. Wat turned the mule onto the bridge with a small movement of the wrist. "He's not cold. People say cold, because he doesn't show. He's the sort that counts a thing because he can't say it. Pate's mother was his nurse's sister. When she died he paid for the burying, and he never said, and nobody was to say it neither." The reins moved a little in his hands. "I'll tell you that for nothing, Master Orryn. It's not in any book."

Beside him, Pate looked at the road, and his ears went red.

Orryn said nothing for a time. The planks of the bridge boomed under the cart; the water ran fast and brown beneath, and the geese went hissing up the bank. He thought of Pate in the stable doorway with his big hands, and of the sentence that Wat had read out at dawn, nothing owed, and it seemed to him for the first time that he had only seen the surface of this cart, and that under it there were things that did not get written down at all.

"There was a blanket in that stable that was not the inn's," Wat said, to the road. "I never saw it."

Pate did not look at either of them.

And that was all. They crossed the bridge.

The land rose in the afternoon, gently, in long swells of stubble and pasture, with hedgerows lopped square and a mill standing on a rise against the sky with its sails turning very slowly in the wind. Cattle stood in the shade of the oaks. A woman in a field straightened with her hand to her back and watched them go by, and Orryn raised his hand to her, and after a moment, she raised hers. He felt the day get longer, and the sun lean toward the west, and the thing at the end of it come nearer. Wat said little more, but twice he pointed with his chin at a landmark and gave it a name: Hob's Cross, where the road to Highgarden went off to the right; the Lamb's Stile, which was a thing nobody had ever explained to him. Orryn repeated the names under his breath, as if he were carrying them.

At the top of the last rise Wat drew the mule to a stop, and the cart's creaking quieted, and for a moment there was only wind in the stubble.

"That's it," he said.

Orryn had seen a great many keeps in his fourteen years, though not many that he had looked at as a place he must live. Grassfield sat in the fold of the valley below, low and broad and grey, with a round tower at one corner and a curtain wall that had been mended more than once in different stone. Smoke went up from three chimneys. A river curled around its foot, thin and bright as a wire, and on the slope above the wall, in rows so even they might have been drawn with a rule, stood the orchards, hundreds of trees, every one the same height as the next.

It was beautiful. It was also, he thought, a place where nothing would ever be allowed to be late.

"He'll be in the yard," Wat said quietly, "or at the window of the upper room. He'll have had word we'd come by dusk. He's never once not known." He lifted the reins and did not shake them. "You'll want to settle how you go in."

What does Orryn do?

1. Ride in ahead of the cart, alone, and present himself to Lord Alaric before Wat can speak.

2. Go in with the cart, beside Wat, and let the book be read in front of him.

3. Ask Wat what Lord Alaric is likely to ask first, and use the last mile to settle his answers.

4. Something else of your own.
