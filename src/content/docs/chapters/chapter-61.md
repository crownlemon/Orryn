Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

The wedding rushes still lay on the floor of the great hall two days after the Footlys rode out, because nobody could spare the water to wash them off the boards. Orryn crossed them with a cushion under one arm and a cup in the other hand, and the dry herbs cracked under his boots and gave up the last of their smell: spilled wine, trodden thyme, and under it something sourer that the guests had left behind. A kitchen girl was scraping candle-wax from a bench, and the small dry noise of it followed him to the stair like a mouse in a wall.

Marya Caswell was waiting at the foot. She was Lady Jeyne’s cousin from the poor side of the Caswells, neat and plain, who had been given a place at Grassfield as companion and kept it by never being in the way. She looked at the cushion and not at him.

“Lady Jeyne is on the wall. She wants that, and she will not thank you for it, and she must not be told she wants it.”

“What am I to say?”

“Nothing, Master Grandison. That is why I am sending you.”

The wall-walk above the gatehouse was the only place in Grassfield where a breeze ever came, and today it did not. The heat stood on the stone like a hand. From the head of the stair Orryn could see the whole of Grassy Vale laid out brown and yellow under a white sky: the orchards grey with dust, the brook a pale scar in its bed, the lower mill with its wheel hanging still.

Lady Jeyne and Lady Brenna stood together at the far angle, and he stopped where he was, because he could see that he was not wanted nearer.

Lady Brenna Tyrell was Lord Leo’s youngest daughter, Lord Armond Caswell’s second wife, and so Lady Jeyne’s stepmother, though she was scarcely older than her stepdaughter. She was slight and brisk, in a gown the colour of dry sage, with the Tyrell rose in gold thread at her sleeve and a fixed smile that seemed to have been pinned there at her dressing. She was talking, in the quick light way he remembered from Bitterbridge, her hands moving a little at her sides as though she were measuring something out for a customer. Lady Jeyne did not answer. She stood with one hand flat on the parapet and her back so straight that the let-out seams of her bodice pulled, and she did not once turn her head. The water in the cup grew warm against Orryn’s fingers.

When it was done, it was done quickly. Lady Brenna kissed her stepdaughter on the cheek, a small bright social kiss, and came back along the wall with her skirts in one hand, like a woman who has carried a full dish a long way and not spilled a drop.

“Master Grandison. You carry that as if it were a relic.”

“Lady Marya sent it, my lady.”

“Of course she did.” Something moved at one corner of the smile and was put away. “Tell my stepdaughter I shall not trouble her again before we ride. She has a great deal to think on, and all of it good.”

He heard her heels all the way to the bottom.

Lady Jeyne had not moved. He crossed the stones to her as he would have crossed a yard to a horse that had been struck. “My lady. Lady Marya sent—”

“I know what Marya sent.” She took the cushion and did not sit on it. She held it against her belly in both hands, looking out at the white brook and the grey trees, and Orryn saw that her brown eyes were doing exactly what her mouth was doing, which he had never seen before and did not like. “Where is my husband?”

“At the mill-pool, my lady. With Lord Armond.”

“Of course he is.” She said it to the orchard. “Fetch them. I want them in the solar, and Lyman Verrall, and Septa Lenora, and my cousin, and Ser Bertram. Tell them I do not care if they are wet.” She turned her head at last. “And you will stand wherever my husband stands, Master Grandison. You are not to bring me anything while I speak.”

He bowed, and ran.

*

The brook lane was white to the ankle, and by the time Orryn reached the mill-pool he had a stitch in his side and a mouth like a stable floor. Lord Alaric stood on the clay bank in his shirtsleeves with his hair on end, pointing his stick at the sluice and explaining it, in the fond quick way he explained anything he owned. Beside him Lord Armond Caswell, Lady Jeyne’s father, stood with his big hands behind his back and his white moustaches drooping, nodding at the sluice as though he had never seen one.

Armond saw Orryn first. He watched him come down the bank at a run, and looked at the boy’s face, and after it, slowly, at the sky, like a man who has been expecting a toothache and has just felt it start.

“She’s told her.”

“Told her what?” Alaric had turned. “Grandison?”

“Lady Jeyne wants you in the solar, my lord. Both of you. And Master Verrall, and the septa, and Ser Bertram.” He got his breath. “She said she did not care if you were wet.”

“Wet,” said Alaric, looking at the pool, where there was nothing but clay. “Armond?”

“Come up, lad. You’ll want your feet under you.” Armond did not look at him. “It’s hers to say.”

Alaric looked at his goodfather a long moment with the stick still in his hand. He was thirty, and not a stupid man, and Orryn saw him begin to understand that he was walking up the hill toward something his goodfather had known about for some time.

*

The solar was built for four and made to hold ten, with the shutters open on the dust and no candles, because there was light enough. Lady Jeyne sat in the carved chair by the window with the cushion behind her back at last. Marya stood at her elbow. Septa Lenora, narrow and straight, with grey hair under a plain veil and a crystal at her breast, had her needlework in her lap and did not touch it. Lyman Verrall, the steward, sat nearest the light with ink on his cuffs and his smile ready. Ser Bertram Corliss, a household knight, lean and grizzled, who had not smiled in Orryn’s hearing since Lord Gawen died, stood against the wall with his arms folded. Lord Armond stayed in the doorway, like a man who might still be wanted elsewhere.

Orryn stood at Alaric’s shoulder and held one hand in the other.

“I shall say this once,” said Lady Jeyne, “and I shall say it here, where it cannot be said afterward in other words. Lady Brenna has brought me news from Lady Arlanna. She begged to be the one to bring it. She was most particular.” She said it pleasantly. “Lady Arlanna has found a husband for Elyce. His name is Harys Oakheart. He is Lord of Old Oak, and he is thirteen.”

Verrall’s face lit. “Old Oak! My lady, that is—”

“Master Verrall.”

He stopped. The smile, which Orryn had never once seen go out, went on by itself for a moment before it understood.

Orryn knew Old Oak as one of the great houses of the Reach, whose lords had sat at Highgarden’s right hand since before Highgarden was a Tyrell’s. He knew that its lady was Lord Leo’s eldest daughter, Ceryse, and that she was a widow. He had not known there was a son.

“It is a very old house,” said Lady Jeyne. “Brenna tells me its lords trace themselves back to Garth Greenhand. She did not tell me that its lord died in the rebellion, and that the house stood divided in it, and that the king has not forgotten. She did not need to. The boy was taken from his mother when it was over and given to Lord Rowan to raise at Goldengrove. He is held there for the good conduct of his house. His mother governs Old Oak in his name, and his father’s brother sits in the same hall and quarrels with her over every sack of barley. That is the husband. That is the house.”

“Jeyne,” said Lord Armond from the door.

“I have not finished, Father.”

She had not raised her voice. Orryn had begun to think she never would.

“I did not ask for this. I want that understood in this room, because it will be told otherwise. My husband asked Lady Arlanna for a husband for Elyce, at Highgarden, before I had given him leave, and I said I would not ask a second time, and I have not. And Lady Arlanna has answered the question I never put, through the one person at Highgarden who is bound to carry it to me smiling. Two women who have never once asked what I wanted for my daughter have decided it between them, and sent me the one to tell me it is an honour. I am to be grateful. I am to say yes in my own solar, with my father in the door.”

“I asked for a proper husband,” said Alaric. “I never—”

“Proper,” said Ser Bertram, from the wall.

He said it flatly, to nobody, as he might have put down a coin he did not trust. Alaric turned his head and looked at him. Lady Jeyne did not.

“Yes,” she said. “Proper.” She smoothed the cushion behind her. “There are houses within three days’ ride that stood for King Daeron from the first day and kept on standing, and would have been glad of Elyce if I had been let to ask them. I should not have had to explain her husband’s father to anyone. Now she is to be a great lady with a very old name, and every lord in the Reach will look at her and see a loyal girl put inside a rebel house to make it look forgiven. Her children will be Oakhearts, with a king’s eye on them. She would outrank Leonette in any hall in the Reach, and be pitied for it.”

“It is a great house, Jeyne,” Armond said. His voice had gone rough. “After the Tyrells and the Hightowers there’s none greater in the Reach—”

“How long have you known?”

He did not pretend. “Since the new moon.”

“You sat at my table at Leonette’s wedding.”

“I’d not have you refuse in front of the Footlys.”

“No,” said Lady Jeyne. “You would not have me refuse at all.”

Outside, a long way off, a hammer was going on someone’s roof, slow and stupid.

Alaric had been standing with his stick in his hand and his face going from red to something paler. When he spoke it was not what Orryn expected.

“I owe your father,” he said. “And Lady Brenna. In the year of Redgrass they pressed me to choose Daeron, and I did, and I have been glad of it since. I do not say that to excuse it, Jeyne. I say that they will think I owe them this, and they will not be wholly wrong.” He rubbed his jaw. “And I have seen what it is to be a boy held in another man’s hall for his father’s oath. I carried out the award that took the mill from Graves, and Graves gave a boy for it. Edmund. He sits at Dunmere Hall and holds Ser Cedric’s stirrup.” He looked at the floor. “Elyce would be married to that.”

“A ward is the guardian’s to give,” he said, after a moment, as though he had found it lying there. “The boy is at Goldengrove. Has anyone asked Lord Rowan?”

“Lord Leo put him there,” said Armond. “Lord Leo may speak for him.”

“Lord Rowan sat at my wife’s table three days and spoke of every well in the Reach but one. If Highgarden is marrying off his ward over his head, it is Cress Hill again, with a girl in it.”

“I do not care whose ward he is, Alaric,” said Lady Jeyne.

She did not look at him. She looked at the cushion, and then, briefly, out of the window, where in the yard below a small figure in a green kirtle was trying to get a bridle onto a big bay colt that had plainly decided otherwise.

“I care whose daughter.”

Orryn watched Elyce lose the bridle, and pick it up, and begin again, with her tongue between her teeth. She did not know. By supper the kitchen would have it, and she would know everything, and none of it would be in the right order.

“Then it is this,” said Alaric.

He set the stick against the wall.

“Nothing is promised. Not a word, not a nod. No betrothal while the boy is anyone’s hostage, and I will have that from Lord Leo’s own mouth, and from the king if Lord Leo must go to the king for it. Armond. You ride for Goldengrove, as you meant to. You will look at the boy, and you will tell Jeyne what you saw, all of it, and not what Brenna would have you say.”

“I’ll tell her what I see.” Armond looked at his daughter. “You’ll not like it.”

“I like very little today, Father.”

“And Lord Rowan will be asked,” said Alaric, “not told.”

“Elyce hears none of this from the kitchen,” said Lady Jeyne. “I will tell her myself, when I know what it is I am telling.” She shifted against the cushion. “And, my lord. You asked first.”

“I did.”

“Do not ask first again.”

“No,” said Alaric. After a moment, he said it again. “No.”

Nobody spoke. He turned, and found Orryn at his shoulder.

“The cart goes tomorrow,” he said. “Lord Osmund’s grain. I gave my word to give it and guard it through Grassy Vale, and I will not have Highgarden say that Meadows sulked. Grandison, you and Haye. Cobb and two men. Hob’s Cross, and whoever Highgarden has sent.”

“Under whose seal?” said Lady Jeyne.

“Mine. My wax on the tailboard. It is Grassfield’s grain, and it goes into the Reach’s share, to be let out in the open. It is not Ser Garth’s, and it is not Ser Moryn’s.”

“Then see that it can be seen,” she said, and turned her face to the window.

Joffrey Caswell sat on the bench under the beech in the yard with his lute case across his knees, not playing, and Orryn had meant to walk past him.

“Has she told them?”

“Told who what?”

“Don’t.” Joffrey did not look up. He was fifteen, Lady Brenna’s son and Lord Armond’s heir, thin and sandy, with rosin on his sleeve and a long nose burned red. “You’ve a face like a plate.” He turned the case a quarter turn. “He’s my cousin, you know. Harys. His mother and mine are sisters.”

“Have you met him?”

“Twice. The first time he told me Old Oak was older than Bitterbridge. The second he asked who had paid for my coat.” Joffrey almost smiled. “He talks about his father as if he’d known him. He was a babe in arms. And he says his mother is a Tyrell who forgot she was ever a wife, and she writes him every moon, and he does not answer.”

Orryn thought of several kind things to say, and remembered what Joffrey’s mother said about kindness afterward, and said none of them.

“Good,” said Joffrey, and looked at the gate. “Go and find your sergeant.”

*

The cart left Grassfield before the sun cleared the orchard, which was the only hour left in the day when a man could stand a horse without being sick of it. It was a long wicker-sided cart behind a pair of heavy greys, carrying eighteen sacks of barley from Grassfield’s own granary, which Lyman Verrall had counted twice the night before and Lady Jeyne once after him. On the tailboard hung a strip of leather, and on the leather a blob of brown wax pressed with Lord Alaric’s ring. Orryn had watched him do it in the yard by lantern-light, thumb and ring and a sharp little grunt, like a man stamping a loaf.

Sergeant Cobb walked at the head, a broad slow grey man with a squint and a spear he used as a staff. The two men-at-arms, Alf and Lem, had been given the cart as either a punishment or a holiday and had not yet decided which. Orryn rode Rook, the Penrose dun, and since the wedding he was aware of the horse under him in a way he had not been before, as though it had been a loan and was now a letter. Baldric Haye rode beside him on a borrowed grey with a loose shoe.

Baldric was sixteen, a head taller than Orryn and twice as broad through the chest, with a square pale face and flat brown hair and a way of looking at a thing before he spoke as though he were adding it up. He was Ser Bertram’s squire. His father was a knight with a small holding so poor that the best thing the family owned was a mill that would not pay, and Baldric had been looking at Orryn since the yard with a face Orryn knew very well. It was the face of a boy who was waiting.

He waited as far as the stone bridge.

“Old Oak,” he said.

“What of it?”

“Piers Fallow’s cook has a niece in Lady Jeyne’s kitchen. It was all over Grassy Vale before the Caswells had their boots on.” He looked ahead at the cart. “A rebel’s name on a lord’s daughter. I’d have said no.”

“Nobody has said anything.”

“Verrall’s face did it.” Baldric shrugged, and the borrowed grey shook its head at the flies. “It’s all one to me, mind. But if it came with Old Oak I’d take the rebel’s whole family and be glad of them. Some of us would marry a gibbet for a keep.”

“You can’t say that about Lady Elyce.”

“I said it about me.” He gave a short laugh. “My father has six horses and four of them pull the plough. When I have my spurs I’ll have a sword and a mill. You’ll have an uncle at Storm’s End to write to, and somebody will find you a place with a good table. I’ll have to be taken. That’s the difference, Grandison. It isn’t that you’re better. It’s that you get asked.”

Orryn did not answer. He thought it was not true, and could not have said why, and thought that if he began to say why Baldric would hear a boast.

“Have you asked him?” said Baldric.

There was no need to say about what. They had read the lease together, in the lower solar with the shutters closed: twenty-five years to Dunstan Fallow rent free, and the toll to him until his repairs were paid, and one clause that said the men of Eaglegrave should grind there free as of old, and nowhere a line to say who bore the loss.

“He’s had other things.”

“He always has other things.” Baldric did not raise his voice. He never did, which was why Orryn disliked arguing with him. “He was pleased for a moon, since the babe. You could have asked him for the mill itself, in the yard, while he was kissing her. Now it’s Old Oak and his wife in a rage, and he wants the squires out of the way. That’s when a lord gives anything. When he’s glad to be rid of you.” He looked sidelong. “It can’t be me. It’s his award.”

“He made the award,” said Orryn. “That’s why it’s hard.”

“It’s why it has to be him.”

It was a fair answer, and the one that gave Orryn the least room. He did not say he would, and did not say he would not, and rode on with the sun on the back of his neck and the feeling of having been handed something small and heavy and told that he need not look at it yet.

Villagers came out along the road, as they always did when a cart passed, and today none of them wanted to be seen watching. At the well by the Lamb’s Stile an old man looked at the wax on the tailboard and at Cobb’s spear and said loudly, to nobody, “That’s going out.”

“Highgarden,” said Cobb, not turning his head.

“Highgarden’s got a hundred barns!”

Orryn drew breath to say that it was going into the Reach’s share, to be let out in the open and counted. Baldric’s hand closed on his rein.

“Don’t,” he said, very low. “Don’t explain it to them. You’ll lose them.”

Behind them somebody spat. It was a small dry sound, and it followed the cart a long way.

*

The Hedgefort bridge was the last on the Grassy Vale road before the Highgarden country, a low humped thing of grey stone over a stream that was a thread of green between two banks of cracked clay. A pole lay across it on two trestles, and behind the pole a tall florid man sat a fat grey cob in the middle of the road, with two toll-men at his stirrup and a moustache like a pair of horns. He was already talking when they came in sight, and he did not stop for the half mile it took them to reach him.

“—dust to the knee, I tell you, and I said to the man, I said, if you think I shall hold the left for Lord Leo’s convenience—Halt! Halt in the king’s name, or Lord Alaric’s, or mine! Whose cart?”

“Grassfield’s, ser,” said Cobb.

Ser Dudley Bushy was fifty-two, and held Hedgefort of Lord Alaric: a hall, a hedge and a bridge. He was the best storyteller in Grassy Vale. At Grassfield they said that when Ser Dudley told you a thing you divided it by three, and that when he told you a thing about Redgrass you divided it by ten. Orryn had met him twice, both times across a hall, and been told about the left both times.

“Grassfield’s, is it.” Ser Dudley leaned out of the saddle until his belly met the pommel and peered at the leather strip on the tailboard like a man reading a bill of fare. “Meadows wax. I know that thumb. Which of you is the Grandison?”

Orryn said that he was.

“Then it’s you I’ll ask. What does she carry, and where’s she bound?”

“Barley, ser. Eighteen sacks. To Hob’s Cross, for Highgarden. It goes into the Reach’s share, to be let out in the open.”

“In the open.” Ser Dudley sat back. “That’s Garth’s word. I’ve heard it in three halls this moon: grain over every bridge in the Reach, free as air. It’s a fine word. It isn’t mine, lad. It’s my bridge. A silver stag for a laden cart and a measure to the bridge-wardens, as it was in my father’s day, and I’ve a hedge to lay and a hall to thatch. I’ll tell you something else. I’ve not had a cart across it this dry that wasn’t on Meadows’ errand or the king’s.”

“Ser,” said Baldric. It was the flat carrying voice he used to recite. “With respect. A lord’s cart under a lord’s seal goes free across his own vassals’ bridges. It’s the custom in Grassy Vale. Ser Bertram says it was so under Lord Gawen, and Lord Gawen’s father. You hold Hedgefort of Lord Alaric. You would be taking toll of your lord.”

“Would I?” said Ser Dudley, delighted. “On his errand, aye. But whose errand is this? It’s Meadows corn on a Meadows road, and it’s bound for Highgarden to be handed round for nothing. A man who gives a thing away isn’t on his own errand, boy. He’s on someone else’s.”

“It’s his grain. His seal. His—”

“And my bridge.”

Baldric had the next point ready; Orryn could see it in his face, and the one after that. He would be at it till dark, and be right, perhaps, in a court, with a lord to hear him. Ser Dudley saw it too, and settled in the saddle like a man who has been offered a good dinner.

Orryn had not been told what to do about tolls. Lord Alaric had said wax, and open, and seen. He had said nothing of silver. Cobb had the purse for the road, and Cobb was looking at the pole and not at either of them, with the face of a man who has been a sergeant a long time and means to be blamed for nothing. Orryn thought of the carter on the Bitterbridge road with the green wax in his hand, paying a man who would not read it, and the cart half a day late. He thought of the stag, which was one stag, and of the hall where they were to sleep, which was Ser Dudley’s. He thought, too, that Baldric was probably right, and that being right at a vassal’s bridge in front of his toll-men was how a vassal came to talk about you at every table in Grassy Vale.

“Pay it, Cobb,” he said.

“It isn’t owed,” said Baldric.

“I know.”

“Then why—”

“It’s one stag.”

“It’s every cart after,” said Baldric, but low, and he did not say it again.

Cobb counted the coin into a toll-man’s palm like a man handing over a tooth. The pole went up. Ser Dudley, who had been robbed of an argument and paid for it, took the loss very well. He turned his cob and fell in beside the cart, and had Orryn’s ear before the pole was down behind them.

“You’ll have heard of the Marches, I suppose.”

Orryn said he had not.

“Not heard! By the Seven.” Ser Dudley lowered his voice for the benefit of the toll-men, the hedge and the next parish. “A drover came through five days since, from the Starpike road, in a cattle-buyer’s hat and not a beast to his name. Forty Dornishmen, he says, on horses that drink sand. Out of the Red Mountains in the dark. A Tarly steading burnt to the ground, and the cattle driven off, and a woman—” He shook his head. “Sixty, I should say, by the way he told it. He said forty, but a man says forty when he is afraid to say more.”

“Last year it was a Dornish army at Blackcrown,” said Cobb, to the greys, “and it was a goat.”

“This drover was a serious man.”

“He had a hat,” said Cobb.

Orryn did not know what to think. He looked at Alf and Lem behind the tailboard, who had stopped talking, and at the dry fields on either hand, and he could not tell whether it was a thing that had happened or a thing Ser Dudley had made out of a hat.

“If it’s true they’ll call the levy,” said Baldric, when Ser Dudley had ridden ahead to shout at his own gate. He said it differently now, in the voice of a boy who has heard of something that might be a chance. “Ser Bertram would have to go. He’d take me. I’d have my spurs before the rains, if there are rains.”

“If it’s true.”

“A Tarly barn. Eleven beasts or forty. It’s all one if they hold a levy.” He seemed to hear himself, and shrugged. “Or it’s a hat.”

*

Hedgefort was a thorn hedge before it was anything else: a ring of hawthorn and blackthorn thirty feet through, laid and woven by somebody’s great-grandfather and kept ever since, brown and brittle this year, and inside it a long stone hall with a stable that was better than the hall. Over the hearth hung a bull’s head as big as a barrel. Ser Dudley’s wife met them at the door. Lady Joan Bushy was a short woman of eight and forty with a sharp little mouth and keys at her belt, who looked at the two of them as she might have looked at two sacks that had arrived without a docket.

“They may have the loft,” she said. “The hall is for people who tell lies, and my husband has first claim.”

“She loves me,” said Ser Dudley.

“Sit down, Dudley.”

They supped on bread and cold pork and a dish of onions, with Ser Dudley’s two sons and a hound under the table, and by the second cup he was at Redgrass. “The left,” he said. “Four hours, and the dust—”

“You held the baggage, Dudley,” said Lady Joan, buttering bread.

“The rear of the left, Joan, which is the front of the baggage.” He beamed at Orryn as though the point had been conceded. “And Dornishmen! The lad hadn’t heard.”

“A drover in a hat,” said Lady Joan, “and no cattle. I would sooner have believed him with a cow.” She put down her knife. “Your brother rode for Daemon, Dudley, and you have told stories about Dornishmen for twenty years so as not to tell that one.”

For the first time all evening Ser Dudley said nothing, and the hound came out from under the table to see why. Nobody took it up. Orryn ate his onions.

*

The loft smelled of old straw and mice. They lay on their backs in the dark with the lantern out, and heard the horses shifting below, and the long dry whisper of the wind in the hedge.

“You paid it,” said Baldric.

“It was a stag.”

“It was Meadows paying Bushy for the privilege of being Meadows. Every carter on this road will hear of it by the new moon, and Dudley will tell it better than it was.” He turned over. “I’d have kept at him. I’d have had it off him in another quarter-hour.”

“Probably.”

“Don’t agree with me, Grandison. It’s worse.”

Orryn lay quiet. After a time Baldric said, in a different voice, “I’ll have my spurs from Bertram, if the sour old bastard lives to give them. Then any lord that pays. Peake, if Peake paid.”

“Peake rose for Daemon.”

“And Haye rose for nobody and got a mill that eats us. I’ll take a rebel’s silver. It spends.” He said it without heat. “What’s yours, then? Your father’s a Stormlander in the Reach’s pay. You’ll want a side.”

Orryn was silent. He did not know what was true of Grandview that he could say in the dark, and he knew what Baldric would do with it if he said it badly.

“I serve Lord Alaric,” he said.

“That’s not a side. That’s a place.” Baldric yawned. “Did you look at Footly’s maids at the wedding? The tall one.”

“No.”

“You never look. You never say who you fancy. Alyn used to say you’d a face like a girl and manners like a septon.”

Orryn felt his ears go hot in the dark. “The dairymaid at Grassfield. The one with the red hair.”

“Maud’s cousin? She’d eat you.” Baldric laughed, pleased, and Orryn heard the straw rustle as he propped himself on an elbow. “I’ll tell you what a girl is, since nobody has. A girl is a matter of asking twice. The first time they say no because they’re meant to. Ask again, and walk her to the mill after the shearing, and—” He made a small wet noise with his tongue. “Three since I was fourteen. Four, if you count the miller’s widow, and I do.”

“Four,” said Orryn.

“Three and a widow. Don’t make me say it twice, I’ll lose count.”

“Which was the best?”

“The widow. She knew what she wanted and said so, and she didn’t cry after, or ask me to marry her. The rest of them look at you as though you’d taken the roof off their house.” He lay back. “And you? Not even a kiss?”

“Not yet.”

“Not yet. By the Seven. You’re fourteen and you talk like ten.” He was pleased, and not unkind. “You’ll get a wife who’ll eat you, you know. They always do, the ones who’ve not practised. Come to me when you’re ready and I’ll tell you which of the dairymaids will say yes at the first asking.” He yawned, hugely. “For a price.”

Orryn laughed, because it was expected, and in a little while Baldric’s breathing changed.

Orryn lay a long time with his eyes open. He tried the thought of the red-haired dairymaid, and it was a pleasant thought, like a warm room he had no wish to go into. He liked girls. He liked Elyce, and his sister, and the maids in the Grassfield kitchen who gave him the burnt end of the bread; he liked them as he liked a good horse or a clever friend. But when the others spoke of what they wanted of them, something in him went quietly out of the room and waited in the passage until the talk was over. He had noticed it before, at Grandview, and put it away. He did not know why it should be so, and it seemed a thing like the shape of his own nose, which there was no use asking about.

He thought of a bench in a gallery at Bitterbridge, and a shoulder against his own, and was glad of the dark.

*

Hob’s Cross was nothing more than the place where two dry roads met, with a squat grey stone at the middle and a cross cut into its face by somebody with a poor chisel and a great deal of time. A few elms stood about a well with the bucket gone, their lower limbs dead, and in the thin shade of them, drawn up in a line along the Highgarden road, were seven carts painted green.

There were two spearmen to a cart, in Tyrell green, with the look of men who had been standing a long time. Two of them wore a cluster of grapes stitched on the green. Before the first cart a knight sat a tall bay as straight as a lance, and he turned his head as the Grassfield cart came up the road, and Orryn saw that he had only one eye.

The other was a patch of boiled leather, cracked at the edge like an old boot. The one that was left was a pale hard blue, and it went from Cobb to Baldric to Orryn to the cart, to the wax on the tailboard and back, and missed nothing. He was sixty or near it, with a long jaw and no humour in his face, and his surcoat had been mended so often that Orryn could not tell what device it had once borne.

“Names.”

“Baldric Haye, ser, squire to Ser Bertram Corliss of Grassfield.”

“Orryn Grandison, ser. Squire to Lord Alaric Meadows.”

“Grandison.” The pale eye rested on him. “A Stormlands house. A Stormlander brings a Reach lord’s grain to the Highgarden road.”

“I serve Lord Alaric, ser,” said Orryn, and felt his face go hot. “He sent me.”

The old knight regarded this as a man regards a paper he suspects of forgery, and found, apparently, that it would pass. “I am Ser Robyn Rhysling.”

“Whose man are you, ser?” said Baldric, who feared nothing that could be put as a plain question.

“I am sent.”

It was all he said. Orryn looked at the grapes on the two spearmen, and the green on the rest, and decided that it meant Highgarden, or the lord seneschal, Ser Arron Redwyne, who was said to write every cart down; and that in the Reach these were very nearly the same thing and very nearly not. He found that he did not much want to know.

“A gift is given in the open,” said Ser Robyn, “and heard. Of whom, what, to whom, and before whom. You will say it. Haye.”

Baldric stood very straight, and Orryn saw he had been drilled in this by Ser Bertram, in a yard, with a stick. “From Alaric Meadows, lord of Grassfield. Eighteen sacks of barley. To the Reach’s share at Highgarden, to be let out in the open. Before you, ser, and the road.”

“Good. Grandison. You carry his wax. Say whose it is.”

“Lord Alaric’s, ser. He said to say it goes to the Reach’s share. Not Ser Garth’s, and not Ser Moryn’s.”

It was exactly what Alaric had said in the solar, and it came out as plainly as a bucket coming up a well. The old knight did not move at all.

“A gift is not given against anyone, boy,” said Ser Robyn Rhysling. “A man who says what a gift is not, at a lord’s cross, in front of a lord’s spears, has made it a quarrel, and a quarrel is not grain. Say it again.”

Orryn’s mouth was dry. Behind Ser Robyn the spearmen with the grapes had gone very still. “From Lord Alaric Meadows,” he said. “To the Reach’s share. In the open.”

“Better. It will be written as said.” Two of his spearmen came down to the cart and began, with a great deal of unnecessary care, to look at every sack. Ser Robyn leaned over and studied the wax on the leather tag, and when he straightened there was something in his face that was not quite approval. “A lord who seals what he gives. It is rare enough to be noticed. Has the cart paid toll on the road?”

“A stag at Hedgefort, ser,” said Baldric, before Orryn could speak. “Ser Dudley Bushy’s bridge. It wasn’t owed. By the custom of Grassy Vale—”

“It is written.” Ser Robyn did not ask what the custom was. He took a small book from inside his surcoat and wrote in it, in a thick slow hand, and Orryn watched the pen go down and understood that his stag had become a line at Highgarden, to be read by someone who had not stood on the bridge. He did not know what the line would be used for. He thought it would be used for something.

“Ser,” said Baldric. “Ser Dudley Bushy told us on the road there’s been a raid. In the Marches.”

“I carry no rumours,” said Ser Robyn Rhysling.

“No, ser.”

That was all. The Grassfield cart was led around to the tail of the line and made the eighth. Ser Robyn raised one hand without turning, and the first green cart creaked, and then the second, and the line began to move down the Highgarden road in a long slow rope of dust, with the spears walking beside it and the old knight riding stiffly at the head.

Alf and Lem sat down in the shade of the dead elms and took off their boots, and agreed that it had been a holiday.

“That’s all it is, you know,” said Baldric. He sat his borrowed grey with his flat hair stuck to his forehead, looking down the road. “Carts. They go where they’re pushed.”

“And what are we?”

“The ones who walk beside.” He turned the horse. He did not say anything about the mill, and did not need to. He rode ahead on the Grassfield road, and Orryn stood a moment longer at the grey stone with the cross cut into it, with an old man at a well and a girl with a bridle and a rebel’s boy at Goldengrove all weighing on him in an order he could not settle.

Then he got up on Rook, and followed.
