Orryn did not dismount.

He knew what he ought to do. He ought to slide down, tie the dun to the rail beside that evil mule, and cross the yard with his hat in his hand and tell the old man the whole account of the ford, Ser Osmund and the rope, Ser Jon and the slip of paper, in order and without hurry. He had the slip in his doublet. He could have taken it out and held it up. But the single eye that had fastened on the horse was the same eye that had looked at him on the Grandview stair, and some cold small part of him, the part that had been nine years old at a miller's door, had heard Osmund won't lend that horse to his own mother and drawn the plain conclusion. He was a boy of fourteen on a lord's horse, a mile from any witness, and an old knight with a sword on the bench was asking how he came by it.

"Ser Wystan," he said. His voice was steadier than he expected. "Why did Grandview wait?"

It was not what he had meant to say. It was what came out, as the thing nearest to hand, the way a man being chased throws the heaviest object he can reach. He heard it hang in the evening air. Somewhere in the inn a woman dropped a pot, and swore. The mule lifted its head.

Ser Wystan said nothing at all.

He sat as he had been sitting, upright, with his two hands folded on the head of his staff and his chin up, and the good blue eye on Orryn's face. The clouded one looked a little to the left, at the road. His mouth had gone thin and pale under the white stubble. In that long silence Orryn understood two things at once: that the question had struck something, and that it was too late to take it back. The dun shifted under him. He found his own hand tight on the rein.

"Who's been talking?" said the old man at last.

"No one, Ser. That is—" He wanted to say Ser Osmund and did not. "A man at the ford. He made a joke of it. He said that my lord Penrose called Grandview prudent."

"Prudent." Ser Wystan tasted the word. A long wheeze came out of him, and it took Orryn a moment to see that it was a laugh. It built slowly, like a pot coming to the boil, until the whole spare frame of him was shaking on the bench and the jug rocked and slopped at his elbow. "Prudent! Seven's teeth! The Penrose said that, did he? To you? On Gawen's own road?" He wiped the corner of his good eye with a knuckle. "Oh, it's well put. I'd have said it myself if I had the wit. Get down off that horse, boy. You'll do yourself a hurt, sitting like a tin pot on a post. Get down and have the bench. I'll have the rest of it from you after. First I'll answer you."

Orryn got down. His knees were not quite his own. He knotted Rook's reins to the rail, a decent distance from the mule, and came across the yard and sat at the far end of the bench, as far as the boards allowed, and put his hands between his knees. The old man smelled of horse and sour wine and a clean, dry, ancient smell underneath, like linen kept in a chest. The sun lay red on the whitewash.

"I wasn't at Redgrass," said Ser Wystan.

He said it flatly, to the road. "Gawen wasn't. That's the shame of it, and that's the reason of it, and you'll hear one without the other all your life. I'll give you both, and you'll make of them what you can."

"Yes, Ser."

"Eleven years since. Spring. A hard cold spring, I mind, with the frost still on the oats in the field." He drank, and put the cup down with a click. "The raven came to Grandview from Storm's End and said Daemon Blackfyre had called his banners. And the Penrose was in the yard before the bird was cold. Roland, the father of those two lads. Rode in with forty men and said, Gawen, the king calls. Gawen sat on his chair in the hall. He listened. He said, I'll think on it." The good eye glittered. "He thought on it for nine days."

"Nine?"

"Nine. I counted, being a man who has little to do. And all that time the Grandview men sat in the yard with their saddles on their knees, sharpening what had already been sharpened, and the whole of the valley waiting to know whose banner it'd be. Your uncle Alester wanted to ride on the second day. A tall lad, brave as a boar, with more courage than sense, and he shouted it at the high table in front of the whole household. Ride, Father, for the king. Gawen told him to hold his tongue." He grunted. "Alester has not forgotten. You'll have noticed."

Orryn had not noticed, in so many words. But he thought of a tall man in a green doublet, laughing too late at a joke, and it fitted.

"And your father stood at the foot of the stair," said Ser Wystan, "with a face like a closed shutter, and said nothing. He was counting. He's good at it. He counted the horses, and the oats, and the men, and the cost of a bad guess. And at the end of the ninth day the word came from the south, that the king had won at Redgrass and the Blackfyre was dead and his sons fled. And Gawen stood up from his chair, and sent for the cook, and called for dinner." The old man put his head back against the whitewash. "That's one of it. We did not ride because he thought, and by the time he had done thinking there was nothing left to ride for. The Penroses had gone and bled and been thanked. And Grandview had kept its men, its oats and its good name, and not one of those was worth the half of what a man might call honour."

"That's the shame," said Orryn.

"That's the shame." He seemed almost to enjoy it. "And here is the reason, which some will tell you was cowardice and some will tell you was cunning, and the Seven alone know which. Hear me. I'm saying what I know." He shifted his staff. "On the first day, Gawen sent a man to Storm's End with a letter. On the second he sent another. Neither came back with an answer, and he waited for one, boy, as a drowning man waits for a rope. He would not move without word from his liege lord, and no word came."

"Lord Edric didn't answer?"

"I don't say that. I say no answer came to Grandview in nine days. I carried the second letter myself." The cloudy eye turned, slowly, to look at Orryn, as though it could see something the other could not. "I was a stronger man then, and rode a good deal faster. I rode to the gate of Storm's End and gave it to the porter, and I rode home again, and I'll tell you now what was in it, which is naught, and I'll tell you what happened to the answer, which is more than I've said to any man living."

He stopped.

The yard had grown quiet. A hen went across it, and the mule scratched its ear against the rail. From the inn, faintly, came the smell of frying onions. Orryn sat very still, and found that he was leaning toward the old man, and that his fear of the dun and the theft and the sword had gone out of him like water out of a cup.

"I'll tell it you when I'm dead," said Ser Wystan.

It took Orryn a moment to understand. Then the old man began to laugh again, silently this time, shaking, and slapped his knee.

"That's a jest, boy. Look at your face. Seven's teeth, you've Steffon's mouth. Do you think I'm such a fool as to tell a story like that on an inn bench to a lad I met yesterday?" He wheezed and wiped his eye. "There's an answer that never came, and a man who knows why, and he isn't in a hurry. That's what you take west with you. You want to know why Grandview waited? A letter, and what came back, and a lord who burned the one and kept the other. And the rest of it is mine." He sat up. "And now. The dun."

So he had not forgotten. Orryn felt the old fear rise up again, a little thinner, but there. "It was lent to me, Ser. At Sedgeford. By Ser Jon Penrose, for his lady mother. I have it written." He fumbled the slip from his doublet and held it out. His hand was not quite steady.

Ser Wystan took it between thumb and forefinger. He did not read it. He held it at arm's length, squinted at the lines with his one good eye, sniffed it, and gave it back.

"Penrose gold," he said. "Of course it was. They'd lend the sun if you wrote it down." He grunted. "You'd have been hanged for a thief, in my day. A boy on a lord's best horse, a mile from help. I had my hand on my sword when you rode in, if you want to know. You were lucky you asked the right question." He took up the jug, and found it empty, and looked at it with the sorrow of a man betrayed. "Be easy. I'm not about to cut you down. I was never fond of Osmund Sedgley."

It was meant kindly. Orryn knew that. He sat with the slip in his lap, with the sun red on his hands, and felt the cold go out of his stomach by slow degrees, and a warm, light, ridiculous relief come in behind it. He had been right to be afraid. He had also been right to ask. He was not sure that the two were as separate as he had thought.

He heard the cart before he saw it: the creak of the axle, the iron-shod wheels on the stony road, and a dry, patient voice saying something to a horse. Then it turned in at the yard gate, with the grey in the shafts and Wat's long brown face above it, and Pate beside him with the whip across his knees. Wat drew up. He looked at the mule, and at the old knight, and at the dun tied at the rail, and at Orryn, in that order.

He said nothing. He set the brake. But Orryn, who had begun to read the small movements of that long, silent face, saw his gaze rest for a moment on the empty space on the bench between the boy and the sword, and understood that it had all been counted.

What does Orryn do?

1. Go to Wat at once and own it. Say you rode ahead without leave, and that you found Ser Wystan here. It is the honest thing. It costs you some pride, and Wat can tell it to Alaric in the way that is plainest to him.

2. Press Ser Wystan about the letter. You have a minute before Wat reaches the door. Ask what was in it, and what came back. He said he would tell you when he was dead. He may be drunk enough to say more, and he may be angry that you pushed.

3. Invite Ser Wystan to supper. Ask him to eat with you and the grooms. It is courtesy to a Grandview man and a way to hear more. But the purse is Wat's, and Alaric's coin was for the road, not for feeding old knights.

4. Go and arrange the rooms with the innkeeper. See to stalls and beds before the dark comes, as you did at Grandview. You have no money, so you must ask Wat to pay, and that gives him something else to carry to Alaric.

5. Say nothing of what Ser Wystan told you. Keep the story to yourself for now. Tell neither Wat nor your father. You hold a secret about your own house, and you decide who hears it and when.
