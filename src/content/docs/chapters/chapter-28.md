Lyman Verrall kept his accounts in a narrow room off the steward's hall, with one window, one candle, and shelves of ledgers bound in calfskin that went up to the rafters like the wall of a very dull cliff. He looked at Orryn's green hands, and his stained doublet, and the bast-cut groove in his thumb, and put down his pen.

"Grafting," he said, "by the look of you. Tull does that to everyone he likes." He moved a stool with his foot. "Sit. Tell it in order. You're good at that, I'm told."

Orryn told it in order. The east block and the fifty-two rows; the canker in Maud Pyke's oldest, four trees with the heart gone and six with rust; the wind off the brook, and eleven rows below. He told what Tull had said of the cost of cutting and the cost of waiting, and what he had answered, and how Tull had said it was a good thought, and how the bread had been missing from it. Verrall wrote as he talked, quickly, in a small clear hand, and when Orryn reached his four hundred and ninety baskets he stopped him with a finger. "Seventy, and seven years. Good. And the price of a basket?"

"I don't know it."

"Two pence at the Hob's Cross market in a fair year, a little less in a glut, a little more in a lean. So call it a thousand pence. Eighteen stags, near enough." The pen moved. "Meal for the two of them, salt and tallow, wood, the thatch mended once. It comes to something like thirty over the seven. Fifty stags, with what you'd pay the son by the day to grub and plant. And I would put the wage on the orchard account and not on the household, so that it is seen to be wages." He looked up. "That is not nothing. It is not a ruin either. It is the price of a good deal of peace in the vale."

"Is it too much?"

"Too much for what? For the row, no. For his lordship's temper, that depends on who tells him first." Verrall tapped the pen on the blotter. He did it without any particular expression. "Tolman Haverill is a decent man, as such men go. He is also afraid, and has been as long as I have known him, and it has made him a poor reeve and a worse messenger. I would not be Master Haverill tonight. I would very much rather be you." He rose. "He will ask three questions in a row, and he will want the answer to the one he asks. Tell him all of it, but tell him in the order of his questions. And tell him the names. He likes names."

Lord Alaric was in the solar, as he seemed to be at every hour that was not the yard, with the candles lit and the shutters open on a purple sky. He listened to the whole of it without writing a word, which Orryn had not seen before and found more frightening than the pen. When Orryn said Maud Pyke he said, "I know the woman. Her husband carried my father's bier." When Orryn said Col, he said, "The lame one. He keeps my nursery." And at Hugh Tull's name he said nothing, but the left foot, which had been still, began to tap again, very slowly, the way a man counts the strokes of a bell.

"Tull told the reeve in the spring," Lord Alaric said, when Orryn had finished.

"So Tull said, my lord."

"And the reeve told me nothing."

"No, my lord."

He reached out and rang a small hand-bell on the table. The sound was thin and cold. A page appeared at the door, the same truthful child with the crooked badge, and was given a name and sent away at a run.

It was a long wait. Lord Alaric did not speak, or look at him, and Orryn sat on the stool with his green hands in his lap and thought about what he had done. He had been asked to bring the thing in words, and he had; and every word had been true, and every word had been a nail. He thought of Tull sitting on his upturned basket, watching the sky. Mind what you do with it, young master. It occurred to him that Tull had known precisely what he would do with it.

Tolman Haverill came in out of breath, and straightened his sleeves at the door. He was forty, thin and nervous, with receding brown hair and quick eyes that went round the room and found Orryn and Verrall's empty chair and Lord Alaric's face, in that order, and stopped. He was a younger son of some house of no importance, Orryn had been told, and looked it: the kind of man who had been in the right place all his life and never once in charge of it. "My lord. You sent—"

"The east block, Master Haverill."

The sleeves were straightened again. "My lord?"

"When did Hugh Tull tell you of the canker?"

There was a silence in which Orryn could hear the candle. "In the—in the spring, my lord. In the spring. I meant to bring it, I wished to be sure of the count before I troubled you. Tull is an old man and he sees blight in every—"

"You counted for five months."

"My lord, I—"

"What is the count?"

Haverill's mouth opened. His eyes went to the window, to the floor. He did not know it. Orryn saw that he did not know it, and saw him understand that Lord Alaric had seen, and then Lord Alaric turned his head, quite slowly, and said, "Master Orryn. The count."

He had not been warned of that. He had thought, absurdly, that the thing would be done to Haverill, and Orryn would sit by and be ashamed of it; he had not thought that he would be asked to hold the knife. But a question had been put to him, and the question was plain. "Four trees with the heart gone, my lord," he said. "Six with rust on the leaf. Eleven rows downwind. It was two trees last spring."

Tolman Haverill looked at him. It was not a look of anger, which Orryn could have borne. It was something closer to wonder, and under it a kind of weary grief, as of a man who has been hit by a child and cannot tell the child it hurts.

"You will bring me bad news the day it is born," said Lord Alaric. "Not when you have counted it. Not when you have found the best way to say it. The day." The foot stopped. "Is that understood?"

"Last autumn, my lord, the tithe barn—" Haverill said, and then, as though a hand had been laid across his mouth, "Yes, my lord."

"That was a different matter," said Lord Alaric.

"Yes, my lord."

"You will come to the block at first light. You will stand behind me and say nothing. Go."

He went. He did not look at Orryn again. At the door he put up a hand to straighten a sleeve that was not crooked, and then took it down, and Orryn understood that he had learnt this habit in this room.

The door closed. The candles bent and straightened. Lord Alaric sat for a time looking at nothing in particular, and when he spoke it was in the same dry voice as before, as though nothing at all had happened.

"The son. Pyke. You said he sent a message."

"Yes, my lord. He said, 'I would sooner be asked than carried.' And that he would tell his mother himself, when the leaves are down. Not the reeve, nor you, nor I, my lord."

"He said that." The hazel eyes lifted. "A lame man's pride. It will cost more than bread." He picked up the pen, put it down. "The wage for grubbing is sound. Verrall's figure is sound. I shall think on it. I do not say I shall do it." And then, a little more quietly than he had said any of the rest, "You did as I asked, and in order. I do not say it was pleasant. I shall be at the east gate at first light, and so will you. Master Tull will show me the trees himself."

"Yes, my lord."

"The woman will know by noon, whatever is done. A lord cannot walk a block without being seen to." The hand-bell rang again, thin and cold, for the page. "That is the price of walking it. Eat your supper."

Orryn stood. His knees were not quite steady. He thought of Col Pyke's long wary face, and the bundle set down so carefully, and the thing he had said at the gate. That's mine to do. He had carried the message to the end of the road, as he had been asked, and the lord had taken it, and said I shall think on it, and rung a bell.

What does Orryn do?

1. Remind Lord Alaric, before he leaves, of Col's wish to tell his mother himself, and ask that tomorrow's visit be only a look at the block.

2. Say nothing more: the message has been delivered, and what the lord does with it is the lord's.

3. Slip out before dawn, find Col, and warn him that the lord is coming, so that he can tell Maud first.

4. Something else of your own.
