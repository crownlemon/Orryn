Orryn opened his mouth with the whole plan in it, the cutting and the carrying and the day's wage for Col Pyke to grub and graft, and found, looking at the long wary face, that it was not his to put into that man's hands. It was a thing for Verrall in figures and Lord Alaric in words, and Col Pyke was owed neither the hope of it nor the fear of it before Lord Alaric had said yes or no.

"We were sent to learn," he said. "Master Tull says you are the best grafter in the block. I know nothing about it. I should like to see it done."

It was perfectly true, and it was not at all what had been asked, and he stood there and let Col Pyke know both.

"That is not an answer," said Col.

"No."

"But it isn't a lie, either." The grey-brown eyes went to Tull, who was inspecting the sky with a scholar's attention, and came back. Col Pyke shouldered his bundle of twigs and turned downhill with the hitch in his stride. "Come, then. The light's going, and a bud won't wait on the lord's boys."

He led them down the slope, past Maud on her ladder, who waved a saw at them and shouted something about supper that the wind took, and along the foot of the block to a strip of ground by the brook that had been hurdled against the sheep. Inside the hurdles the earth was dark and carefully hoed, and set in rows, none higher than Orryn's knee, were hundreds of thin grey whips, each with a loop of bast knotted at the base. It looked like a very small, very patient army. It was the lord's nursery, Col said, and had been his to keep for nine years. The smell was green and cool after the sour heat of the slope, and the brook went by on the other side of the hedge, talking to itself.

"Pips," said Col. "Every pear you ever ate began as a pip, and a pear grown from a pip is a wild thing. Small, hard, thorny, sour as a rotten plum. It will grow, and it will root in anything, and it will give you nothing." He untied the bundle and laid it across his arm; the shoots were as long as a man's forearm, with the leaves stripped and a little stalk left at each joint. "So you take the root of the wild one, which knows the ground. And you set on it a bud cut from a good tree. Two trees, pretending to be one. By the time it bears, the root has forgotten it was ever sour."

He drew a knife from his belt, a short curved blade worn thin as a leaf from a hundred years of stones, and showed them. He did it quickly, without hurry, as a man does what his hands have done ten thousand times: a cut across the stock a hand above the soil, a cut down from it to make a T, a thumbnail run along the bark to lift the two wings of it open like a door. Then a bud from the shoot, the knife drawn under it in one flat sliver, the sliver slid in under the wings, the bark closed over, the whole bound with a turn of bast and tied. It took him less time than it took to tell. "That one is Maud Pyke's," he said, and tied off. "Row of the old white pear. Her husband's father's wood. It will do as well as any."

"The same pear?" Piers said. He had stopped looking at the wasp, and was bending close.

"Aye. Same as the one that's dying. I cut the wood from the sound trees of her row, while there are sound ones to cut it from." Col looked at the little knot of bast, not at them. "A pear tree is only the last of a long line of cuttings. It's not so easy to kill, as long as someone keeps the wood."

He gave Orryn the knife.

It was heavier than it looked and sharper than anything Orryn had ever held, and he went at the stock as he went at everything he wished he knew, by gripping too hard. The first cut was crooked. The second sawed. When he pried the bark it came away in a shred and the pale wet wood showed under it, and he made a noise in his throat that he hoped no one had heard. "Wrong," said Col, without heat. "You've the knife in your fist like a spoon. It's not in the hand. Look." And he took Orryn's wrist in his own hard one and turned it a quarter, and drew the blade through with Orryn's hand under his. "In the wrist. The knife only goes where you let it."

It seemed to Orryn that everything worth doing in the world was in the wrist.

He tried again, on a fresh stock. This one lifted. The bud went in crooked and he bound it too tight, and Col undid the turn and did it again, not saying that it was wrong, merely doing it right, which was worse. Piers took the knife next and ruined a stock in four strokes, swore, and wanted to try again. Neither of them said anything about the other. Orryn was surprised how little he minded.

"Will it take?" Piers asked, when he had finished.

"Look in three weeks," said Col. "If the leaf-stalk drops and the bud stays green, it's taken. If it shrivels, it's not." He wiped the blade on his thigh. "Half of them won't, mind. Any year. You set more than you need, and you grieve for the ones that fail, and you don't say so."

They worked on for a while in the long light, the three of them, with Tull sitting on a hurdle beside the brook and not offering a word. Orryn did eleven, and Col undid two. His fingers were stained green and sticky and the bast had cut a groove in his thumb, and the ache in his ribs had gone quiet somewhere in the middle of it, which he noticed only afterwards. There was a peace to it. He had not known he was missing it. He did not know any task at Grassfield that he did not do under someone's eye.

"Have you anything of your own, lord's boy?" Col asked, without looking up. He was tying off a bud for Piers, patiently, as one tends a child's mistake.

Orryn considered it. "A short sword. A clean shirt that isn't mine." He thought. "A horse I was lent."

Col laughed once, short, a breath through the nose. "Then you know a little of how it is," he said, and that was all.

The sun had touched the ridge when they finally went up. Col walked with them to the gate of the hurdled plot. He had Orryn's bast-cut thumb in his hand a moment, turning it to the light, a courtesy that seemed almost an afterthought, and then he let it go and looked at him straight.

"You'll carry what you saw to the lord," he said. "That's what you're for. I'll not ask what you mean to say. But carry this as well. I would sooner be asked than carried." He said the word as if it tasted of something. "I'll tell my mother myself, when the leaves are down. Not the reeve, not the lord, not you. That's mine to do."

"I can't promise what Lord Alaric will do," said Orryn.

"I'm not asking you to. I'm telling you what to carry." He bent and took up the bundle. "Mind the knife, next time. It wants oiling."

He went off along the hurdles towards the brook with his rolling stride, and did not look back.

Tull came up beside them on the path. He did not speak for a hundred paces, and then only to the evening. "He has never said so much to a lord's man in his life," said Hugh Tull. "Mind what you do with it, young master. It is yours now as much as mine. Haverill will not like to hear it, and Lord Alaric does not like to be told things by anybody who is not Lord Alaric."

Behind them the whips stood in their rows, grey in the dusk, each with its bit of white bast. Piers walked at Orryn's elbow with his hands stained green to the wrist and his doublet ruined, and for a long time said nothing at all. At the gate he said, quite low, "That was clever. Not answering."

"I didn't think it was clever."

"That's why it was." Piers looked at his hands. "My father would have told him everything he wanted and meant none of it. You'll find out which is worse." And he went in at the gate before Orryn could ask him what he meant.

The lamps were being lit in the keep. It would be time for supper soon, and before it, the hour with Verrall, and what he would say there. Orryn stood in the dusk with his green hands and the smell of the pear still in his clothes, and thought of everything he now knew that Lord Alaric did not: the black bark and the eleven rows, Tull's spring warning, Haverill's silence, and what a man had asked him to carry.
