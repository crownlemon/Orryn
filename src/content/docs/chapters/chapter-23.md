The page was perhaps nine, with a scab on one knee and the steward's badge pinned crooked to his tunic, and he had plainly been told to say wash first in exactly those words and to watch that it was done.

"Where does Master Matthos go at noon?" Orryn asked.

The boy's mouth fell open a little. "The smithy yard, Master Orryn, when the smith's at his dinner. But his lordship said—"

"I heard what his lordship said." Orryn was already walking. "Tell the steward I am being washed. It will be true by the time you find him."

It was not the best lie he had ever told, and he did not like how easily it had come. But the bell was still swinging, and if he did not do it now he would not do it at all. By supper the yard would have made up its mind about him, as the yard made up its mind about everything, and a boy who was the goose on his first day would be the goose on his hundredth.

He did not feel that he was deciding. He felt, crossing behind the stables with his ribs complaining at every step, that it had been decided a long while ago, in the great hall at Grandview, the Harvest Home when he was ten.

There had been a ribbon for the best at the quintain, blue silk cut from one of his aunt Lady Lynesse's old gowns. Orryn had struck the board six times in eight. His cousin Arlan, who was ten as well and the heir of Grandview, had struck it twice, and had gone so red about the neck that the old steward, who had a good nose for weather, announced that the ribbon was for the house, and carried it to Arlan with a bow. Orryn's mother had put her hand on his shoulder and left it there. His father, Ser Steffon, had said nothing at all. He had a way of saying nothing, with a muscle moving at the back of his jaw, that Orryn knew as well as he knew a column of figures. Only later, over the cold meat, his father said to the salt cellar, "Six in eight. I counted." Orryn had looked up, hoping. There had been nothing in his face for him. It was not pride. It was a grievance against Ser Steffon's elder brother, Lord Alester, who ruled Grandview and was owed the first of everything, and Orryn had only been the sum it was worked on.

It was no different for his sister Alyssa, the sharpest person at Grandview, who was to marry Ser Edric Wagstaff of Greenward and his hounds, while their cousin Elenei had gone to the Penroses of Parchments and Arlan was promised to the Cafferen girl. Ser Edric was a decent man; everyone said so, in the tone people kept for weather. Nobody had meant it. Nobody ever meant it. The good places only ran downhill, like water, to whoever stood nearest the lord, and the rest of them were taught from the cradle to hold their cups and wait. He had been losing nicely all his life. He was very good at it. That was what made it so foul to find that he was about to do it on purpose.

He heard them before he saw them: iron on iron, a clang and a ringing, and then a roar. Behind the smithy the forge stood banked and cold, and the smith's boy sat in the doorway eating an onion. Beyond him, on a patch of trodden earth, an iron stake had been driven into the ground, and another stood fifteen strides off, and the squires were pitching horseshoes between them. Alyn Merryweather, the eldest of them, leaned on a barrel, long and lazy. Baldric Haye was keeping the tally on a slate. Perkin Fenwick was hopping from foot to foot. Piers Fallow, the miller's son, sat on the mounting block with his chin on his knee. Osric was not there. Osric, Orryn supposed, was at his prayers.

And in the middle of them stood Matthos Meadows, Lord Alaric's son and heir, with a shoe in each hand and his blue tunic torn open at the neck, taking aim.

The shoe went out in a flat turning arc and rang round the far stake. Perkin whooped. Matthos bowed to nobody, and then he saw Orryn, and the yard went quiet in the way it had gone quiet that morning.

Orryn made himself walk the last of it. "That was well thrown," he said, and it was true, which helped.

"Goose!" said Matthos, as if he had been expected. "Come to see how it's done?"

"Come to lose, if there's room." It came out lighter than he had hoped. "My ribs want me to lie down. I thought I'd lose standing up."

Alyn laughed outright. Perkin looked quickly from one of them to the other, uncertain whether he was permitted to. Matthos stood quite still for a moment, the shoe in his hand, and Orryn watched something cross his face and be put away: surprise, perhaps, at a thing he had not expected to be handed, and close behind it a pleasure as bright and open as a small child's. "Three throws each, turn and turn about," he said. "Only rings count." He tossed Orryn a shoe. It was cold and heavier than it looked. "I'll go first. You can have the last word."

Orryn had paced the distance when he came, out of that old habit he could not break. Fifteen strides. He did not think of it again until Matthos stepped up to the line, which was a plank laid in the dirt, and the plank moved under his toe, only a little, a hand's breadth towards the far stake, and when he threw he stood a full pace nearer than Orryn would.

Orryn's eyes went to Piers. Piers was looking at the sky.

He cheats, and calls it cleverness, Piers had said in the stable. The rest of us have learned not to see the cleverness.

He did not see it. Matthos's first shoe rang round the stake. Orryn's own fell short and spun, and Matthos's second went wide by a hand, and then Orryn's, by luck rather than skill, went high and wobbled and clinked into the ring as if the iron had a mind of its own. "Ha!" said Alyn. "The goose has a wing." Matthos's third was a ringer. He walked back to the plank with his chest out and his grin enormous, and Orryn felt his jaw tighten. It was a very fine throw, and he disliked him for it.

One ring to Orryn, two to Matthos, and the last shoe cold in his hand. He needed a ring to tie. He thought he could make it, and he stood there in the glare weighing the iron, and the whole yard, quite kindly, waited. He could hear the smith's boy chewing his onion. He could hear his father name the price in a breath, and call it fair, and be right, which was the worst of it. Piers had said that Matthos could not bear to lose. What Orryn found he could not bear, with the shoe in his hand, was knowing it.

He let his wrist go slack at the last. The shoe flew high and long, and thumped into the dirt a yard beyond the stake.

"Ohh," said Perkin, in real sorrow.

"Wide," Orryn said, and shook his head at his own hand. "You throw like a siege engine, cousin. I should like to know how it's done."

He had not lied. That was a small private comfort, and he held on to it as one holds a rail on a stair in the dark: every word had been true, except the ones he had left out.

Matthos whooped and clapped him on the back, on the ribs, on the exact bruise, so that the world went white at the edges for a heartbeat. "Wrist, Goose! It's all in the wrist and the knees. Here, look—" And he was showing him, with two shoes and enormous goodwill, how to hold the iron so that it lay flat on the air, and Orryn nodded and bent his knees as he was told and found himself, to his horror, listening. Matthos talked the way he threw, with his whole body. He said that Ser Everard would not show the low feint because it was low, and that Ser Lucan could not, being a plank and born one. He would teach it tomorrow after the yard. He had it from a huntsman's son. "Same time tomorrow, Goose," he said. It was the same sentence he had spoken in the dust, in the same bright voice, and it was an entirely different sentence, and the horror of it was that Orryn found he liked being asked.

"Father's sent for you. The whole yard heard your page." Matthos was winding a strip of rag around his palm, and the shine had slipped a little. "The solar. Before dinner. He's not sent for me before dinner in my life, unless I'd broken something."

"Perhaps I have," said Orryn.

That got the laugh, and the grin, and he saw Baldric smile into the slate and make a small mark that was not a score. "He'll ask what you can do," Matthos said. "When I was nine I said I could ride, and he said, so can the horse." He tossed the rag away. "Say less than that. Go on, you'll be late. Wash first."

Piers had not moved from the mounting block. As Orryn passed he said, quite low, without turning his head, "You threw that last one wide."

"I slipped."

"Aye," said Piers. "So does he."

Orryn walked on and did not look back. The sun on the yard was very hot, and the iron smell of the forge was still in his nose, and he could not tell whether what lay in his belly was shame or a grim sort of pride, or only a pebble of each. He had spent something, that was certain. He did not yet know what it had bought.

The pump stood in the lower yard. He worked the handle one-handed and ducked his head under the spout and gasped at the cold, and scrubbed the dust from his neck and wrists, and beat the worst of the yard from Lem's shirt, which was still too big for him. Then he climbed the stair to the solar with the bell silent at last and his hair dripping down his collar. The page, who had been waiting at the top with great dignity, looked at the wet head and then at his face, and said nothing, which Orryn counted as a kindness.

He raised his hand to the door. From the other side came the dry scratch of a pen, and then Lord Alaric's voice, not raised.

"Come."

What does Orryn do?

1. Answer what Lord Alaric asks him and no more, as Matthos advised, and let him lead.

2. Put himself forward before he is asked: offer to be set to work on Lyman Verrall's accounts, where Lord Alaric can count what he does.

3. Ask Lord Alaric's leave to write the three letters he owes, to Alyssa, to Elenei and to Lady Alysanne, and to have his keep reckoned honestly, so that he knows what he costs.

4. Something else of your own.
