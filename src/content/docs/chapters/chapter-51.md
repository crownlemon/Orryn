Highgarden, 208 AC

The great hall of Highgarden was larger than the courtyard at Grassfield Keep.

Orryn had not believed it possible for a hall to be so broad. Three long tables stretched away from the raised dais, and there was room between them for servants to pass carrying platters, flagons and great baskets of bread. Above, the rafters disappeared into shadow. Hundreds of candles burned along the walls, beneath painted shields and banners whose colours he could not begin to remember.

The Tyrell rose hung highest, worked in gold upon green silk. Beneath it, Lord Leo sat in a carved chair at the centre of the dais, with Lady Arlanna beside him.

She was a tall woman, though age had begun to bend her shoulders. Her grey hair was dressed beneath a headdress of green velvet, and pearls glimmered at her throat. She watched the movement of guests and servants with an attention quite unlike her husband's.

Leo was laughing.

He had been laughing when Orryn entered, and continued while Lord Alaric was presented, apparently finishing some tale about a horse that had thrown a knight into a stream.

"A fine animal," Leo declared. "Never missed its footing until somebody foolish enough to call himself a knight climbed upon it."

A grey-haired lord seated near the dais answered that the horse had been badly trained.

"Then its rider should have taken greater care," Leo said.

There was laughter at the high table.

Orryn followed Alaric through the hall, keeping a pace behind Matthos. A steward directed them toward the seats prepared for House Meadows, not far from the dais.

Alaric bowed to Leo and Lady Arlanna.

"Welcome to Highgarden," the lady said.

Her voice was measured, and though she smiled, Orryn thought she looked more closely at Alaric's clothes than at his face.

"Your wife remains at Grassfield?"

"Lady Jeyne is well, my lady. She sends her respects."

"And her children?"

"Well also. Save this one, whom I have brought to trouble your household."

Matthos bowed so deeply that the cloak slipped from his shoulder.

Lady Arlanna regarded him.

"Then I trust he shall prove a small trouble."

"I shall endeavour to be none at all, my lady," Matthos said.

"That would make you an uncommon boy."

Leo laughed again.

"Come, sit. We have enough solemn men here already."

Alaric was shown to a place among several visiting lords. Matthos sat farther down, while Orryn remained behind his master's chair until a servant brought him a stool.

At Grassfield, he knew the household well enough to judge when he might sit, when he must serve, and which dishes were intended for whom. Here, every movement seemed to require another man's permission.

A page in green and gold set a cup beside him.

"You're with Meadows?"

"Yes."

"Then that's yours. They'll feed the squires at the second table once the first dishes are served."

"Thank you."

The boy departed before Orryn could ask his name.

Across the hall, a company of knights occupied several places near the high table. Orryn recognised the golden tree of House Rowan upon a surcoat. Beside it were the three black castles of House Peake.

He remembered seeing those arms among Maester Perrin's rolls at Grassfield.

House Rowan held Goldengrove in the northern Reach, with lands and bannermen far greater than those of House Meadows. House Peake had once possessed three castles in the Dornish Marches. Two had been taken from them after the Blackfyre Rebellion.

Orryn had heard enough arguments at Grassfield to know that the war had not ended all quarrels when the fighting stopped.

He was still studying the table when a man in a dark green doublet approached Alaric.

"Lord Meadows?"

Alaric rose.

"Ser Moryn."

The man smiled and offered his hand.

Ser Moryn Tyrell was younger than his father, though the resemblance was plain around the eyes and mouth. He was broad-shouldered without being especially heavy, with brown hair beginning to recede at the temples and a neatly trimmed beard. He moved with the ease of a man accustomed to being greeted wherever he went.

"My wife has spoken very favourably of your household," he said.

"Lady Melessa has been most generous."

"Melessa is generous when she likes someone. Less so when she does not."

Alaric smiled.

"I shall endeavour to remain in her favour."

"An excellent ambition. I have pursued it for twelve years and cannot claim success every day."

Moryn's attention passed to Matthos.

"And here is the young man."

Matthos stood at once.

"Ser Moryn."

"So you wish to become a knight."

"Yes, ser."

"That's fortunate. I have little use for squires who wish to become merchants."

"Though merchants are useful," said Alaric.

"Very useful. Particularly when their purses are open."

Moryn laughed, then rested a hand briefly on Matthos's shoulder.

"We shall speak properly after supper. My wife has arranged some wine in the south gallery, and there are several people who would be pleased to make your acquaintance."

"I should be honoured," Alaric replied.

"Good. Bring the boy. And your young Grandison, if he has not already found better company."

Orryn was surprised to hear himself mentioned.

Moryn looked toward him.

"You are Orryn Grandison?"

"Yes, ser."

"I remember the name from my wife's letters. You serve Lord Meadows?"

"I do."

"Then you must have learned patience."

Alaric raised his cup.

"Among other virtues."

"I have found patience a particularly difficult one to teach."

Moryn inclined his head and moved on to greet another guest.

Matthos remained standing until his father told him to sit.

"He knows who I am," Matthos whispered.

"He knew you were coming," Orryn answered.

"That isn't the same."

Orryn was not sure why it mattered so much. Matthos looked pleased enough to forgive any disagreement.

*

The first course brought roast capons, river fish dressed with herbs, fresh peas in butter and small pastries filled with mushrooms.

Orryn ate hungrily once the servants had found him a place among the younger attendants.

From there he could see the high table without being obliged to stand beside Alaric's chair. Boys in Tyrell colours sat near him, along with two squires whose masters he did not recognise.

One complained that the bread was stale.

Another said that if he wanted fresh bread he ought to rise before dawn and help bake it.

"Then I'd be hungry again by supper."

"You're hungry now."

"That proves my point."

Orryn listened while pulling a piece of capon from the bone.

The hall was warm. The candles smoked, and beneath the smell of roasted meat lay the scent of rushes, spilled wine and too many people gathered in one place.

At the high table, Leo was speaking to a knight with a red beard. Lady Arlanna interrupted a servant who was placing a platter before her.

She said something Orryn could not hear.

The servant lifted the platter and carried it to another part of the table. A moment later a different dish was brought.

No one else appeared to notice.

Orryn wondered whether Lady Jeyne would have done the same at Grassfield.

She might have. But there she could have called Marya and explained what was wrong, instead of instructing a servant she had perhaps never seen before.

A little farther down the table, Ser Garth Tyrell was speaking with a man in a dark red doublet.

Garth looked older than Moryn. His hair was thinning, and his manner was quieter. He listened while the other man spoke, then answered with his fingers resting upon the table.

Orryn had heard Garth's name often enough. He was Leo's eldest son and heir, married to Lady Alys Redwyne of the Arbor. One day Highgarden would be his.

Yet it was Moryn who seemed most at ease among the guests.

A few moments later, Garth rose and left the hall with the red-clad man.

Leo continued eating.

When the second course arrived, Alaric sent for Orryn.

"Grandison, fetch my gloves."

"They're in the guest chamber, my lord."

"Of course they are. I left them there. Fetch them all the same."

Orryn rose.

"And find Matthos. He has vanished."

Orryn looked toward the place where Matthos had been sitting.

The chair was empty.

"Yes, my lord."

He found Matthos near the far end of the hall, talking to two boys wearing the golden rose.

"They say Ser Moryn has four squires already," Matthos said when Orryn approached.

"Then he may not want a fifth."

"One is nearly old enough to be knighted."

"That doesn't mean he will be."

Matthos frowned.

"Must you always say that?"

"Your father wants you."

"Now?"

"He said to find you."

Matthos looked regretfully toward his new companions.

"I shall see you tomorrow," he told them.

One of the boys nodded. The other had already turned back to his food.

As they passed the dais, Lord Leo was raising a cup.

"To the king," he declared, "and to a summer that may yet remember how to rain."

The company drank.

Orryn thought of the fields beyond Grassfield, the low water in the Blueburn and the hard earth beneath the horses' hooves.

He drank too.

*

After supper, the guests dispersed through several galleries and chambers overlooking Highgarden's gardens.

Orryn followed Alaric beneath an arcade where the air was cooler. Open windows overlooked a terrace planted with climbing roses. Somewhere outside, water ran into a basin, and the voices of men in the garden drifted through the darkness.

The south gallery was a long chamber with cushioned benches, painted panels and a broad hearth standing empty in the summer heat. Candles burned upon two tables. Servants had set out wine, sweetmeats and dishes of fruit.

Ser Moryn stood beside his wife.

Lady Melessa Tyrell, born Ashford, greeted Alaric with a warmth that suggested she had been expecting him.

"My lord. I am glad you came."

"I could hardly refuse your husband's hospitality."

"Most men can refuse him very little. That is one of his more troublesome habits."

Moryn put a hand over his heart.

"My lady wounds me before our guests."

"You have survived worse."

Lady Melessa turned toward Matthos.

"And you must be Lord Alaric's son. Lady Ashford spoke well of your father's household."

"Thank you, my lady."

"I trust you have enjoyed Highgarden."

"Very much."

"Have you seen the gardens?"

"Some of them."

"And the sept?"

"Not yet."

"The practice yard, then?"

Matthos brightened.

"Yes, my lady."

"I thought so."

Her smile was small but not unkind.

Orryn remembered Lady Ashford at Grassfield, speaking privately with Alaric about the advantages of serving a Tyrell knight. He wondered how much Lady Melessa had heard of that visit.

Moryn called Alaric toward another part of the chamber.

"Come. There is someone I should like you to meet."

The man standing beside the window wore a dark cloak fastened with a golden tree.

He was tall, with a long face and a beard streaked with grey. His hands were broad, and the fingers of his right hand were stained faintly with ink.

"Lord Rycherd Rowan," Moryn said, "Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield."

Rowan inclined his head.

"Meadows. I know your name."

"And I yours, my lord."

"Then we are spared a tedious introduction."

He offered Alaric his hand.

Orryn noticed that Rowan's grip was firm, though the older lord did not smile.

"How fares the country about Grassy Vale?" Rowan asked.

"Dry, like most of the Reach."

"All the way to the Blueburn?"

"The river still runs, but not as it ought. The upper brooks are low."

Rowan made a dissatisfied sound.

"Goldengrove has had little rain since the spring. My steward has begun arguing with the millers over who owns the water before it reaches the wheel."

"Have they settled it?"

"They've written me three letters. That generally means they have not."

Alaric laughed.

Moryn drew a stool toward the window.

"Sit, Rycherd. I shall have wine brought."

"I have wine."

"Then you shall have better wine."

"See that it is."

Moryn went to speak with a servant.

Rowan turned to Alaric.

"I understand you have come about a place for your son."

"That is my hope."

"An honourable service, if Ser Moryn agrees."

"I believe it would be."

"Have you considered what it will cost you?"

Alaric seemed amused.

"I have some experience of maintaining squires."

"Not at Highgarden."

Matthos looked at his father.

Rowan continued, "A boy who serves among the sons of great houses learns quickly what other boys possess. Horses, armour, clothes, coin to spend. You will discover that a new saddle is indispensable because three other boys have one."

"I have heard something of this," Alaric said.

"It does not mean you must indulge him. Quite the contrary."

Matthos lowered his eyes.

Orryn found himself liking Rowan a little better.

Then the lord looked toward him.

"And this one?"

"Ser Orryn Grandison's son," Alaric replied. "He serves me."

"I am no ser, my lord," Orryn said before he could stop himself.

Rowan raised his brows.

"Neither was your father at fourteen, I imagine."

Orryn felt heat rise in his face.

"No, my lord."

"Then you have time."

Alaric smiled into his wine.

*

More guests joined them as the evening continued.

A man with dark hair and a long, narrow nose entered beside Lady Arlanna. He wore a heavy chain of gold and a doublet worked with three black castles.

Moryn went to greet him.

"Uncle Gormon."

The man embraced him briefly.

Orryn recognised the name.

Lord Gormon Peake of Starpike. Lady Arlanna's younger brother.

He had fought for Daemon Blackfyre, and his house had suffered heavily after the rebellion. Orryn knew this because Lord Alaric had spoken of Peake's lost castles more than once, sometimes with sympathy and sometimes as a warning.

Lady Arlanna greeted Lord Rowan with familiarity.

"Rycherd. Your lady wife has not come?"

"Clarice remains at Goldengrove, my lady."

"Then I shall write to her. She has neglected her correspondence."

"She would say the same of you."

"Then she would be wrong."

Rowan bowed his head, though Orryn saw him smiling.

Lady Arlanna took a seat. Servants immediately brought another candle and moved a table closer to her.

Lord Peake remained standing for a time, speaking quietly with Moryn.

Orryn could not hear them.

He was rather glad of it. There were already more important names in the room than he could comfortably remember.

A servant offered him a cup of watered wine.

He accepted and stood near the window, where he could see Matthos trying to draw himself taller beside his father.

Lord Rowan had returned to speaking of the harvest.

"We have grain enough," he said, "if the summer breaks before the fields are ruined. It is the carrying that troubles me."

"Your roads?" Alaric asked.

"Our roads. Their bridges. The men who collect at them."

Alaric laughed.

"I have just come from Bitterbridge."

"Then you will understand."

"Lord Armond was speaking of your grain carts."

"Was he?"

"He said there had been some disagreement concerning the western toll."

Rowan's expression changed very little.

"Armond has never found a toll he did not consider his own."

"His wife seems rather less certain."

"Lady Brenna is her father's daughter when it suits her."

Moryn returned with a cup of wine.

"Which of my sisters has offended you now?"

"None. We were speaking of Bitterbridge."

"Then I am relieved. I feared this was a family quarrel."

"It may become one," Rowan said.

Moryn sat.

"That would be unfortunate. I have enough family already."

Lord Peake approached at the mention of Bitterbridge.

"What has Armond done?"

"Nothing yet," Rowan answered. "He is being asked to produce rolls and grants because the Arbor has discovered that my grain carts pass his bridge without paying the same toll as theirs."

"Do they?"

"They have done so for longer than I have been lord."

Peake took a cup from the table.

"Then I should have thought the matter settled."

"Some men believe a thing is settled only when they have written it in a book," Rowan said.

"Others believe an ancient right is a thing that grows larger every time it is remembered," Moryn replied.

Rowan regarded him.

"Are you speaking of me?"

"Of my father's bannermen generally. You are merely the one nearest."

Peake gave a short laugh.

Alaric shifted in his chair.

"Lady Brenna said Garth's officers had asked for copies of the toll books."

"Indeed they have," Rowan said. "I am not opposed to keeping records. I employ men to do precisely that. What I question is why a factor of the Arbor should presume to demand the instruments by which my forefathers held their rights."

"Perhaps he wishes to know why his own carts pay," Alaric said.

"He may ask Lord Armond. He collects the money."

"And if Lord Armond asks you?"

"Then I shall answer him."

"With the grant?" asked Moryn.

Rowan looked at him again.

"If my steward can find the proper copy."

"And if he cannot?"

"Then I shall remind Armond that his father collected the toll in precisely the same fashion."

Moryn drank.

"That may be sufficient for Armond."

"It ought to be."

"It may not satisfy his wife."

For a moment no one spoke.

Then Peake said, "The trouble with officers is that they are forever discovering reasons to reconsider things that other men have settled."

Orryn thought the remark had been made casually. Yet Lord Peake was no longer smiling.

Rowan turned his cup between his hands.

"An office can be granted," he said. "A right inherited is another matter."

"Sometimes they discover that an office was granted to the wrong man," Peake replied.

Orryn remembered the stories of Peake's lost castles. He wondered whether the old lord was thinking of them.

Moryn set down his wine.

"If we are to begin judging all my father's appointments tonight, we shall require another barrel."

Lady Arlanna, who had been listening from her chair, spoke without raising her voice.

"My brother has not come to Highgarden to quarrel over the Dornish Marches."

"No, sister," Peake said.

"And Lord Rowan has not come to quarrel over a bridge."

"I had hoped to avoid it," Rowan answered.

"Then you may both succeed."

There was an awkward silence.

Moryn broke it by calling for fresh cups.

Orryn could not tell whether the disagreement had ended or merely been put away.

He had seen Lady Jeyne perform something similar at Grassfield. She had a way of silencing arguments without allowing anyone the satisfaction of believing he had won.

*

It was Lady Melessa who returned the conversation to Matthos.

"Ser Moryn, you have kept Lord Alaric waiting long enough."

Her husband looked across the room.

"Have I?"

"You invited him to speak about his son."

"So I did."

Moryn waved Matthos closer.

"Come here, boy. Let us have a proper look at you."

Matthos crossed the room so quickly that Orryn feared he might stumble over the edge of a carpet.

Moryn remained seated.

"How old are you?"

"Fourteen, ser."

"And how long have you been riding?"

"Since I was little."

"That is a most imprecise answer. Were you little yesterday?"

"No, ser."

"Good. Then we have established that."

Lady Melessa looked amused.

"Do be kind."

"I am being kind."

Moryn studied Matthos for a moment.

"What have you been taught?"

"Riding, weapons, the lance, caring for horses. Ser Everard drills us. Ser Lucan also."

"Can you read?"

"Yes, ser."

"Write?"

"Yes."

"Count?"

"Yes."

"Good. You will find counting useful. Especially when someone tells you that you have lost a horse."

Matthos blinked.

"Lost one, ser?"

"Suppose I send you to fetch my grey gelding. You return with a brown mare. What have you done?"

"Brought the wrong horse."

"And what would you do about it?"

"Fetch the grey."

"Quite right. But suppose the grey is lame."

Matthos hesitated.

"I would tell you."

"Before or after you saddled him?"

"Before."

"Excellent. And suppose the groom says he was sound yesterday?"

"I would look at him."

"Where?"

"At his legs."

"All four?"

"Yes, ser."

Moryn smiled.

"That is a beginning."

Matthos glanced toward Alaric, who said nothing.

Moryn leaned back.

"Do you know what a squire does when his knight has been drinking too much to remember where he left his sword?"

The question surprised Orryn.

Matthos frowned.

"Finds it, ser."

"And if he cannot?"

"Looks again."

Moryn laughed.

"Very good. That is the answer most of us require."

Lord Rowan, still seated by the window, spoke.

"And if the knight is a fool, he may spend his whole life looking."

"Then he has chosen his service poorly," Moryn answered.

He turned back to Matthos.

"I have squires already. They will not all welcome another boy. Some will be older than you, some better trained, and all will believe that they deserve my favour."

"I understand, ser."

"Do you?"

Matthos said nothing.

Moryn's voice grew more serious.

"You may be Lord Meadows's son at Grassfield. Here you will be expected to do the work of a squire. If a horse must be cleaned, you will clean it. If an older squire is ordered to assist me, you will not assume that you ought to have been chosen instead."

"Yes, ser."

"And you will obey Ser Luthor Risley when he instructs you in the yard."

Orryn recognised the name only because the master-at-arms in the practice yard had been mentioned earlier.

"He has served my father for more than twenty years," Moryn continued. "He has forgotten more about swordsmanship than some knights ever learn, though I should advise you not to tell him so."

Matthos smiled nervously.

"Yes, ser."

Moryn rose.

"Come to the lower practice yard tomorrow. Early. Wear clothes you can spoil, and bring the boots you travelled in. I shall see what you can do."

Matthos looked as though he might leap from the floor.

"Thank you, ser."

"I have not accepted you."

"No, ser."

"But I am willing to look."

Alaric stood.

"You are generous to consider him."

"Let us see whether he is worth considering."

Moryn gave Matthos a friendly clap upon the shoulder.

"Now go and enjoy the evening. Tomorrow you may discover that Highgarden is rather less pleasant before breakfast."

*

They returned to their chambers after the candles had begun burning low.

Orryn carried Alaric's gloves and the empty wine cup his lord had taken from the gallery without remembering to leave it behind.

Matthos walked ahead, talking without pause.

"Did you hear him? He said I should come tomorrow. And he asked about my riding. I told him everything Ser Everard has taught me."

"Not everything," Alaric said.

"Nearly everything."

"He asked whether you could look at a lame horse."

"I can."

"Then see that you do."

Matthos fell silent for several steps.

"Do you think he will take me?"

"I think he wishes to see you ride."

"That is a good sign."

"It is better than a refusal."

Matthos considered this.

"Lord Rowan was friendly."

"He was courteous."

"He said it would be an honourable service."

"He did."

"Then he approves."

Alaric stopped beneath an arch.

"Matthos."

"Yes, Father?"

"You have heard a great many things tonight. You would do well not to decide what all of them mean before you have slept."

Matthos looked displeased.

"I only said he approved."

"And I only advised you to sleep."

They resumed walking.

Orryn felt tired enough to sleep standing.

At the guest chamber Alaric dismissed Matthos and told Orryn to prepare the papers for the morning.

"Not all of them. The smaller packet with the blue cord."

Orryn found it in the leather case.

"The one addressed to Ser Garth?"

"Yes."

"And the other rolls?"

"Bring those as well. The Seneschal may wish to see them."

Orryn looked at the packet.

"Will Ser Garth receive you himself?"

"I should think so."

Alaric sat on the edge of the bed and pulled off a boot.

"His people have certainly made a proper arrangement of it."

"Did you like Ser Moryn, my lord?"

Alaric considered.

"He was agreeable."

"And Lord Rowan?"

"An old lord who knows what he is owed."

"Is that good?"

"It depends what he is owed."

Alaric put down the boot.

"Enough questions. Go to bed, Grandison."

Orryn bowed.

As he reached the door, Alaric spoke again.

"And tell Matthos that if he fails to rise tomorrow, I shall send him home in disgrace."

Orryn smiled.

"Yes, my lord."

He found Matthos already sitting on his bed, polishing his riding boots.

*

In the morning Orryn woke to the sound of someone knocking upon the door.

A servant had brought hot water.

Beyond the window, the garden court was bright beneath a pale sky. The air smelled of damp stone and freshly cut grass, though no rain had fallen.

Matthos had been awake before Orryn. His bed was empty, and his travelling boots were gone.

Orryn wondered whether he had remembered to eat.

Alaric was dressing when Orryn entered.

"Where's the boy?"

"At the practice yard, I think, my lord."

"Then he has discovered how to rise early after all."

Orryn laid out the blue-corded packet.

"Are these the papers?"

"Yes."

Alaric fastened his collar.

"Have you eaten?"

"Not yet."

"Then see to it. I don't want you fainting before Garth's clerks. They'll put the expense in their accounts."

Orryn laughed.

He went to the servants' chamber, where bread, cheese and watered ale had been set out for visiting retainers.

Several men were discussing a cart that had arrived late from the Arbor.

"Three barrels short," one said.

"Then count them again."

"I counted twice."

"Perhaps the third time they'll appear."

A broad-shouldered knight in a plain dark doublet stood beside the doorway, speaking with two servants. His surcoat bore the black bars of Bandallon.

"...not in the west range," the knight was saying. "The west range is full."

"But Lord Rowan's household..."

"Lord Rowan's household has rooms enough for every man who came with him. If another six have appeared overnight, you may ask them which six they intend to put outside."

The servant shifted uneasily.

"And the Arbor men?"

"They may have the chambers beside the south stair. Not the storeroom. I told you yesterday that the storeroom is full of bedding."

"Yes, ser."

"Then why are we discussing it?"

The servant departed.

The knight noticed Orryn watching.

"You're with Meadows?"

"Yes, ser."

"Ser Owen Blackbar. Steward of Highgarden."

Orryn bowed.

"Ser Owen."

"Lord Meadows is expected in the Seneschal's chamber after the morning meal. Have you his letters?"

"Yes, ser."

"Good. And do not carry them through the kitchens. Somebody will put a pot upon them."

Orryn looked down at the packet beneath his arm.

"I wasn't going through the kitchens."

"Then we shall both be spared trouble."

Ser Owen turned to another servant.

"Have the north chamber made ready for the Beesbury men. Their horses are staying another night."

"Yes, ser."

"And send someone to find out whether Lord Rowan's people have finished moving their baggage. I should prefer not to spend the day hearing about it."

He walked away.

Orryn thought of the men quarrelling over rooms the previous afternoon.

At Grassfield, Marya could usually settle such matters with a word and a rearrangement of beds.

At Highgarden, it seemed to require a knight.

*

The Seneschal's chamber lay beyond the inner court, in a range of buildings overlooking a smaller garden.

There were no painted shields upon its walls. The windows were narrow, the furniture heavy, and the tables covered with parchment, account books and small wooden boxes holding seals.

A clerk led Alaric and Orryn into an adjoining room.

Three men were waiting.

The first stood beside a broad table, wearing a dark red doublet embroidered with grape leaves at the cuffs. He was about Garth's age, perhaps a little younger, with closely cropped hair and a carefully trimmed beard.

"Lord Meadows," he said. "Ser Arron Redwyne. I trust you were well accommodated."

"Very well, ser."

"I am pleased to hear it."

He offered his hand.

"I serve Lord Leo as Seneschal. We received your letter concerning the eastern roads and thought it better to discuss the matter while you were here."

Alaric nodded.

"I had hoped to speak with Ser Garth."

"He will join us shortly. In the meantime, allow me to present Lord Osmund Leygood, our Justiciar, and Ser Lyman Beesbury, Lord Treasurer."

Lord Osmund was an older man with a narrow face and grey hair cut short around his ears. He wore a dark robe over a knight's belt, and his hands rested upon a closed book.

Ser Lyman Beesbury was younger, dressed in brown and yellow, with a pale face and a small mark of ink upon one finger.

Both greeted Alaric politely.

Orryn stood behind his lord's chair with the papers beneath his arm.

Ser Arron indicated the table.

"If you have brought the earlier correspondence?"

"Grandison."

Orryn stepped forward and laid down the blue-corded packet.

Alaric untied it.

Several letters emerged, together with a folded copy of a grant.

Ser Arron examined the seals.

"Good. You have brought the copies as requested."

"I have brought what I possess," Alaric said. "Whether it is what you require is another question."

"Then we shall discover it."

Orryn noticed that Ser Arron spoke more easily than most officials he had encountered. He seemed accustomed to discussing difficult matters without making his guest feel that he had done anything wrong.

Lord Osmund opened his book.

"Your concern is the road eastward from Grassy Vale?"

"One of them."

"Toward the Stormlands?"

"Yes. There are several stretches maintained by my tenants, and others where the obligations are less clear."

"Less clear in what manner?"

Alaric leaned forward.

"The road is used by men who do not hold land from me. Merchants, travellers, carts carrying goods toward the border. Some pay passage. Others claim exemption. I have men repairing the worst stretches, but the work has grown beyond what the old obligations contemplated."

Ser Lyman took up a small sheet.

"Your letter mentions gravel, timber and drainage."

"It does."

"Have you the sums?"

"I have some."

"Some?"

"I did not travel to Highgarden carrying every account from Grassfield."

Ser Lyman looked as though he might answer, then thought better of it.

Ser Arron spoke.

"We have not asked you to surrender your accounts, my lord. What Ser Lyman requires is enough information to judge whether the present arrangements are reasonable."

Alaric turned to him.

"And what will follow from that judgment?"

"That depends upon what we discover."

"Then I should like to know what authority you intend to exercise before I begin producing figures."

Orryn recognised the tone. Alaric had used it with Dunstan Fallow when the dispute over the Alder Mill grew difficult.

Lord Osmund folded his hands.

"A reasonable question."

Ser Arron inclined his head.

"Indeed."

The Seneschal opened one of the letters.

"Lord Leo has authorised an examination of several roads and crossings whose upkeep has become a matter of dispute. We are gathering information. No new obligation has been imposed upon House Meadows."

"Not yet," Alaric said.

"Not by this examination."

"Then that is worth knowing."

Ser Lyman spoke quietly.

"If the work costs more than it once did, my lord, there may be grounds to consider a different arrangement. But it is difficult to judge that without accounts."

Alaric regarded him.

"How much do your people at Honeyholt spend repairing roads?"

Ser Lyman seemed surprised.

"That depends upon the year."

"And the weather?"

"Naturally."

"And the number of carts?"

"Among other things."

"Then you understand why the cost changes."

"I do. That is why I have asked what it costs."

Alaric was silent for a moment.

Then he smiled.

"Very well, ser. You shall have what figures I can provide."

Orryn had expected the exchange to become unpleasant. Instead, Ser Lyman made a note.

He wondered whether the Treasurer knew that he had won anything.

*

The door opened, and a clerk announced Ser Garth Tyrell.

Everyone rose.

Garth entered without ceremony, carrying a folded letter. He wore a dark green doublet with a golden rose worked upon the breast, and there was a little grey in his beard.

He greeted Alaric by name.

"Lord Meadows. I trust my father received you properly."

"Most generously."

"He has never been accused of offering too little hospitality."

Garth seated himself beside Ser Arron.

"Where have we reached?"

"The eastern roads," the Seneschal replied. "Lord Meadows has brought copies of the earlier correspondence. We are considering the extent of the maintenance obligations."

Garth looked toward Alaric.

"I have read your letter. You say the road has grown more heavily travelled in recent years."

"It has."

"Because of the trade with the Stormlands?"

"In part. And because more goods now move through Grassy Vale that once took other routes."

"Do you regard that as a good thing?"

"Trade is good for the town."

"But the road suffers."

"The road suffers whether I welcome the trade or not."

Garth smiled faintly.

"An important distinction."

He took one of the papers.

"Your grant mentions obligations for the maintenance of the road, but it does not specify the amount of traffic those obligations were expected to support."

Lord Osmund leaned forward.

"That is not unusual. An older grant frequently describes a service without attempting to calculate every circumstance in which it may be performed."

"Then the circumstances change while the words remain the same," Garth said.

"Precisely."

"And someone must decide what they mean."

"Someone with lawful authority," Lord Osmund replied.

Garth looked toward him.

"Of course."

Orryn could not tell whether they disagreed.

The Justiciar spoke in a dry, careful manner. He seemed to regard each word as something that might later be repeated in evidence.

Garth, by contrast, moved quickly from one question to another.

He asked how much timber Alaric's men used, how often ditches were cleared, and whether carts paid more in spring than in autumn.

Alaric answered some questions readily.

Others he answered with questions of his own.

When Ser Lyman asked whether the present toll receipts covered the cost of repairs, Alaric gave a short laugh.

"If they did, I should not have written to Highgarden."

Garth considered the papers.

"Could the obligation be divided among those who use the road?"

"It already is, in part."

"Could the arrangement be extended?"

"By whose order?"

Garth looked at Ser Arron.

The Seneschal replied.

"Such a change would require the agreement of the parties concerned, or a judgment upon their existing obligations."

"Then we should establish those obligations."

"That is what we are doing."

Orryn saw Garth's fingers tighten around the paper.

Only briefly.

"Yes," the heir said. "It is."

Ser Arron continued, as though nothing had happened.

"We may prepare a recommendation once the information is complete. Lord Leo will decide whether further action is warranted."

Alaric settled back in his chair.

"Then we are still some distance from an answer."

"Some distance from a final answer," Ser Arron corrected.

"At Grassfield, the road will not wait for your final answer."

Garth looked up.

"No. It will not."

There was no laughter in his voice now.

He turned to Ser Lyman.

"Could we at least send someone to inspect the worst stretch?"

"That could be arranged."

"Then arrange it."

Ser Arron spoke before the Treasurer could answer.

"With Lord Meadows's agreement, certainly. An officer can make an inspection and report what he finds."

Garth looked toward Alaric.

"Would that be acceptable?"

"It would."

"Good."

Ser Arron wrote a brief note.

Alaric watched him.

Orryn wondered why Garth had not written it himself.

*

The discussion continued for some time.

Lord Osmund examined the copy of the old grant. Ser Lyman asked for a list of expenses. Ser Arron considered which officer could be spared for the journey east.

Orryn began to grow restless.

He had been standing so long that his left foot ached. The chamber was warmer than it had seemed upon entering, and the open window admitted little air.

He found himself thinking of Matthos in the practice yard.

By now the boy might have ridden before Moryn.

Perhaps he had fallen.

Perhaps he had done well.

Orryn hoped it was the latter, though he would have enjoyed hearing Matthos explain the former.

His attention returned when Lord Osmund mentioned Bitterbridge.

"The Caswell rolls are being examined in another matter," the Justiciar said. "It may be useful to consider whether similar exemptions exist along the eastern road."

Alaric looked up.

"The Rowan toll?"

"Among others."

Ser Arron set down his pen.

"We have asked Lord Armond for copies of certain entries. No judgment has been made."

Garth turned toward Alaric.

"You have heard of it?"

"Lord Armond is my father-in-law. I have just come from Bitterbridge."

"Then you know something of the difficulty."

"I know that Lord Rowan's grain carts have long passed without the toll paid by other merchants."

Ser Lyman looked interested.

"That is the account we have received."

"And I know that Lord Rowan considers the exemption an old right."

"Has he produced the grant?" asked Lord Osmund.

"I cannot say."

"Then the matter remains open."

Garth leaned forward.

"The difficulty is not whether Lord Rowan possesses rights. Of course he does. The difficulty is determining which rights are established, and by whom."

Alaric answered carefully.

"An old right does not cease to exist merely because a clerk asks for proof."

"No," Garth said. "Nor does a claim become true merely because it is old."

Lord Osmund raised one finger.

"A claim may also be established by long and recognised custom, even where the original instrument is lost."

Garth turned toward him.

"Yes. I have not suggested otherwise."

"I wished only to make the distinction plain."

Ser Arron folded the letter before him.

"We should avoid treating the Caswell matter as decided while the relevant documents are still being gathered."

"Agreed," said Garth.

He did not sound entirely pleased.

Alaric regarded the heir for a moment.

"Lord Rowan was at supper last night."

Garth looked up.

"Was he?"

"He spoke of the matter."

"I imagine he did."

"He does not appear disposed to surrender his exemption."

"No lord is disposed to surrender an exemption he believes belongs to him."

Alaric smiled faintly.

"That includes me."

"Then I hope you will assist us in establishing yours."

The answer came without a pause.

For a moment Alaric looked surprised.

Then he laughed.

"Very well, ser. I shall do my best."

Garth smiled too.

Orryn thought the heir seemed more comfortable with Alaric when they were arguing than when they were agreeing.

*

A servant brought fresh water.

Ser Lyman drank and turned another page of his accounts. Lord Osmund consulted a note prepared by one of his clerks.

Ser Arron spoke quietly with Garth.

Orryn heard only part of it.

"...may be gathered by correspondence."

"And how many months will that take?"

"Fewer than a quarrel with three of your father's bannermen."

Garth said something too softly for Orryn to hear.

Ser Arron replied.

"If Lord Leo wishes to compel the production of the rolls, he may do so. Until then, we can request them."

There was a brief silence.

Garth leaned back.

"Very well."

Alaric had turned toward the window and appeared not to be listening.

Orryn wondered whether that was deliberate.

Ser Arron returned to the table.

"Lord Meadows, I believe we have enough for the present."

Alaric rose.

"Then I thank you for your attention."

"You shall receive a letter when the inspection has been arranged."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward we shall consider what the officer reports."

Alaric looked toward Garth.

The heir stood.

"I would like to see this matter settled properly. Your roads are important to more than your own tenants."

"They are," Alaric agreed. "And the tenants bear the burden of keeping them passable."

"I understand."

"I hope so."

For a moment Orryn feared Alaric had been too sharp.

Garth only inclined his head.

"I shall remember it."

He offered his hand.

Alaric took it.

"I am grateful for your time, ser."

"And I for your trouble in coming."

As they turned to leave, Ser Arron addressed Orryn.

"Master Grandison."

Orryn stopped.

"Ser?"

"Do not forget the copies."

Orryn looked at the table.

The blue-corded packet remained beside Lord Osmund's book.

He flushed and hurried to collect it.

Ser Arron smiled.

"Documents are more easily lost in a castle than on a road."

"Yes, ser."

"Though I should advise you not to test either proposition."

Orryn bowed and followed Alaric out.

*

Outside, the air felt wonderfully cool.

They crossed the inner court beneath a sky streaked with high white clouds. Servants were carrying baskets of rushes toward the hall. Two men struggled to manoeuvre a narrow cart around a fountain, arguing over which wheel ought to be lifted.

Alaric walked slowly, his hands clasped behind his back.

Orryn carried the packet.

For a time neither spoke.

Then Alaric said, "What did you make of them?"

Orryn looked up.

"Ser Arron?"

"All of them."

"They knew a great deal about roads."

Alaric laughed.

"They knew a great deal about what they wished to learn."

"Ser Lyman seemed fair."

"He may be."

"And Lord Osmund."

"He is careful."

"Is that good?"

"In a Justiciar? I should hope so."

They passed beneath an arch opening toward the gardens.

Orryn hesitated.

"Did Ser Garth help you?"

Alaric considered before answering.

"He listened."

"Will he make the road better?"

"Perhaps."

"Then why did you seem displeased?"

Alaric stopped beside a low wall, where roses climbed around a carved stone pillar.

"I am not displeased with him."

Orryn waited.

"He sees things clearly," Alaric continued. "Clearer than some men who have ruled their lands for thirty years. But he sees them from Highgarden."

"Is that wrong?"

"No. He must, if he is to rule it."

Alaric looked toward the gardens.

"The trouble is that a road may look very different when you stand upon it."

Orryn thought of the ruts near Grassy Vale, the men hauling stones into place, and the carts that passed over the repairs without slowing.

He thought of Ser Lyman's neat figures upon the table.

Perhaps Alaric was right.

Yet the road had looked rather different to Orryn when they had ridden it, too. From horseback, he had mostly been concerned with keeping the packhorse clear of the deepest holes.

Alaric began walking again.

"Come. We should see what has become of Matthos."

Orryn followed him toward the lower galleries.

The sound of steel carried from the practice yard, sharp and regular beneath the voices of boys and men.

As they approached, Orryn saw several squires gathered around the riding enclosure. A groom led a grey horse past the railings. Beyond him, a knight was calling instructions to someone Orryn could not yet see.

Alaric quickened his pace.

Orryn hurried after him, the packet of papers still held beneath his arm.

Somewhere beyond the arch, Matthos was trying to prove himself worthy of Highgarden.

And Orryn was eager to see whether he had succeeded.
