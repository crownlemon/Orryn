Rook knew him. That was something. The dun gelding put his nose over the half-door of the third stall when Orryn came in out of the yard, and blew warm breath down his neck, as if to say that he, at least, had no opinion about geese.

It was the squires' custom to see to their own mounts after the lesson, and the stable at Grassfield was long and low and smelled of hay and horse-sweat and leather, with swallows' nests in the rafters and a barred window at either end. Orryn took a curry-comb from the nail and began on Rook's flank. His ribs hurt whenever he lifted his arm. He lifted it anyway, because the hurt was better than thinking, and thinking was what he had been avoiding since the yard.

Same time tomorrow, Goose.

That was what rankled, more than the blow, which was only a bruise, more even than the name. He had stood there with his mouth shut and his sword in the dirt and let the whole yard have it. And Matthos was his cousin. A second cousin, but a cousin; Matthos himself had used the word twice, in that bright false voice, and meant something quite different by it. Orryn rubbed harder. Rook shifted his weight and gave him a patient look over his shoulder.

He did not mean to do anything about it. He was not such a fool. But he meant to know. If he had learned one thing from his father it was that a man who walked into a room without knowing who stood in it paid for the walking, and sooner or later he would have to walk into a room with Matthos Meadows in it. The trouble was how. He could not simply ask. Perkin would tell the kitchen by supper, and the kitchen would tell Elyce, and Elyce would tell her brother, with a gander the size of an ox.

"Easy on the near flank, boy. He's got a bruise there from the road." A man had come down the aisle with a lantern-hook over one shoulder and a hawk-glove tucked in his belt. He was lean and weathered, with grey-brown hair and a close beard, and his hands, when he laid one on Rook's neck, went very still, as if the horse had asked it of him. "Ser Wyman Latham," he said. "I keep the horses and the hawks. I did not see you at the stalls last night. You are Alaric's new one."

Orryn said he was, and that he was sorry about the bruise. Ser Wyman only nodded. He looked down the aisle to the end stall, where a big roan stood with his head high and his ears back, and Orryn saw a half-healed split in the corner of the roan's mouth.

"Master Matthos's," said Ser Wyman, following his look. "A fine horse. I have taught the boy to ride very well." He paused. "I have not yet taught him to ride gently." And then, as though he had said too much, he took his hand from Rook's neck and went on down the aisle without another word, and the hawk-glove swung at his belt.

Orryn watched him go. He thought it was probably the most honest thing anyone had said to him that day.

"I am sorry about the kitchen."

Perkin had come in behind him with a saddle on his arm and the look of a dog who has chewed a boot. He did not meet Orryn's eye. "I only told the cook. And the cat was there. And it was so funny, and I did not think it would go round the whole keep before the porridge. I'll not do it again. I swear it by the Seven."

"It is done," Orryn said. He meant it, mostly. It was easier than he expected. Then he turned back to Rook, because there was a question he badly wanted to ask, and Perkin was the one person in the stable he could not ask it of; Perkin, who plainly adored Matthos, and who would have repeated every word in the yard by noon. Perkin went away, relieved, whistling out of tune.

So he began with Baldric.

He found him in the tack room, oiling a bridle with small thorough strokes, the cuffs of his patched sleeves turned back and clean. Orryn took the other end of the bench and a rag and a bit that did not need doing, and for a time they worked together without speaking. Then he said, as if it had just occurred to him, "What does a squire owe the lord's heir? In the yard, I mean. I'd not like to get it wrong again."

Baldric's brown eyes lifted and rested on him, briefly, and returned to the leather. "That depends what he wants, and he wants different things on different days. Courtesy. A good seat on a horse. A shield carried when he's sulking." He turned the bridle. "He gives, you know. He gave me this knife at Midsummer, for the shield." It was a good knife, plain-hilted, at his belt. "He gave the cook's boy a pony once. He took it back when the boy won a race against him. Matthos gives generously, and he takes back just as generously, and the whole art of the thing is to be where the giving is." The rag moved. "He is going to be the lord here one day. I'm poor. I can't afford to be on the wrong side of anyone, and I certainly can't afford it with him." A small friendly smile. "You can afford more, being kin. But not much more."

It was a perfectly useful answer. Orryn thanked him and could not have said what Baldric had told him that Baldric had not meant to.

Piers Fallow was in the next room, hanging a saddle on its peg with a good deal more force than the saddle required. Orryn thought of five ways to begin and used none of them. In the end he said, "Does Matthos ever lose at dice?"

Piers did not turn round. "No."

"Never?"

"He loses when he's winning slowly. He doesn't like it said. He cheats, and calls it cleverness, and the rest of us have learned not to see the cleverness. I lost eleven silver stags to him one Midsummer and never once saw the dice." He hung the bridle and finally looked round, quick brown eyes narrowed, and the curls fell over his forehead. "You're asking about Matthos."

Orryn felt his face go warm. "I was only—"

"Everyone does, in the end. The first week or the first year. The only question is who they ask." Piers wiped his hands on a rag, slowly, one finger at a time. "Don't ask Haye. He'll tell you what is useful to Haye. Matthos can't bear to lose, you know. That's the whole of him. So everyone lets him win, and it is the first thing a squire learns at Grassfield. You didn't learn it. That is why he'll like you for a week and hate you for a year." He tossed the rag onto the bench. "I'm not telling you for nothing, Grandview. I don't tell anyone for nothing. I'll remember that you asked."

It was not a threat, quite. Orryn was unable to decide what else it was, and went out into the sunlight with the warm feeling of having both gained something and lost something and not being sure of the weights.

Alyn he found at the horse-trough in the yard, stripped to the waist, ducking his head in the cold water and coming up blowing like a seal. His chest was pale and long-boned and covered with bruises, some old, some of the morning. Water ran off his chestnut hair into his green eyes. "Grandview!" he said. "How are the ribs? He hits like a mule, that one. I have known him since he was eight."

It was an opening, and Orryn stepped into it as lightly as he could. "Were you like him? At thirteen?"

"Worse!" Alyn laughed, delighted, and rubbed his head with a sacking towel. "I had no one to stop me. He has Ser Lucan, and the lord's belt, and old Everard when he's sober. I put a live eel in the septon's boot once. I was seven. It seemed an interesting thing to do." He wrung out the towel. "Matthos is a good lad. He's a lion cub, you see. A lion cub is a delightful thing. You do not pull its tail." He grinned. "There was a business last Harvest, when he lost a race to the cook's boy. The cook's boy's pony went lame the next day. Nothing proved. Nobody asked. Boys." He shrugged his long shoulders. "Lose to him once, nicely. He'll love you for a week, and a week is a very long time at thirteen."

He said it with such perfect easy affection that Orryn did not know, for a moment, whether he had been told a story about a monster or a story about a child. He suspected Alyn did not know either.

"The lord's heir is not a subject for the horse-trough."

Osric Dunmere stood at the corner of the stable with a bucket in each hand and the little silver star lying on his chest. He had come up without a sound, which seemed to be his habit. He did not look at Alyn, or at Orryn. He looked at the middle distance, and his thin mouth was shut very tight.

"Osric, we're only—" said Alyn.

"I do not discuss the lord's heir." And he went on with his buckets, upright and unhurried, towards the kitchen yard. Orryn watched him go. This was the boy who had reported a loose step twice, and had promised to tell Ser Everard about the cup of the board. Matthos had struck a boy after the halt, in front of a household knight, and Osric would not say it aloud in a stable.

He added it to the list he was making. It was a short, strange list, and it did not yet add up to anything. The cook's boy's pony had been taken back, according to Baldric, and had gone lame, according to Alyn, and Orryn could not tell whether that was one story told twice or two separate cruelties. But the list was much longer than it had been at dawn.

The noon bell began to ring from the keep, slow and heavy. A small boy in a steward's livery was running across the yard towards him, red with the effort. "Master Orryn! His lordship's compliments. He says you are to come to the solar, and to wash first."

What does Orryn do?

1. Keep what he has learned to himself, and say nothing and do nothing about Matthos for now, watching quietly while he learns the keep.

2. Quietly seek Matthos out, and play the long game Alyn suggested: lose to him nicely, flatter him, and make himself a friend while the nickname is still new.

3. Look for common ground with Piers Fallow, the one boy who spoke frankly, and see whether a quiet understanding can be made between two boys who are both tired of losing to Matthos.

4. Something else of your own.
