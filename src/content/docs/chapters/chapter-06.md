"Why does she want it?"

It came out smaller than he had meant. Orryn had rehearsed it for the length of the ford, in the more courteous form of Might I ask what moves Lady Alysanne to such kindness, and by the time Ser Jon's horse was steaming against the wheel it had worn down to five words and a question mark. He heard Wat, behind him, let out a breath through his nose.

Ser Jon Penrose seemed pleased by it. He had expected, Orryn supposed, a stammer of thanks, or a refusal wrapped in manners, and a plain question was more of a treat. He turned a little in the saddle and looked at the willows as if they might have an answer, and then he scratched the back of his neck, where the auburn hair stood up in a tuft, and said, "Do you know, no one has asked me that. In twenty-two years. I'm not certain anyone has ever asked my mother's reasons, even my father, and he was married to her."

"I only meant—"

"No, I like it. It's a fair question and I have a poor answer. Give me a moment." He frowned, which on that handsome, mobile face made him look briefly like a boy shown a sum. The sun lay along his shoulders. The crimson coat had dried stiff where the spray had caught it, and at his throat the lacing of his shirt hung open on a strip of tanned skin, sweat-bright at the hollow, and Orryn found that he had been looking at it for some time. He looked at the grey horse instead, and then at his own knees.

He did not know where else to put his eyes. Ser Jon had hands like a man who earned his living with them, long and square and scarred across the knuckles, the left thumbnail gone black from a lance, and he was resting them loosely on the courser's neck. Orryn could not look at those either. It was ridiculous. He had sat across a table from this man at his cousin's wedding feast and watched him toast the whole hall with a cup of Arbor gold, and he had not felt this. He thought, with a sort of dismay, that it had been easier at a distance, with a hundred and fifty other people to look at.

"I think," said Ser Jon at last, "it is because she was insulted, and you were there."

"I did not insult her."

"No. That's why. She told me this morning that the boy was used, and that one does not blame the instrument." He gave a short laugh at the phrase, and Orryn could not tell if it was admiration or something else. "She is a Hightower on one side, you know, and a Targaryen on the other. She was brought up in a house where the way one is wronged and the way one answers it are both written down in advance. Alester wronged her. I'd say he did it by oversight, which in my mother's book is worse. She cannot repay him; he is a lord, and a host, and my brother has his daughter. So she must repay someone." He looked down at Orryn, and for the first time his smile faded to something almost thoughtful. "I think she means to be seen to be generous where she was shamed. A horse is a very small shame to put right. But it is public, and it is hers."

It was a sharper answer than Orryn had expected, and it stopped him. He turned it over. It made a sort of sense; it had the clean edges of something worked out by a person who had lived a long time inside the rules. And it also seemed, in some way, to be not the whole of it.

"Is that all?" he said.

"No." Ser Jon grinned. "That's what I think she would say if you asked her. The rest is what I think she thinks. You're going to Grassfield to serve a man who writes numbered letters. She has a weakness for men who write things down. It's why she married my father, I believe, and a weakness is a rare thing in her. She said, I thought rather oddly, I should like to know what manner of man keeps a boy that exactly." He lifted a shoulder. "Don't make anything of it. She says a great many things. She says them for the pleasure of having decided."

Orryn said nothing at all.

What he felt was not at all what he would have chosen to feel. He would have liked to be wary. Wary was what Steffon would have been, and Alyssa after him, with a sharp eye for how a gift was hung on a hook. He was wary. He could feel it in his back. But beneath it, warm and shameful as a coal in straw, was another thing, and he could not stop it: the memory of the pale eyes in the torchlight, and the narrow, upright figure in grey silk, a woman of Oldtown and of the dragon who had walked past forty people and a lord and spoken one sentence to him. You counted well. It had been cold. It had been, by every reasonable measure, a rebuke. And she had remembered his name at sunrise, and said it to her son.

Nobody had ever said Orryn's name at sunrise. He could not recall a time when anyone of her rank had said it at all, and certainly not as a thing to be put in a ledger. It was flattery, of a kind so subtle that he almost had not seen it, and he had gone and swallowed it whole. He knew that he had. He turned his face a little toward the water so that Ser Jon should not see it go warm.

Ser Jon saw it anyway.

"Seven help us," he said, gently. "You're blushing. Master Counter, you're not to look so; I'm not a lord. I'm a second son with a good horse and a bad habit of telling the truth before breakfast." He laughed, kindly enough, and not at all unkindly. "You do it very well, though. My mother would be pleased. She says the only honest people in the world are those who can't help colouring."

It was the worst thing he could have said, and the kindest. Orryn could not speak. He felt the colour climb past his collar into the roots of his hair, and he had the dreadful certainty that every person on the bench could see it, Wat and Pate and the boy on the bay, and that all of them would know the cause. They would not. He told himself so, firmly, and went on feeling it. They would think that it was shyness before a knight. They would think that it was the Hightower. That was all. Nobody could see a thing like that on a hot afternoon, from the outside. He did not quite know what he meant by a thing like that, and he did not stop to ask.

On the bay, Willem Tarn twisted his sulky mouth sideways and looked up at the willows, with the weary patience of a boy who has seen the same scene played out too many times on too many roads.

"Well," said Ser Jon, who was not troubled. "That's my account. It isn't much. I can't pretend to read her. I could never even read her account books, and she has four." He took up his reins. "The horse is in Osmund's stable, if you want him. Dun, sound, uncomplaining. Osmund will grumble, of course. He'll grumble till the boar's dead and probably after."

From the far bank, as if he had heard, Ser Osmund Sedgley shouted something across the water. It was a long and elaborate complaint, and the only words that carried were my best dun and Penrose. Ser Jon waved to him cheerfully, without turning round.

"He's fond of that horse," he said. "I told him he would be fonder of my goodwill."

Wat did not move. Pate, on Orryn's other side, was looking at the hands on the reins as if he could read there what to do. A water-bird called twice from the reeds, and the wet gravel ticked as it dried. The afternoon had worn down to the gold hour, and the shadow of the willows lay long across the road.

"Well, Master Counter?" said Ser Jon Penrose, and smiled at him. "The dun, or the cart? I'm told I'm not to press you. I'm bad at being told."

What does Orryn do?

1. Accept the horse outright. Take the dun gelding and ride it to Grassfield. You arrive mounted, on a Penrose gift, with Lady Alysanne's name in the matter. It is the simplest answer, and it lets her see you took her notice with pleasure.

2. Accept it as a loan. Say you will gladly ride it, and name how it will be returned: in the autumn, by one of Alaric's grooms, to Parchments. You keep a clean account, as Alaric would want, and you owe no more than the horse.

3. Decline politely. Thank Ser Jon and Lady Alysanne and say that Lord Alaric sent a cart, and you will ride in it. You owe nothing and defy no one, but you refuse her first gesture, and Ser Jon may carry that home as a slight.

4. Ask Wat first. Turn to the elder groom and ask whether Lord Alaric would object. Wat must answer, and his answer is Alaric's, however little he says. It shows your lord's men that you do not decide alone. It also leaves Ser Jon waiting, and he is not a man who is used to it.

5. Accept, and send your thanks. Take the horse, and ask Ser Jon to carry a message to Lady Alysanne in your own words. This answers her notice with your own, and builds something between you. It also tells her she was right to think you would be flattered.
