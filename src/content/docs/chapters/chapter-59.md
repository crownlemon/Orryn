Grassy Vale, 208 AC

The first thing Orryn noticed was that nobody had stolen his boots.

He had left them beside the foot of his bed, with the toes turned toward the door, where he could find them without a candle. They were still there when he woke, though someone had dropped a shirt across one of them.

Matthos would have hidden them.

Not every morning, but often enough that Orryn had learned to look beneath the beds before accusing anyone. Once Matthos had taken his left boot and put it in the kitchen, where the cook's boy filled it with turnip peelings. Orryn had gone all the way to morning prayers with one boot and one borrowed shoe, and Matthos had laughed until Lady Jeyne threatened to send him to the kitchens himself.

There was no Matthos now.

Orryn sat up and pulled the shirt from his boot. It was Perkin's. He knew it by the torn cuff and the smell.

The squires' chamber was already awake. Through the shutters came the scraping of a rake on the yard stones and the distant complaint of a mule. Baldric Haye stood at the washstand in his smallclothes, rubbing something into the leather of his sword belt. Perkin Fenwick was on his knees beside a chest, looking for a stocking. Osric Dunmere sat on his bed, fully dressed, with his head bowed over his morning prayers.

Piers Fallow was snoring.

"He's not moved," Perkin said, following Orryn's gaze. "He was asleep when I came in last night. He'll be asleep when we bury him."

"You came in drunk," said Osric, without raising his head.

"I came in tired."

"You knocked over the stool."

"It was in my way."

"You put it there yourself."

"Then I shall have a word with myself about it."

Baldric turned his belt over. "You broke one of its legs."

"It had three others."

"Two."

"Then it had lived a long life."

Orryn laughed. He had missed Perkin's foolishness, though he had been away scarcely a fortnight.

The chamber was not large, and seven boys had made it seem smaller. Their beds stood against the walls, with narrow chests beneath them and pegs for cloaks and belts. There was a single table by the shutters, marked with ink, candle grease and the point of a knife. A pitcher stood upon it, empty.

Now there were only five.

Alyn's bed had been stripped. The mattress remained, but someone had folded its blankets and carried them away. His peg held nothing. Orryn could still see the pale mark upon the wall where Alyn's sword belt had hung, the plaster cleaner beneath the leather.

Matthos's bedding was gone as well. His chest had been removed, leaving a patch of floor that had not been swept properly.

Orryn looked away before anyone noticed.

"Did you sleep?" Baldric asked.

"Well enough."

"You were late coming up."

"Lord Alaric took me to the lower mill."

"The mill?"

"The stop was opened."

Baldric went on rubbing his belt. "That will do the pond some good."

"Some."

"There's not enough to grind."

"No."

Baldric considered this, then set the belt aside.

"You saw the Tarly knight at Bitterbridge, then?"

"I did."

"Bertram says he was an ass."

"He was loud."

"That's the same thing in a Tarly."

Perkin emerged from his chest with two stockings, neither of which matched the other.

"Did you see him fight anyone?"

"No."

"Did he threaten anyone?"

"He threatened to cross the Mander without Lord Caswell's bridge."

"And did he?"

"Yes."

"Then he wasn't an entire ass."

"He had thirty men with him."

Perkin looked impressed. Baldric did not.

"Thirty men can cross a ford," Baldric said. "That doesn't make their captain a good one."

"Nor does rubbing your belt make you a knight," said Perkin.

"It makes me ready for inspection."

"Then I pity the inspector."

Osric finished his prayer and stood.

"Ser Everard will be in the yard at first light."

"He always is," Perkin said.

"Then we ought not keep him waiting."

"He's not a woman, Osric. We needn't bring flowers."

Orryn reached for his boots.

He had expected the chamber to feel different without Alyn and Matthos. He had not expected it to be so much quieter when the others were making so much noise.

*

Ser Everard Blythe had put away the practice shields by the time they reached the yard.

That was a poor sign.

He stood beneath the beech tree with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the boys approach. His silver hair was neatly combed, his beard trimmed and his clothes clean. He wore no sword. On the bench beside him lay six wooden practice blades and a bundle of leather straps.

The master-at-arms was an excellent swordsman when he had not been drinking. At dawn he was usually at his best.

Orryn wished he had slept later.

"Well," said Ser Everard, looking them over. "Grandison has come back to us."

"Yes, ser."

"Did you learn anything at Highgarden?"

"Some things, ser."

"Good. Perhaps you'll teach me."

Perkin grinned.

"Five," Ser Everard said. He looked from one boy to the next. "There used to be seven of you."

"Alyn was knighted, ser," said Osric.

"I was there."

"And Matthos has gone to Ser Moryn."

"I know that too. I can count, Dunmere. It's one of the few gifts the Seven gave me without charging for it."

He picked up a practice sword and tossed it to Baldric.

"Pairs. No shields. We're working on the line of the blade, not the weight of your arms. If you cannot keep the point where it belongs, it makes little difference how hard you strike."

Orryn found himself beside Piers.

Piers had grown since Orryn first came to Grassfield, though he remained soft through the shoulders and broad in the face. His hair was neatly cut. His boots were better than any other squire's in the keep, and his father had recently supplied him with a new quilted jack, the stitching close and even.

He held his practice sword too tightly.

"You're to work with me?" Piers asked.

"So it seems."

"I was hoping for Perkin."

"Why?"

"He's faster."

Orryn looked at him.

Piers seemed to realise how that sounded.

"I meant I need practice against someone quicker."

"Then you ought to have asked."

"I did."

"Well, you have me."

"Yes."

They took their places.

Ser Everard had arranged five short courses in the dust, marked by sticks. One boy was to advance through the course while the other made three measured attacks, giving enough time for the defender to find the proper response.

It was the sort of exercise Orryn understood perfectly when Ser Everard explained it.

He understood where to put his feet. He understood why a sword should be held with its point threatening the other man's face. He understood that he ought not reach farther than his legs could carry him.

Then Piers came forward.

Orryn stepped aside, brought his wooden sword across to catch the blow and found that his feet had crossed.

Piers's blade touched him upon the shoulder.

"Again," said Ser Everard.

Orryn set his feet.

Piers advanced. Orryn caught the first stroke, but the second came lower than he expected. He brought his sword down too late.

The wood struck his thigh.

"Again."

The third time he moved backward so sharply that his heel caught the marker stick. He stumbled and sat down in the dust.

Perkin laughed.

Orryn looked up at him.

"Get up," said Ser Everard.

Orryn did.

"Why did you fall?"

"I caught the stick, ser."

"I saw that. Why did you catch it?"

"I stepped backward."

"Why?"

"Piers was coming at me."

"He was meant to come at you. You were meant to stand where you could strike him. Again."

Orryn returned to his place.

This time he held his ground too long, and Piers's sword struck his ribs.

The blow was not hard. Piers had barely touched him.

That made it worse.

"You're reaching," said Ser Everard. "Your sword is out before your foot. You cannot cut a man from where you wish you stood."

"Yes, ser."

"Then stand where you ought."

Orryn tried.

He lost again.

On the other side of the yard, Baldric and Perkin were going at one another with enough force to make their practice swords crack. Baldric was heavier, but Perkin was quick, darting in and out of distance. Neither could land a clean blow.

Osric stood waiting with his blade lowered. As the eldest squire, he had taken the first turn outside the pairs. He watched the others with the solemn attention of a septon examining a disputed marriage.

Orryn wished he would look elsewhere.

Ser Everard called them together.

"Grandison. Show me."

Orryn stepped forward.

"Attack."

He raised his sword and struck.

Ser Everard moved his own blade perhaps the width of two fingers. Orryn's cut passed harmlessly beside him.

"Again."

Orryn struck harder.

Ser Everard caught the blow, turned his wrist and sent Orryn's sword away from the line. Before Orryn could recover, the older knight's wooden blade rested against his throat.

"Dead."

Orryn withdrew.

"Again."

He tried to be quicker.

The sword struck his wrist.

"Dead."

He took a breath.

"Again."

The third time he feinted at the head and cut toward Ser Everard's side.

He had seen Ser Androw Ashford do something like it at Grassfield, months earlier, when the Ashford knight had sparred with Alaric and Ser Lucan. It had seemed effortless then: a movement of the blade, a turn of the body, and the other man's defence suddenly in the wrong place.

Orryn's attempt was neither effortless nor surprising.

Ser Everard stepped inside it and struck him across the shoulder.

"Dead."

Perkin stopped smiling.

Orryn felt his face burning.

"You're opening your whole body to make a little cut," Ser Everard said. "Stand properly."

"I am trying, ser."

"I know you're trying. You're trying very hard at the wrong thing."

He placed the wooden blade against Orryn's elbow.

"Here. Don't lift it so high. Keep the point before you. When you move, move with your feet and bring the sword with you."

Orryn nodded.

Ser Everard demonstrated. He moved slowly enough that Orryn could see the work of his hips and knees, the way the blade remained steady as his weight shifted.

Orryn copied him.

"Better. Now again."

It was not better when he tried to strike.

The sword went wide.

Ser Everard caught it, turned him and put a foot behind his ankle.

Orryn was on the ground before he knew he was falling.

He heard Perkin make a small noise.

He did not know whether it was laughter.

He lay still for a moment, staring at the branches of the beech.

He had thought that Highgarden might make him better.

Not simply from seeing good knights fight, although he had seen them. He had travelled with Alaric, listened to conversations in great halls, watched Ser Olyvar's men and learned how much more there was to knighthood than the lessons at Grassfield.

He had imagined returning with something of that larger world upon him.

Instead he lay in his own familiar dust, unable to keep his feet beneath him.

"Grandison."

He got up.

Ser Everard handed him back his sword.

"Again."

Orryn hated the word.

By the time they were permitted to drink, Orryn's shirt was wet against his back and his arms had begun to tremble.

He had lost to Ser Everard five times. To Piers three times. To Baldric twice.

Perkin had beaten him once, striking his leg when Orryn mistook a feint for a full attack. The second time Orryn had managed to catch Perkin's sword, only to have the smaller boy slip around him and place a wooden point against his back.

"You're dead again," Perkin said.

"I know."

"I thought you might not. You looked surprised."

Orryn wanted to hit him.

Instead he lowered his blade and looked toward Ser Everard.

The master-at-arms was correcting Baldric's grip.

"Grandison! Dunmere! You're together."

Osric came forward.

He was seventeen, long-limbed, narrow in the face and usually so careful about his appearance that even his exercise clothes appeared folded around him. A few weeks earlier he had collapsed during Ser Lucan's terrible harness run in the noon heat, and had spent days recovering under Maester Perrin's care. Orryn had visited him in the infirmary.

Since then, Osric had taken every correction as though it were a matter of personal honour.

"Ready?" he asked.

"Yes."

They faced each other.

Osric was neither fast nor especially graceful. His movements had the stiffness of someone who had practised the same lesson so often that he feared departing from it. But he kept his blade between them.

Orryn struck.

Osric parried.

Orryn struck again.

Osric stepped aside and brought his sword down upon Orryn's shoulder.

"Point," said Ser Everard.

Orryn drew back.

"Again."

The next exchange lasted longer.

Orryn managed to force Osric backward two steps. For a moment he thought he had him. He pressed forward, cutting high.

Osric caught the blade and thrust.

The point struck Orryn in the stomach.

"Point."

Orryn bent over, more from anger than pain.

"You came too far," Osric said.

"I know."

"You might try keeping your right heel down."

"I know that too."

Osric looked at him.

"Then why don't you?"

Orryn straightened.

He knew Osric had not meant to insult him. That made the question unbearable.

"Perhaps you'd care to tell me."

"I was telling you."

"Then you've done it."

Ser Everard looked over.

"Enough talk. One more."

Orryn raised his sword.

He meant to wait. He meant to keep his heel planted and his point steady, to force Osric to come to him.

But Osric advanced slowly, with such caution that Orryn could see the opening beneath his arm.

Orryn went for it.

His blade was caught and turned. Osric struck his wrist.

The wooden sword slipped from his fingers.

It fell at his feet.

Nobody laughed this time.

That was worse than laughter.

Ser Everard walked over, picked up the sword and put it back into Orryn's hand.

"Your right heel."

Orryn stared at him.

"What's wrong with it, ser?"

"It doesn't go where you tell it."

"I do tell it."

"Then perhaps it's deaf."

A few of the boys smiled.

Orryn did not.

Ser Everard looked at him for a moment.

"You're tired. That's plain enough. But you're also angry, and anger won't make your footwork any better."

Orryn wanted to say that he had not been angry when they began.

He had been cheerful.

He had been glad to see the yard, glad to hold a sword again, glad to train beside the boys he had missed. He had thought of the great practice yard at Highgarden, of Ser Luthor Risley's hard discipline and Ser Denys Tarly's confidence, and had imagined that Grassfield might seem easier after all he had seen.

Now he could not bear the thought of another bout.

"I can go again, ser."

"You can. But you won't."

Ser Everard took the wooden sword.

"Sit beneath the tree. You'll finish with the others when I call you."

Orryn stood where he was.

"Did you hear me?"

"Yes, ser."

"Then sit."

He went.

Under the beech, the shade was thin. His right foot ached where Lord Leo's troublesome colt had stood upon it at Highgarden, though that had been several days earlier. Orryn loosened the strap of his shoe and rubbed the top of the foot.

He wanted the injury to be the reason he had failed.

It was not.

He had been falling badly before the colt ever touched him. Ser Everard had corrected his stance last spring. Ser Lucan had corrected it. Alaric himself had corrected it.

Even Alyn had once taken Orryn by the shoulders and moved him into place, laughing at the way he stood.

You're not a goose, Grandview. Stop trying to fight like one.

Orryn remembered his hands on his shoulders.

He wished he did not.

In the yard, Piers was facing Baldric.

Baldric struck him almost at once.

Piers stepped back, recovered badly and lost his balance. His sword went up instead of across, leaving his whole side open. Baldric tapped him beneath the ribs.

"Again," said Ser Everard.

Piers tried.

He lost.

The third time Baldric struck so quickly that Piers barely raised his blade.

Perkin laughed.

"Your father ought to buy you a longer sword."

Piers's cheeks reddened.

"Your father ought to buy you better manners."

"He's not as rich as yours."

"No," said Piers. "He isn't."

There was a silence.

Orryn looked up.

Perkin had stopped laughing. Baldric glanced between them, his practice sword lowered.

Piers seemed to realise what he had said. He opened his mouth, then closed it.

"Again," Ser Everard called.

Baldric raised his sword.

Piers took his stance.

Orryn watched the next three exchanges. Piers lost the first two. His blade was slow, and his shoulders tightened whenever Baldric advanced.

Then Baldric made a low cut. Piers stepped back, not far, brought his sword down to meet it and recovered the point before Baldric could strike again.

Ser Everard nodded.

"That's the one."

Piers looked startled.

"What, ser?"

"Your feet. They stayed beneath you."

Piers smiled.

Orryn looked away.

The smile annoyed him more than it ought.

He knew Piers had worked hard. Ser Wyman made him practise in the stable yard with a wooden sword when his horses were finished, and Piers often went on until his arms shook. Orryn had seen him do it.

He knew, too, that Piers had paid for none of the lessons himself. Dunstan Fallow paid Ser Wyman. Dunstan paid for the good boots and the new jack and, Orryn suspected, a good many other things besides.

A boy whose father owned mills could afford to be patient.

Orryn's own father had sent him to Grassfield to earn his place.

He did not know whether that was a fair comparison.

He did know he disliked Piers smiling.

*

The morning lessons ended with a short bout for each of them, and Orryn wished they had ended earlier.

Ser Everard called him back from the tree.

"With Fallow."

Piers came forward.

They were both tired. Piers's face shone with sweat, and his hair had fallen across his forehead. Orryn's arms felt heavy, and his right hand stung where Osric had struck him.

"Ready?" Ser Everard asked.

They nodded.

"Begin."

Orryn advanced.

Piers retreated.

Orryn knew what was wrong. He was going too quickly. He could feel himself doing it, his upper body moving ahead of his feet.

He tried to slow.

Piers struck.

Orryn caught the blow and turned it aside. His sword came across in reply.

For a moment Piers seemed unable to move.

Orryn pressed forward.

Then Piers stepped to the side, exactly as Ser Everard had shown him, and Orryn's cut went past his shoulder.

Piers struck him in the ribs.

"Point," said Ser Everard.

Orryn withdrew.

"Again."

He set his feet carefully.

Piers was watching him.

That was what angered him most. Piers was watching his feet, waiting for the mistake he knew Orryn would make.

Orryn held his ground. Piers feinted high.

Orryn moved to answer.

The low cut came under his guard and touched his thigh.

"Point."

Orryn bit the inside of his cheek.

He had not lost his balance. He had not dropped his sword.

He had done everything he meant to do, and it had still been wrong.

"Again," said Ser Everard.

The third time Orryn struck first.

He put all the strength left in his arm behind the blow. Piers caught it, but not cleanly, and the two wooden blades knocked together with a crack.

Orryn drove forward.

Piers stumbled.

Orryn thought he had him.

He struck again.

His heel slipped in the loose dust. His weight went sideways. Piers recovered just enough to push him off balance with the flat of his blade.

Orryn fell upon one knee.

Piers's wooden sword rested against his shoulder.

"Enough," Ser Everard said.

Orryn rose.

He could feel every eye upon him.

Piers lowered his sword.

"That last one was close."

Orryn turned to him.

"Was it?"

"You nearly had me."

"Then I suppose I ought to thank you."

Piers looked surprised.

"I didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant."

"Grandison," Ser Everard said.

Orryn stopped.

The master-at-arms was watching him with an expression Orryn disliked. It was not anger. Nor was it pity.

"Take the swords to the rack," he said. "Then wash. You're done."

Orryn took his own sword and Piers's.

He wanted to throw them both.

He carried them to the rack instead.

*

At noon, Ser Wyman Latham put him to work with the horses.

Orryn was grateful for the task until he discovered how much his arms hurt.

The long stable was cooler than the yard but no less dry. The straw had become a dusty mixture of broken stalks and old chaff, and the horses had begun worrying at the edges of their bedding. Water was being brought from the yard well in small, carefully measured buckets.

Ser Wyman stood beside a grey gelding, examining a split in the leather of its headstall.

"Take Rook down to the trough after the bay," he told Orryn. "Not before. The bay's been out since dawn."

"Yes, ser."

"And don't let him drink himself sick."

"No, ser."

Ser Wyman glanced at him.

"Had a poor morning?"

Orryn was rubbing Rook's neck.

"Somewhat."

"Everard still sober?"

"Yes, ser."

"Then it can't have been entirely wasted."

Orryn said nothing.

Ser Wyman returned to the headstall.

"His lessons aren't easy," he said. "And you're not a large boy."

"I know."

"You'll be larger."

"Perhaps."

"Don't sound so hopeful. I was your size once."

Orryn glanced at him.

Ser Wyman was lean rather than broad, but his arms were strong and his hands made a horse obey without appearing to move.

"Did you lose every bout?" Orryn asked.

"I lost enough."

"To Piers Fallow?"

Ser Wyman looked at him.

"No. He wasn't born."

Orryn flushed.

The knight bent over the headstall again.

"I've been watching Piers. He has improved."

"He has good boots."

"He does."

"And a good jack."

"Yes."

"And he has you to train him."

"He does." Ser Wyman pulled the leather through his hands. "He also gets up in the morning and does as he's told. Sometimes badly. Sometimes less badly. Those are the lessons he pays for."

Orryn looked down at Rook.

The horse nudged his arm.

"I wasn't saying he didn't work."

"You weren't saying much of anything."

Ser Wyman set down the headstall.

"Bring me the narrow awl, will you?"

Orryn fetched it.

The matter was finished.

That was another thing he disliked about Ser Wyman. A man could be made to feel foolish without being rebuked.

Rook, at least, was glad to see him.

The dun lowered his head as Orryn approached, then turned to present a place beneath his mane where he liked to be scratched. Orryn obliged, and felt the familiar weight of the animal leaning gently against his hand.

He knew Rook.

He knew when the horse was restless, when he wanted water, when he had an uncomfortable pebble beneath a shoe. He knew how to make him stand while another boy approached with a saddle, and how to keep him from nipping a careless groom.

The horse did not seem to mind that Orryn had lost his bouts.

"Come on," he murmured.

Rook followed him without complaint.

By the time they reached the trough, Orryn's temper had eased a little.

He wished it had eased more.

*

It was Perkin who found him after the midday meal.

Orryn was sitting beside the harness room with a rag and a pot of oil, cleaning the leather straps of Alaric's travelling saddle. The saddle had been dusty when they returned from Bitterbridge, and Ser Wyman had instructed him to attend to it before evening.

Perkin sat on an overturned bucket.

"You've made a mess of yourself."

Orryn looked down at his hands.

"They're oily."

"Not your hands. In the yard."

Orryn bent over the saddle.

"Thank you."

"You shouldn't have let Fallow see you angry."

"Why not?"

"Because he'll talk about it."

"Piers doesn't talk much."

"He will now."

Orryn looked up.

"Did he say something?"

"No."

"Then how do you know?"

Perkin shrugged.

"He beat you."

"He did."

"Three times."

"I can count."

"I thought perhaps you'd forgotten, since you kept going back for more."

Orryn threw the rag at him.

Perkin caught it and laughed.

For a moment Orryn laughed too, though he had not meant to.

"He's getting better," Perkin said.

"I noticed."

"Not much better."

"Better than me."

Perkin looked at him.

"Today."

Orryn rubbed a hand across his mouth.

"That doesn't help."

"I wasn't trying to help."

"Then what were you doing?"

"Talking."

Perkin stretched his legs.

He was fifteen, a year older than Orryn, and had always seemed smaller than his age because he moved so quickly. His sandy hair was cropped unevenly, his face freckled, and one of his front teeth sat a little apart from the others. He had been the first boy at Grassfield to make Orryn laugh without asking for anything in return.

He could also be very tiresome.

"Matthos would have enjoyed it," Perkin said.

Orryn stared at the saddle.

"He would."

"He'd have told everyone."

"He usually did."

"He'd have told his father."

"Probably."

"And then he'd have told you that you were standing wrong."

"He always did."

Perkin grinned.

"I almost miss the little bastard."

Orryn looked up sharply.

Perkin saw it.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"He's a bastard when he's here."

"He isn't a bastard."

"You know what I mean."

Orryn returned to the saddle.

Perkin watched him for a moment.

"Has Highgarden made you fond of him?"

"No."

"Good. I should hate to think you'd brought back some disease."

Orryn laughed despite himself.

Perkin leaned back against the wall.

"Is he doing well there?"

"I think so."

"Does he know anyone?"

"Some. There's another squire, Will Risley, who showed him about."

"And is he an ass?"

"Will?"

"Matthos."

"Yes."

Perkin nodded, satisfied.

"Then he'll be happy."

Orryn smiled.

He thought of Matthos in green, sitting among the young men of Highgarden, trying to appear older than he was. He thought of Gyles Peake calling him names and Ser Denys Tarly towering over the company, and Matthos laughing at remarks he did not wholly enjoy.

At Grassfield, Matthos had possessed the certainty of his father's house. He could be cruel, and there would still be a place for him at the high table.

At Highgarden he was only one more boy trying to be noticed.

Orryn had felt a certain satisfaction in seeing it.

He was less pleased with the memory now.

"He's well enough," he said.

Perkin stretched.

"Good."

They sat for a while in companionable silence.

Then Perkin said, "I do wish Alyn were here."

Orryn's hand stopped.

"Why?"

"He'd have made a song about you."

Orryn threw the rag again.

This time Perkin missed it.

*

Supper was taken in the great hall, where the shutters stood open and the air scarcely moved.

Grassfield was not Highgarden. There were no hundred candles, no long ranks of attendants, no musicians playing beneath painted ceilings. The hall had plain stone walls, a broad hearth, and a dais from which Lord Alaric's family could look down upon everyone who ate under their roof.

Orryn had thought of it often while he was away.

He was glad to be back.

He was less glad when he came to the squires' table.

Piers had taken Alyn's place.

There was nothing remarkable about it. Piers had arrived first and sat down on the bench nearest the door, where Alyn had habitually sprawled with one leg stretched into the passage.

It had never been Alyn's place by law. There was no written order assigning it to him, and nobody had ever been forbidden to sit there.

Yet Orryn stopped when he saw Piers occupying it.

Perkin came up behind him.

"Are you going to eat standing?"

"No."

"Then move."

Orryn sat opposite Piers.

There was a bowl of onions and barley between them, a loaf of coarse bread, and a dish containing the remains of some boiled meat. The kitchen had been stretching the better stores while the dry weather continued, and the boys had learned to watch how much they took.

Osric stood at the head of the table.

He waited until everyone had seated himself, then bowed his head.

"May the Father judge us kindly, the Mother keep us in mercy, and the Smith strengthen our hands in honest labour."

Perkin looked at Orryn.

Orryn kept his face straight.

"May the Warrior grant us courage and the Maiden preserve us from dishonour. May the Crone show us wisdom, and may the Stranger pass us by."

"Amen," said Baldric.

They sat.

Perkin reached immediately for the bread.

"Honest labour," he said, tearing off a piece. "I hope the Smith was watching Grandison today."

Orryn took the bowl of onions.

"If he was, perhaps he'll give me something better tomorrow."

"He gave you Osric," said Perkin. "What more could you want?"

Osric frowned.

"I beat you last week."

"Then the Warrior wasn't watching."

Piers laughed.

It was a pleasant sound, which annoyed Orryn because he had no good reason to dislike it.

Baldric began dividing the meat.

"So," he said, "Highgarden."

"What of it?"

"Tell us."

"What would you know?"

"Everything."

"Then we'll be here until morning."

"We've no hay to fetch," said Perkin. "There isn't any."

"Did you see the lord's daughters?" Piers asked.

"Lord Leo's?"

"Any daughters."

Orryn considered.

"I saw Lady Arlanna, his wife."

"She's not a daughter."

"She must have been once."

Perkin snorted into his bowl.

"That's better. He's brought back Highgarden wit."

"How many knights?" Baldric asked.

"More than I could count."

"Did you see Ser Olyvar?"

"Yes."

"Did he train in the yard?"

"I saw him there. Ser Luthor Risley was judging Matthos's riding."

"What was he like?"

"Risley?"

"Olyvar."

Orryn thought of the Tyrell knight's dark green coat, the handsome face and the easy way he stood among men who were older and more experienced than himself.

"He looks like a man who expects to be followed."

"Is he good with a sword?"

"I don't know. I didn't see him fight."

"Did you speak to him?"

"A little."

Baldric looked disappointed.

"You spoke to a son of Lord Leo and didn't ask him how he fights?"

"He was speaking to Lord Alaric."

"You could have asked afterward."

"Perhaps you should go and ask him yourself."

"I might."

Orryn believed him.

Baldric had always been serious about earning his spurs. His father was a poor landed knight, and no one at Grassfield pretended that knighthood would be enough to make him wealthy. Baldric would need service, patronage and a reputation that could be carried beyond the Grassy Vale.

Orryn understood the ambition. He did not always like the manner in which Baldric pursued it.

Piers reached for the meat.

"What were the other squires like?"

"There was Will Risley," Orryn said. "He's older than Matthos, and he knows the household."

"Is he good?"

"I haven't fought him."

"Does he ride well?"

"He seems to."

"What sort of answer is that?"

"The only one I have."

Perkin leaned forward.

"What did Matthos do?"

Orryn smiled.

"He made a fool of himself over some wine."

"Good."

"He lost at dice."

"Better."

"And he was made to work in the tackroom."

"Best of all."

"He's a squire," Osric said. "A squire ought to work."

"Of course he ought," Perkin said. "But Matthos has always thought his chief labour was telling the rest of us how poorly we worked."

Baldric laughed.

Piers smiled into his cup.

Orryn found himself smiling too.

He remembered Matthos standing in the yard at Highgarden, sleeves turned back, trying to look untroubled while Ser Luthor Risley watched him with the patience of a man who had seen a thousand boys more important than they were clever.

"He'll learn," Orryn said.

"Will he?" Perkin asked.

"He might."

"You've greater faith than Osric."

"I have faith," Osric said.

"Yes. You mention it often."

They laughed.

Then Piers asked, "And Alyn?"

The question came so naturally that Orryn looked up.

"What of him?"

"Did you hear where he'd gone?"

"Not certainly."

"Not to Highgarden?"

"He might have been offered a place there. I don't know what he decided."

Perkin tore another piece of bread.

"Longtable, I should think. His mother will want him home."

"His mother wants him anywhere he can be seen," Baldric said. "That's what Bertram says."

"Bertram says everyone ought to be somewhere else," Perkin answered.

Orryn did not laugh.

He was remembering the way Alyn had sat at this table, with his cup concealed beneath the bench and his long legs stretched across the passage. Alyn had been eighteen when Alaric knighted him, taller than any of the other boys, with chestnut hair that never remained combed and a smile that could make the whole table attend to him.

He had been generous when it pleased him. He had been cruel without seeming to notice. He had made Orryn feel foolish, grateful, ashamed and happy, sometimes within a single hour.

Orryn could picture the exact place where Alyn's elbow used to rest.

Piers's elbow was there now.

"Did you say farewell to him?" Osric asked.

Orryn glanced up.

"Yes."

"Properly?"

"What do you mean?"

"Did you take his blessing as a knight?"

Perkin groaned.

"Seven save us. Is there a way to say farewell improperly?"

"There are duties between a knight and the household he leaves."

"He was knighted," Orryn said. "Lord Alaric gave him his leave."

"I know that."

"Then what do you want to know?"

Osric looked puzzled.

"Nothing. I only asked."

Orryn returned to his food.

He was ashamed of the sharpness of his answer. Osric had meant nothing by it.

That, too, was becoming an irritation. Everyone seemed able to say perfectly ordinary things that Orryn could not bear to hear.

At the high table, Lady Jeyne was speaking with Maester Perrin. Lord Alaric had not yet come down. Leonette sat beside her mother, picking at her meal, while Elyce leaned forward to watch the servants enter.

Orryn could see the place where Matthos usually sat.

It was empty.

He had been glad when Matthos went away. He had imagined the freedom of it: no more insults at breakfast, no more arguments over horses, no more being reminded that he was only a younger Grandison serving in another boy's father's house.

Now the chair stood empty, and Orryn found he wished Matthos were in it.

Not forever.

Not even for the whole supper.

Only long enough to hear him complain that the meat was dry.

Piers was telling a story about the lower mill. Orryn had missed the beginning.

"...and Hob told him that if he wanted the wheel to turn, he'd best find a river to put beneath it."

Perkin laughed.

"To your father?"

"To his clerk."

"That's nearly as good."

"Father laughed when he heard."

"Of course he did. It's his mill."

"It's Lord Alaric's land," Baldric said.

"The mill is ours," Piers replied.

"The lower mill," Baldric said. "Not the Alder Mill."

Piers looked at him.

"I know which mill my father owns."

"He holds the other by lease."

"Yes."

"And the rights are disputed."

"They're written."

"Not clearly enough."

Perkin looked from one to the other.

"Must we eat with lawyers now?"

Baldric ignored him.

"Your father says the exemption belongs to Haye."

"And my father says the lease doesn't say that."

"Because the clerk left it out."

"Then perhaps the clerk ought to be beaten."

"Perhaps he ought."

Orryn watched them.

He knew the lease. He had read it with Baldric, and he knew that the men of Eaglegrave had been promised their old milling exemption without the document making clear who bore its cost.

He had meant to ask Alaric about it.

Then the journey to Highgarden had come, and Bitterbridge, and the great court, and the strange things he had seen there.

He had not asked.

Baldric glanced at him.

Orryn looked down at his bread.

Perkin reached across the table and took the last piece of meat.

"Well," he said, "if everyone who has a grievance over a mill must fight, I hope they do it after supper."

Piers laughed.

Baldric did not, but he let the matter drop.

For a while the talk turned to horses, and the prices at the last market, and whether Lady Leonette would truly take the grey mare to Tumbleton. Piers thought the mare was a waste of a good animal. Perkin thought Leonette would be a greater waste of a good husband.

Orryn found himself defending her.

"She's cleverer than you think."

"That wouldn't be difficult," Perkin said.

"Ser Garlan seems a decent man."

"Then she ought to marry him quickly, before he notices."

Orryn laughed.

The laughter felt good.

He had not realised how much he wanted to laugh with them.

*

After supper they remained at the short table until the servants began clearing the dishes.

In the old days Alyn would have found a reason to keep everyone there. A flask of wine, a game of dice, some tale about a girl at Longtable or a knight who had made himself ridiculous at a tournament.

Matthos would have insisted upon having the last word.

Tonight Perkin kept them laughing, and Piers answered more readily than he once would have dared. Baldric listened, occasionally adding something sharp enough to make them all stop. Osric protested at the worst jokes, though he seldom left the table before the others.

Orryn sat among them.

He was not happy in precisely the way he had expected to be.

His arm hurt. His right foot throbbed beneath the table. He could still feel the blows from Piers's wooden sword, and every time he remembered Ser Everard calling him dead, a fresh little wave of anger rose through him.

He resented Piers for winning.

He resented Perkin for laughing.

He resented Osric for knowing what he had done wrong.

Most of all he resented the knowledge that Ser Everard had been right.

He could tell himself that he was younger and smaller than the others, but Piers was hardly a great warrior, and Perkin was scarcely bigger. He could blame the sore foot, the heat, the journey, the dust.

He knew none of those things explained why he had failed so often.

Perkin leaned back.

"We ought to have dice."

"We've none," said Baldric.

"Matthos took the good ones."

"He did not," Osric said.

"He would have."

Piers reached into his pouch.

"I have a pair."

Perkin stared at him.

"Since when?"

"Since my father bought them."

"Does your father buy everything?"

"Not everything."

"Can he buy us some wine?"

Piers laughed.

"He might."

"Then I begin to see the use of him."

Orryn looked at the little bone dice in Piers's palm.

Alyn had owned a pair much like them, worn smooth at the corners. He had always thrown them with his left hand, though he used his right for a sword. Orryn had noticed such things about him, foolish things that no one else seemed to care about.

He missed him so badly for a moment that he could not speak.

Perkin was calling for a wager.

Osric objected. Baldric said a copper would do no great harm. Piers began arranging the dice upon the table.

Orryn watched them.

He thought of Matthos at Highgarden, in the company of boys who did not know how easily he could be wounded. He thought of Alyn, somewhere beyond the roads of Grassy Vale, perhaps already serving another lord or sitting at another table.

He wondered whether either of them was thinking of Grassfield.

He could not imagine Alyn missing him.

Matthos might miss having someone to torment.

The thought was unkind.

It also made him smile.

"Grandison," Perkin said. "Are you playing?"

Orryn looked up.

"What's the stake?"

"A copper."

"I haven't a copper."

"Then you may owe me."

"That's how Fallow's father gets his mills," said Baldric.

Piers rolled his eyes.

"Are you going to say that every time?"

"Until it stops being true."

"Then you'll be saying it a long while."

Perkin laughed and pushed the dice toward Orryn.

"Come on."

Orryn took them.

He threw.

The dice struck the wood, rolled beneath Baldric's cup and came to rest.

Two ones.

Perkin howled.

"Even at dice!"

Orryn stared at the little white cubes.

Then he laughed.

He laughed because it was ridiculous, because Perkin was laughing, because Piers had nearly spilled his drink, and because for the first time all day the sound came without effort.

They played until the candles were shortened and one of the servants told them to go.

Orryn lost three coppers he did not have.

Perkin insisted upon recording the debt.

Piers offered to lend him the money.

Orryn refused.

He did not mean to sound as angry as he did.

Piers put his purse away without another word.

*

The chamber was hot when they returned.

Perkin had taken the best part of the wash water. Baldric complained. Osric knelt to pray. Piers sat upon his bed, working a knot from the laces of his boots.

Orryn undressed carefully.

His body had begun to stiffen. He had bruises upon both arms, a tender place beneath his ribs and a sore right wrist where Osric had struck it.

He rubbed the wrist and looked at the floor.

There was just enough room between the beds for a boy to take a proper stance.

He set his feet apart.

Right foot behind. Left forward. Weight held evenly.

He remembered Ser Everard's instructions.

Don't reach farther than your legs can carry you.

He made the movement without a sword.

His foot went too far.

He tried again.

This time his weight fell upon his heel.

He tried a third time.

He could not tell whether it was better.

"What are you doing?" Perkin asked from his bed.

"Nothing."

"You're stamping."

"I'm not."

"You are."

Orryn tried again.

"Grandison."

"What?"

"Some of us would like to sleep."

Orryn stopped.

In the dim light he could make out Alyn's empty bed and the bare patch of floor where Matthos's chest had stood.

He had spent the whole day wanting to be better than he was.

He had lost to Piers Fallow. He had lost to Osric, and Baldric, and Perkin. Ser Everard had knocked him down so many times that he had begun to dread the sound of his own name.

Tomorrow they would train again.

He did not know whether tomorrow would be any different.

From his bed, Perkin said, "If you're practising for the yard, you ought to do it in the yard."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

There was a pause.

Then Perkin added, "Matthos would have told you the same thing."

"He'd have said it sooner."

"He'd have said it louder."

"And he'd have been wrong."

"Of course."

Orryn smiled in the darkness.

He climbed into bed and pulled the thin blanket over himself.

Osric finished praying. Baldric set his belt upon its peg. Piers blew out the candle.

For a little while the chamber was full of small, familiar sounds: someone shifting beneath a blanket, a chest closing, Perkin muttering because he had forgotten where he put his stockings.

There were only five of them now.

Orryn listened until the others grew quiet.

He still wanted to be back at Highgarden. He wanted to see Alyn again, wherever he had gone. He wanted Matthos to come into the chamber and start an argument over nothing.

He wanted, most of all, not to lose again tomorrow.

At last he turned upon his side.

His right foot hurt. He moved it beneath the blanket, finding the position Ser Everard had shown him.

Then he moved it again.

From the darkness came Perkin's voice.

"Grandison."

Orryn lay still.

"If you're fighting someone under there, tell him to surrender."

Orryn covered his mouth.

He tried not to laugh, and failed.
