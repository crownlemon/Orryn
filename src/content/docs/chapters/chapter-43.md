The first thing Lady Ashford cost Grassfield Keep was six measures of oats.

That was what Baldric Haye said, at least. Orryn had counted four, but Baldric had counted the packhorses as well, and the grooms were already arguing about where those would be put.

They were at Fenwick's stables, a little way out of Grassy Vale, where Lord Alaric had arranged to borrow stalls for the visitors from Longtable. The yard was broad enough for twice as many horses as it held, but one of the roofs had been patched with thatch which was still pale and new, and half the mangers were empty. A boy was scraping the last of the old hay from a loft.

Ser Fenwick himself stood with his thumbs tucked into his belt, listening to Baldric explain how many animals were expected.

"Four was the number," the knight said.

"Four was the number before Lady Ashford joined them," Baldric answered.

"And her ladyship cannot come without horses?"

"Not from Ashford, ser."

"She may come on six legs if she chooses. My stalls are still four."

Orryn had been sent with a note from Verrall, the steward at Grassfield, promising payment for the animals' fodder while they were kept at Fenwick. He took it from his sleeve, though Ser Fenwick had read it once already.

"Lord Alaric will pay for what they eat, ser."

"I know what your lord will pay. It's what I have to feed them that concerns me."

Behind the knight, two men were carrying a broken manger out into the sunlight. One held it by the sound end. The other was trying not to put his hand through the rotten boards.

Orryn thought of Perkin Fenwick, who slept across the squires' room from him at Grassfield and had spent the previous evening making jokes about his family's hospitality. He had said nothing about the broken manger. Perhaps he did not know it was broken.

Baldric glanced at the roof.

"Six under cover, if the little shed is cleared."

"The little shed leaks."

"It hasn't rained in weeks."

"Does that mean it never will?"

Orryn looked up at the empty sky. It was white with heat from one horizon to the other.

"We could keep the pack animals in the meadow," he suggested.

Ser Fenwick looked at him.

"With what grass?"

It was a fair question. Beyond the yard a small field stretched toward a line of willows, all its grass cropped down to brown stems.

Orryn put Verrall's note back into his sleeve.

In the end the knight agreed to six horses under cover. The others would have to go farther down the road to a farm belonging to one of Lord Alaric's tenants. Fenwick would provide the men to lead them there, but not the fodder, and he wanted the arrangement written down.

Baldric said that it would be.

On the ride back Orryn asked why Ser Fenwick had been so particular.

"About the oats?"

"About writing it down."

"Because he doesn't have them."

"He has horses."

"He has his own horses. That isn't the same."

Baldric rode on for a while, his long face turned toward the road.

"Your lord's guests will be fed whether Ser Fenwick has hay enough or not," he said. "If the bill isn't written, someone will remember it differently afterward."

Orryn remembered the mill lease and the clause that did not say who paid for the Graves corn. He had meant to go down with Verrall and read the original award before the visitors arrived. There had always been something else to do.

"Verrall will write it," he said.

"I know."

Baldric did not sound reassured.

*

The news had reached Grassfield in a letter two days earlier. Lady Odessa Merryweather was bringing her daughter Wylla and the girl's betrothed, Ser Androw Ashford, as expected. But Lady Ashford, Androw's mother, had ridden to Longtable to join them and intended to continue to Grassy Vale.

Lady Jeyne had already spent the greater part of a fortnight arranging rooms for the three visitors. Now there were four.

Orryn found her in the east passage, looking at a bed that two men had only just set down.

"Not here," she said.

The men lifted it again.

"Where, my lady?" one asked.

Lady Jeyne considered the narrow passage, the open windows, the pair of washstands waiting against the wall.

"Into the chamber at the far end. We shall move the small chest."

Marya Caswell, her cousin and companion, made a mark on the tablet she carried.

"And Ser Androw?"

"The east chamber beside the stair."

"He was promised the gatehouse."

"His mother may have it."

"Will he think himself put aside?"

"Then he may ask for his father's company instead of mine."

Lady Jeyne spoke without impatience. Marya wrote something down.

Orryn stood against the wall holding one end of a folded screen. He had carried it from the west range and was waiting to be told where it belonged.

"Master Grandison," Lady Jeyne said, "you may take that into Lady Ashford's chamber."

"Which one, my lady?"

"The gatehouse."

"But the screen will not fit the stair."

"Then it must go through the lower door."

"The lower door is too narrow."

Lady Jeyne looked at the screen. She looked at the door.

"Can it be taken apart?"

Orryn examined the hinges. They were of iron, fitted with little pins.

"I think so."

"Ask the joiner to do it. Not you. I should prefer the screen whole."

He bowed.

As he turned away, Marya said, "That is the third time we have moved that screen."

"Then it should know the way."

It was the first time Orryn had heard Lady Jeyne make a joke about the preparations.

He smiled before he could help himself.

She noticed.

"Master Grandison, you have an errand."

"Yes, my lady."

He carried the screen away.

By the time the joiner arrived, Lady Jeyne had found another use for it, and the screen remained exactly where it was.

*

The visitors came on the following afternoon, later than the first messenger had promised.

The heat had made the road slow. A rider sent ahead to announce their approach said that one of the baggage wheels had cracked at Hob's Cross, and the whole party had been obliged to stop while a carpenter fitted a new pin through the hub. The wheel would hold, the rider thought, as far as Grassfield.

Lord Alaric sent a cart with water and two men to meet them. He sent Orryn as well.

"You'll know the road now," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"Then see that the water reaches the women and not their horses. There's pond water for horses. We have little enough fit to drink."

Orryn rode Rook westward with Wat and Pate driving the cart behind him. The dun gelding had grown used to the dry roads, though his hooves needed watching, and Orryn was careful where the stones showed through the dust.

They found the travelling party beyond the Lamb's Stile, where the road dipped between two banks of bramble. The broken-wheeled baggage cart stood beneath a tree which gave almost no shade. Men in Merryweather colours were tightening straps about its load. Farther along, two armed riders carried orange pennons showing the white sun and chevron of Ashford.

There were more people than Orryn had imagined.

Lady Odessa's retainers wore the horn of plenty on their breasts. The Ashford men had their lord's colours sewn upon sleeves and saddlebags. There were serving women, grooms, a cook with a red face and a boy leading a mule that appeared to hate him.

Wat drew the cart to the side of the road.

"Water from Grassfield," he announced.

A woman in a travelling veil turned at once.

"From the well?"

"From the cellar spring, my lady."

"Then I am obliged."

She was Lady Odessa Merryweather of Longtable. Orryn knew her from Maester Perrin's descriptions and from the way everyone made room when she approached. She was not tall, but she stood very straight, and the cut of her riding gown gave her an importance which the dust had done little to diminish. Her auburn hair was gathered high beneath the veil, and a narrow gold chain lay against her throat.

"Which of you is Lord Alaric's man?" she asked.

Wat inclined his head toward Orryn.

"His squire, my lady."

Orryn dismounted and bowed.

"Orryn Grandison, my lady."

"Grandison?"

"Of Grandview."

She gave him another look.

"Then your grandmother was a Meadows. I recall the family."

"Yes, my lady."

"Good. You may see that the water is carried to my daughter first. She has been complaining of the heat since we crossed the Mander."

Orryn turned toward the nearest serving woman, who was opening the lid of the water barrel.

A girl sat on a broad-backed bay horse near the damaged cart. She was rather fat, with a plain, round face and a narrow mouth, and her expensive riding habit had been cut with great care to fit her. A line of tiny pearls bordered her cuffs. She looked miserably hot.

"Does the water have anything in it?" she asked when Orryn brought the cup.

"Nothing, my lady."

"No lemon? Mother said there would be lemon."

"It is fresh spring water."

"Oh."

She drank a little, then looked into the cup.

"At Longtable we put mint in it when the days are warm. My mother says there is nothing more disagreeable than warm water served as though it were a favour."

Orryn thought the water was very cold, and almost painfully good after the ride. He made no answer.

"Will there be a place to change before supper?" she asked.

"Yes, my lady."

"I have two gowns with me. One is pale gold, and the other is blue with a border worked in seed pearls. Mother says the blue is better for a first evening, though I think the gold is more becoming."

Orryn had never understood how a gown became a woman, or the woman the gown, but he had been instructed not to look puzzled when noble visitors spoke.

"The blue sounds very fine, my lady."

"It is. The trouble is that blue looks so common in a candlelit hall. Everyone wears it. At Highgarden they say green is returning to favour, though Mother says it never truly left."

She had scarcely looked at him throughout the conversation. Now she gave him back the cup.

"Thank you, Master Grandison."

He bowed again, privately relieved.

So this was Lady Wylla Merryweather. Orryn had heard Leonette complain of her manner, and Alyn had said that she was better once one knew her. Orryn could not yet see what difference knowing her would make to a conversation about blue cloth.

On the other side of the road a man was examining the broken wagon. He stood with one boot on the axle and an arm braced against the wheel. His doublet was velvet, as Alyn had promised, though it was dark with travel dust at the shoulders and looked too warm for the day. He had broad hands and a long, lean face. The groom beside him was speaking rapidly.

"I said it would serve until the keep."

"And if it fails at the gate, we block the road with my mother's linen," the knight replied. "Put the heavy chest on the other cart. Leave the lighter things where they are."

"There's no room."

"Then make room. That is why I brought you."

His manner was not particularly loud. The groom went away at once.

The knight looked down from the wheel and found Orryn watching.

"You are the Grandison squire."

"Yes, ser."

"Ser Androw Ashford. My thanks for the water."

Orryn bowed.

Androw descended from the wheel, tested it once with his foot, then looked along the road toward Grassfield.

"How far?"

"Less than an hour, ser."

"With these carts?"

"Perhaps longer."

The knight gave a short breath that might have been a laugh.

"A proper answer. My father would have told me half an hour and expected the road to oblige him."

Before Orryn could reply, a woman called Androw's name.

Lady Ashford had been sitting on a low travelling stool beneath the tree. She rose as her son approached. Her gown was a dark, practical wool, relieved by a fine belt and a silver clasp at her throat. She had taken off her veil, and her face was narrow and strong-featured, with brown hair threaded by grey. Orryn noticed that she had brought a little book from the litter and had kept one finger between its pages.

"Androw," she said, "the last chest is to go with mine. Not with Lady Odessa's."

"It will unbalance the second cart."

"Then take something off the second cart."

"What?"

"The carpets."

"They are yours."

"They can bear the dust."

Androw said nothing for a moment.

Then he turned toward the groom and repeated the order.

Orryn had not expected the knight to be so readily corrected before his own men. Androw did not seem pleased, but neither did he argue.

Lady Ashford turned her attention to Orryn.

"Grandison of Grandview?"

"Yes, my lady."

"Your people are near Nightsong, by the long road."

"Not very near, my lady. But nearer than Ashford."

"Most places are."

She closed her book.

"My brother is Lord Pearse Caron. You may have heard of him."

"I have, my lady."

"Then I hope you have heard something better than what men say of him after a third cup."

Orryn felt his face grow warm.

He had heard many things of House Caron. He did not know which of them she meant.

"I have not met him," he said.

"No. Of course not."

She looked toward her son, who had gone back to the wheel.

"Will your lord receive us today, Master Grandison, or have we offended his household by coming so late?"

"He sent the water, my lady. He is expecting you."

"A sensible answer. Better tell him we are slower than we meant to be."

Orryn bowed and went to Wat.

They were still on the road when the first of the spare horses was sent ahead to Fenwick. Two Ashford men led them. A Merryweather groom protested that the finest of the horses ought not to be lodged apart from the house, and Ser Androw asked him whether he preferred a dry stable at Fenwick or the open yard at Grassfield.

The groom chose Fenwick.

Lady Odessa said the arrangement was inconvenient.

Lady Ashford said it was accommodation freely offered, and that they would be grateful for it.

Orryn rode ahead with the news.

*

The welcome at Grassfield took place beneath the shade of the hall steps, where Lady Jeyne had had a cloth awning hung to spare the ladies the worst of the sun.

Orryn was sent to take Rook to the stable, so he missed the first greetings. By the time he returned, Piers Fallow was carrying the bread and salt back toward the buttery, and Lady Odessa had taken Lady Jeyne's arm to go inside.

That was the way Orryn first saw the two women together: one in pale gold, the other in green, walking as though they had known one another for years.

Alyn followed them.

His mother had taken his chin between her fingers and examined his newly cut hair before the household. Orryn had seen it from the stable doorway. Alyn had borne the inspection without complaint, though his ears had turned a little red.

Now Lady Wylla walked beside him, talking.

"Mother says it will have to be done again before we return to Longtable. There is too much left behind your ears. I told her the back is worse."

Alyn looked over Wylla's head and caught Orryn's eye.

Orryn looked away quickly, but not before he saw Alyn smile.

The rooms were settled more easily than the horses.

Lady Odessa had the chamber above the hall. Lady Ashford was given the gatehouse room with the glazed window. Ser Androw accepted the smaller guest chamber in the east range, though Orryn heard him ask Verrall twice where his men were to sleep. Lady Wylla was lodged with Leonette Meadows, who greeted her with such perfect courtesy that Orryn was almost convinced they liked each other.

Then Wylla examined the little bed.

"It is rather narrow."

"It was mine when I was younger," Leonette said.

"I should have thought you were younger now."

Leonette smiled.

"I am fourteen."

"Then you are."

She touched the coverlet.

"Mother says a guest bed ought always to be made with two sheets. Is there another?"

Leonette's smile stayed where it was.

"I shall ask."

Orryn, standing with the empty water ewer, saw her turn toward the door. He did not envy the servant who would next be summoned.

The guests washed and changed, and at last the keep was quiet enough for Orryn to eat a piece of cold bread in the stable doorway.

He had scarcely finished when Verrall appeared with a slate.

"Six horses at Fenwick," the steward said.

"Yes."

"Two at the tenant's farm?"

"Two pack animals. They were led away from the road."

"And the hay?"

"Ser Fenwick wants a written reckoning."

Verrall made a small noise.

"He has one. I sent it."

"He wants it brought back to him signed."

"Does he indeed?"

Orryn waited.

Verrall rubbed his forehead.

"Very well. Tomorrow. There is always tomorrow."

The steward walked away, leaving Orryn with the last crust of bread in his hand.

He had heard those words before, about the chest in the undercroft.

He did not like them any better this time.

*

Supper was served in the great hall after the candles had been lit.

The table had required another argument over the seating. Lady Ashford's arrival had taken the chair once intended for a household knight, and the places of the ladies and the younger children had been altered twice. In the end Lord Alaric sat between Lady Odessa and Lady Jeyne, as planned. Lady Ashford sat next to Odessa, with Wylla beyond her, while Ser Androw was placed on Jeyne's other side. Leonette, Matthos and Septon Lorimer filled the remaining places.

The household knights ate below the dais with Maester Perrin, where Ser Tobin Whitlow declared himself fortunate to be near the ale.

Orryn served Lord Alaric. Alyn, by Lady Jeyne's order, stood behind his mother.

It was a good supper, though the cook had been obliged to change the fish course when the carrier from the coast failed to arrive. There was roast fowl, a pie of onions and mushrooms, two fat hares, bread still warm from the oven and a bowl of small yellow plums preserved in honey. The smell of the hares filled the hall, and for a little while Orryn wished he had been assigned a place at one of the lower tables instead of a jug.

Lady Odessa praised the food. She praised the room and the hangings and the care taken with her chamber. Each compliment had a little edge of expectation upon it. Lady Jeyne received them with a composure that Orryn admired.

Lady Ashford spoke less. When she did, it was often to ask after some detail of the estate or the people who served it.

"Your well is low, I hear."

"It is," said Alaric.

"Yet you have found water enough for so many extra horses."

"The horses drink below the yard. We have kept the cellar spring for the house."

"Wise."

Alaric looked pleased. He began to explain the sluice at the lower mill, but Lady Odessa interrupted him with a story of a spring at Longtable that had once flooded the bakehouse.

The conversation drifted toward roads and bridges.

Ser Androw mentioned that the cart road south of Ashford had been repaired after the last rains and that the work had cost far more than his father's steward had expected.

"Then he employed the wrong steward," Alaric said.

"I have sometimes thought so."

Lady Ashford looked at her son.

"It cost more because the first men laid poor stone, and the work had to be done twice."

"That too," Androw said.

"And because your father wanted a new stone arch where the old timber bridge would have served."

"That as well."

"Then perhaps the steward was not wholly responsible."

Androw smiled at her.

"Perhaps not."

Orryn had been waiting to pour. He found himself enjoying the exchange.

It reminded him of Lord Alaric at the forge, speaking of the horse he would not buy. A nobleman could be made to look foolish over an ordinary expense as readily as any groom.

Lady Odessa leaned forward.

"At Highgarden they are forever counting what the roads and bridges cost. Ser Garth Tyrell has clerks who seem to know every toll between Oldtown and Bitterbridge."

"Not every toll," said Lady Ashford. "Only those they have been told of."

"Then the others ought to be congratulated."

Lord Alaric lifted his cup.

"Who pays for these clerks?"

"Highgarden," said Androw.

"That was not my question."

Androw considered him.

"Then I imagine you know the answer, my lord. The lordship pays, as it pays for all its servants."

"Only if they are its servants."

For a moment Orryn was sure he had missed something.

Lady Jeyne took a plum from the bowl before her.

"My stepmother once told me that Ser Garth could never see a door left open without asking which man had paid for the hinge."

Lady Odessa laughed.

"Lady Brenna has a good memory."

"She does."

"And what did Ser Garth say?"

"I was not there to hear him."

Jeyne smiled and ate her plum.

Orryn remembered that Lady Brenna Caswell had been born a Tyrell. She was his lady's stepmother, married to Lord Armond Caswell of Bitterbridge, and had lived at Highgarden before becoming the second wife of the Caswell lord. He wondered whether she truly had said such a thing. Lady Jeyne had told it as though it were a family amusement, but he knew she was not always amused by her stepmother.

Ser Androw set down his knife.

"There is talk," he said, "that the officers at Highgarden would like more exact rolls of service from the lesser lordships."

"Talk where?" Alaric asked.

"Among stewards. And among men who must answer stewards."

"That is not much of an answer."

"No, my lord. It is not."

Androw did not look offended.

"A good return might save a lord trouble," he continued. "If a clerk knows what service is owed, there need be less argument when the call comes."

"And when the clerk does not know?"

"Then the lord must instruct him."

Alaric gave a short laugh.

"A lord who must instruct another man's clerk will soon have no time for his own."

"That is certainly one view."

"And what is yours, ser?"

Androw glanced toward Lady Ashford before answering. It was a small movement, so brief that Orryn might have missed it if he had not been waiting with the wine.

"My view is that a man ought to know what he owes before another man tells him."

Alaric nodded, as though this answer pleased him more.

"On that we agree."

Lady Ashford reached for her cup.

"Provided both men agree what has been owed," she said.

"That is what the records are for," Alaric replied.

"Are they?"

She asked it pleasantly. Alaric's brow moved a fraction.

Orryn thought at once of the Alder Mill. The lease said one thing, and the men who held the mill and ground at it said another. In the undercroft lay the award that might put them right. He had not read it.

He was glad that no one at the table was asking him about it.

There was a scrape of chairs below the dais as a servant brought in the next course. Someone called for more ale. At the far end of the hall Perkin was arguing with Ser Tobin over whether a partridge had one good leg or two. The moment passed, and Lady Odessa began speaking of the wedding arrangements at Longtable.

Lady Wylla brightened.

"The gowns are being made in Highgarden," she said. "Mother says the finest needlewomen are there, though I think the cut from Oldtown is more graceful. Lady Arlanna wears gowns with a narrow sleeve now, and everyone has begun copying them, but Mother says broad sleeves will be back before anyone knows it."

Orryn listened as long as courtesy required.

Then he looked at Ser Androw.

The knight was cutting his meat. He did not appear to be listening to his betrothed any more closely than Orryn was.

*

There was dancing after supper, but Orryn saw little of it. He spent most of the first figure fetching fresh candles from the buttery and missed the beginning of Ser Androw's dance with Wylla.

When he came back, Wylla was finishing a slow turn beneath the candles. She moved carefully, with all the precision of a girl who had been taught each step several dozen times. Her gown was pale gold after all. The seed pearls at her sleeves caught the light when she lifted her hands.

Leonette danced next with Alyn.

Orryn knew she would. Alyn had promised her the second dance and Elyce the third, with Orryn as witness. He did not watch the whole of the dance. The sight of Leonette's hand in Alyn's was enough to make him busy himself with the ewer, although it was not yet empty.

Alyn kept the third promise too. Elyce stepped on his foot in the second turn, and the younger girl looked so stricken that he said something into her ear and made her laugh. It was a generous thing to do. Orryn saw that much before he was sent for more wine.

When the music stopped, Lady Odessa summoned her son.

Orryn was returning along the passage when he heard her voice from the little antechamber beside the hall.

"You are eighteen."

"Yes, Mother."

"You need not sound so surprised."

"I have had some practice being eighteen."

"And very little practice being anything else, to judge by your letters."

The door stood half open. Orryn slowed, then wished he had not.

Lady Odessa was sitting on a bench, her hands folded. Alyn stood before her with his sleeves rolled to the elbow.

"Your brother has duties at Longtable," she said.

"Lymond has always had duties. He has a remarkable talent for finding someone else to do them."

"He is your elder brother."

"I had noticed."

"And your father's heir. You will remember that when you speak of him."

Alyn looked down at his hands.

"Yes, Mother."

Lady Odessa's voice softened a little.

"Lord Alaric tells me you have served him well."

"He is generous."

"He is particular. I should rather have his judgment."

Alyn did not answer.

"He also tells me you wish to leave his household."

Orryn stopped breathing for a moment.

"I do."

"Then we shall speak of where you mean to go."

"Not tonight, Mother."

"Not tonight," she agreed. "But before I leave. You cannot expect one household to keep you forever merely because you are liked there."

"Nobody has asked it to."

"Good."

Orryn went on before either could see him.

He had known that Alyn meant to leave. Alyn had told him beside the dry stream, with the stars coming out over the willows. Yet hearing the matter discussed so plainly between mother and son made it seem nearer than it had before.

He carried the wine back to the hall and poured it where he was told.

*

The next morning brought no relief from the heat.

Lord Alaric had agreed to show Ser Androw the repaired bridge and the lower pastures before the household took its midday meal. Orryn was to ride behind them. Alyn had been asked to accompany Lady Odessa on a walk through the gardens, and Ser Lucan kept the other squires in the yard.

Ser Androw rode a bay horse with a white mark between its eyes. Its tack was very fine, but the saddle had been repaired at the girth, and Orryn noticed that the stitching was new.

The knight had not worn velvet today. He had a plain riding coat, loose enough at the shoulders to move in, and an old glove on his sword hand.

They rode beneath the trees along the brook. The water was still running, though thinly, and the grass on either bank had been eaten close by cattle brought down from the dry uplands.

At the bridge, Alaric dismounted and showed Androw the stonework.

"It has stood four years," he said. "We lost the old bridge in the flood."

"Who held the obligation to maintain it?"

"My house, by custom. The tenant at the farther bank was to provide men and carts when called."

"Did he?"

"After a quarrel."

"Over the number?"

"Over whether the carts were his to send."

Androw put a hand on the stone parapet.

"And you settled it?"

"I sent my own carts. Then I reduced his rent by what they cost."

Orryn looked at his lord in surprise.

That was not how he had expected the story to end.

"Cheaper than spending a year arguing," Alaric said.

"And did the man thank you?"

"No."

Androw smiled.

"Then you saved only the money."

"I saved the bridge."

They stood looking out over the shallow water.

"At Ashford," Androw said, "the court has begun asking for the oldest grants before it hears a complaint. My father prefers witnesses, but witnesses grow old, and sons remember what their fathers wished had happened."

Alaric bent to examine a place where the mortar had cracked.

"And parchment may lie."

"Only as well as the man who writes it."

Orryn thought of Dunstan Fallow's clerk and the words as of old.

He shifted in his saddle.

Lord Alaric straightened.

"Have you come to inspect my bridges, Ser Androw, or to tell me what Highgarden will demand of them?"

The question was quiet. Orryn wished suddenly that he were farther away.

Androw did not answer at once.

"Neither, my lord. My mother wished to see the country, and I was glad to accompany her. But I have had enough of my father's accounts to know how much trouble a lord can find in a clerk's question."

"Then you know why I dislike being asked for figures without knowing who wants them."

"Yes."

"Good."

The lord turned toward the horses.

For a time they rode without speaking.

At the edge of the lower pasture Androw asked whether Matthos was old enough to serve another knight.

"He is thirteen," Alaric said.

"A useful age to begin."

"He has already begun. He has the yard here."

"A boy may learn a great deal at his father's hearth. He learns other things away from it."

"That depends on the hearth."

"And the man who takes him."

Alaric glanced across at him.

"And which man is that?"

Androw smiled, but Orryn could not see amusement in it.

"I did not come with an offer in my pocket, my lord."

"Then don't empty it."

Androw laughed at that, and after a moment Alaric laughed too.

They rode back by the orchard.

The trees were beginning to shed a few yellow leaves into the dust, though the summer had not broken. Col and another man were carrying water in small buckets to the youngest grafts. Alaric stopped to speak to them, and Ser Androw waited on his horse.

Orryn watched Col lift his cap to the lord. The old man's eyes moved across Orryn's face, but he did not smile.

Orryn remembered telling Maud more than he should have, and the way Col had looked when he learned the lord knew.

Some debts did not come with a written reckoning.

*

When they returned, Lady Jeyne was waiting in the hall with Marya Caswell and the two visiting mothers.

A table had been brought near the window, away from the heat of the hearth. Upon it lay a roll of family names, two letters and a small painted map of the roads between Longtable, Ashford and Highgarden.

Orryn recognised Maester Perrin's hand on the map. He had helped hold it flat when the maester painted the line of the Mander.

Lady Odessa had one finger upon Longtable.

"The river road is better, of course, but a wedding party cannot spend three days in mud to save a day's riding."

"It will not be three days in mud unless the rains come," said Lady Ashford.

"Which they will, precisely when they are least convenient."

Lady Jeyne looked up as Alaric entered.

"My lord. We were discussing the journey to Ashford."

"Then you will want the road, not my bridge."

"I should like to know which road you recommend."

He came to the table, and the ladies made room for him.

Orryn followed with his lord's gloves and stood aside. He could see the small red mark for Grassfield, the broader ribbon of the Mander and the route south-west to Ashford.

For a little while the discussion concerned where the wedding party might sleep and how much baggage could be carried over the smaller bridges. Lady Odessa wanted a comfortable stop halfway. Lady Ashford thought they ought to travel farther on the first day. Androw, coming in behind Alaric, suggested that the slower carts could leave ahead of the riders.

Wylla was sitting near the window with Leonette, showing her a piece of pale blue silk.

"Mother says the blue will travel badly," Wylla told her. "But the gold is worse. It creases. Do you find that?"

"I have never taken both on a journey," Leonette said.

"Then you really must. One never knows who might be received along the way."

Orryn looked away. He could hear Leonette's answer but could not make out whether she meant it kindly.

At the map Lady Odessa was speaking of Highgarden again.

"Ser Moryn has a good household. I told Lord Merryweather so when he was considering where Alyn ought to finish his service."

Alyn was not in the room.

"Ser Moryn has many seeking him," Lady Ashford said.

"Yes. And many who claim to have been promised a place."

Her tone made Lady Odessa look up.

"Surely he would not refuse a boy of a house he knows so well."

"He must refuse somebody. Unless he means to fill every chamber in Highgarden with squires."

Lord Alaric bent over the map.

"Matthos will serve where I judge he ought."

"Of course," Lady Odessa said.

Lady Jeyne placed her hand upon the corner of the parchment.

"And we are not discussing Matthos's service today. We are discussing Lady Wylla's wedding."

Lady Ashford inclined her head.

"Quite right."

For a time there was only the sound of the ladies' voices considering roads, baggage and suitable places to break the journey.

Orryn had thought the argument was over.

Then Androw said, "If the roads are entered properly in the Highgarden rolls, perhaps it will be easier to settle these journeys."

His mother turned toward him.

"Which rolls, Androw?"

"The new ones. The officers have asked for the principal crossings."

"Asked whom?"

"Several lords."

"At your uncle's bidding?"

He hesitated.

"I did not say that."

"No."

She reached for the map and rolled it a little farther toward herself.

"Then let us not give Lord Alaric to understand that we can promise what Highgarden has not promised."

There was no rebuke in her voice that Orryn could hear, but Androw's mouth tightened.

Lady Odessa smoothed the edge of the parchment.

"Well," she said, "whoever keeps the rolls, I hope he keeps the roads in better order than the men who repaired our wheel yesterday."

Lady Jeyne asked Marya to fetch the list of chambers at the stopping places. The talk returned to the wedding.

Orryn wondered whether Ser Androw had been speaking for Highgarden or merely of it. He had made the place sound very near during supper, full of men who wanted figures and rolls. Now his mother had spoken as though even an important knight might be careless with another man's name.

The map lay between them, its painted roads running in several directions.

He had expected the guests to come together because of a marriage. That was plainly why they had come. Yet they had found room to speak of bridges, accounts, squires and the ways in which one lord might become useful to another.

He understood little of it.

He understood that no one had promised Lord Alaric anything.

And Lord Alaric had promised nothing in return.

*

In the afternoon Orryn went down to the buttery to fetch the tally for the horses at Fenwick.

Verrall had finally written it out. Six horses in borrowed stalls, two pack animals at the tenant's farm, so many measures of oats and hay each day, the cost to be settled from Grassfield's accounts. There was a space at the bottom for Ser Fenwick's mark.

Orryn carried it out to the yard.

Baldric was waiting beside Rook, fastening the saddle straps.

"Did he write it?"

Orryn held up the paper.

Baldric took it and read, moving his lips only once.

"He has allowed for the pack animals."

"Yes."

"And the hay?"

"Yes."

"Good."

He handed it back.

Orryn looked toward the gatehouse, where one of Lady Ashford's women was shaking dust from a travelling cloak. Across the yard, Alyn was helping his sister onto a horse for a short ride around the lower meadows. Wylla had changed her gown again. Its sleeves were green, as she had said the fashion at Highgarden required.

Beyond them Ser Androw stood with Lord Alaric at the farrier's shed, examining a horseshoe.

It might have been any afternoon at Grassfield, except that the yard was fuller and the men carried other houses' colours.

Baldric mounted.

"Are you coming?"

Orryn put the tally safely into his sleeve.

"Yes."

He took Rook's reins and followed him toward the gate.

The paper lay flat against his wrist. It was a little thing, no longer than his hand, and it had taken two days of asking to have it written. When Ser Fenwick marked it, the extra horses would be fed and someone would know who must pay.

He wondered whether Maester Perrin and Verrall would find time for the undercroft when the visitors were gone.

The answer was still below the keep, in a chest with three locks.

But for now Baldric had reached the road, and Orryn urged Rook after him.
