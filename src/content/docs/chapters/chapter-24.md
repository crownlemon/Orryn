The solar smelled of ink and warm wax and the pears set out to dry on a board in the window. Lord Alaric Meadows sat at the long table with his back to the light, writing, and did not look up.

Orryn stood inside the door with his wet collar going cold against his neck and waited as he had been taught to wait at Grandview, with his hands where they could be seen. Under the table Lord Alaric's left foot was tapping. He was thirty years old, lean as a hound, with light brown hair that never lay down and ink on the side of his hand, and he wrote as he did everything, as though every minute of the day were owed to someone who would be asking for it back. He was Ser Steffon's cousin and Orryn's lord, and Orryn had not yet decided which of the two words he was more afraid of. At the end of the table sat Lyman Verrall, the steward, forty-four, round and sunburnt and good-humoured, with a ledger open before him. He glanced up at Orryn and then, with great care, down again.

Beside the window a map of the vale hung pinned to the plaster in Verrall's tidy hand, the orchards drawn in dotted rows and the mill-streams picked out in blue, with Grassfield no bigger than a thumbnail in the middle of it. It was a smaller room than the solar at Grandview, and a busier one. Nothing in it was for show.

"Shut the door," said Lord Alaric.

He shut it.

"You are wet."

"I washed, my lord."

"An hour ago I was told that you were being washed." The pen did not stop. "The steward's page is a truthful child. It is a fault he will have to be cured of." At the end of the table Verrall made a small noise into his ledger. Orryn said nothing, since nothing had been asked of him, and found it was a great deal easier than he had thought.

Lord Alaric finished his line, sanded it, and set the pen across the inkwell. "Sit."

There was a stool. Orryn sat on the edge of it, and his ribs let him know about it.

"The rider left at first light with my letter to Ser Wystan." It had been Lord Alaric's letter in every word but one sum, and the sum had been Orryn's, but that was not a thing to say. "If the old man comes he will be four days on the road, or six. He is not to be spoken of in the hall, or in the yard, or in the squires' chamber. Not to Osric Dunmere. Not to Perkin Fenwick, who would think it a jest, nor to any of the rest, who would think it news."

"No, my lord."

"Good." The foot tapped twice and stopped. "Now. What can you do?"

He'll ask what you can do, Matthos had said, winding the rag round his palm. Say less than that. Orryn thought that he had better say something, or he would be sitting there until the pears were dry.

"I can ride, my lord. I can keep a count. Neither as well as I should like."

"So can the horse," said Lord Alaric.

Orryn kept his face where he had put it. It cost him something in the cheek. Lord Alaric's eyes came up from the page at last, hazel, level, quite without humour, and rested on him for a moment too long.

"Someone has told you."

"Yes, my lord."

"My son."

"Yes, my lord."

Something crossed the lean face that Orryn could not read, and went; at the end of the table a ledger page turned with sudden attention. "He remembers that one," said Lord Alaric. He looked at the wall for a breath. "Verrall keeps the household book. This house eats and wears and burns and pays, and every bite of it is set down in his hand. From today, between the yard and supper, you will sit with him for an hour, or two if he can bear you. You will not be his clerk. You will be shown what Grassfield costs to keep standing, line by line, and it will not be what a boy of fourteen supposes."

"The upper granary tallies, my lord," said Verrall mildly. "He might begin there. They have never agreed with the carters. He will find them a dreadful bore, and by the end of the week be very pleased with himself."

"Pleased is a state I have no time for," said Lord Alaric.

"Yes, my lord," Orryn said. His heart gave a hard small jump, and he could not help it, and he tried to keep it from reaching his mouth. What a house cost: he had wanted to know that for as long as he could remember. At Grandview he had only ever seen it as Ser Steffon's tally, kept against him in a man's head and never shown to him, so that every shirt and every saddle had come with a number he was not allowed to read. Here he was to be given the page itself. He was not at all sure he was ready for what it would say.

"Do not look so pleased," said Lord Alaric. "It is dull." And then, in the same voice, as though it were the next line in the same ledger, "Ser Lucan Marwick came to me before the noon bell. He does not often come to me. He told me that my son struck you after Ser Everard had called halt."

The yard was a great way off from this room. From the window came the sound of a wheelbarrow, and a cook's voice, and a dog barking at nothing. Orryn found that his hands were pressed flat between his knees.

"Is it so?"

"Yes, my lord."

"Ser Everard has said nothing of it to me."

"No, my lord."

He did not say that Ser Everard had held his sword with both hands. He had not been asked, and it was no kindness to anyone to be asked a thing like that. Lord Alaric let the silence stand just long enough for Orryn to understand that he had not needed telling. "Ser Everard is a man who says little before noon," he said. "Did you touch my son?"

"Once, my lord. Before the halt. Ser Everard called it a touch."

"Hm." The foot began again, slower. "Ser Lucan finds a fault wherever there is one. It is why I keep him, and why no one loves him. My son is thirteen. He is also what comes after me. I shall deal with the one in my own time, and with the other. Not at your asking. You have not asked."

"No, my lord."

It was the answer Matthos would have approved of. Orryn thought that it was also, perhaps, a very little cowardly, and he did not know which of the two weighed more. He had come up the stair meaning to answer what he was asked and no more, as a sensible boy should before his lord. He had not reckoned on how it would feel, to stand on the lip of a thing and say only yes and no while it went by. The sun lay warm across the table and the dried pears and Lord Alaric's inky hand, and across his own wet shoulders, and he was hungry. Dinner would be set below by now. He could smell it faintly through the floor, onions and bread, and it seemed to him the most distant thing in the world.

"Now," said Lord Alaric. "My steward's page tells me that after the bell you asked him where my son goes at noon."

"Yes, my lord."

"And did you find him?"

"In the smithy yard, my lord."

"Doing what?"

"Pitching horseshoes."

"And you?"

"I pitched too, my lord."

The foot under the table had stopped. Verrall did not turn another page. "Who won?" said Lord Alaric.

"Master Matthos, my lord."

"By how much?"

"A ring."

The plank in the dirt moved under a toe, a hand's breadth, and Orryn kept it behind his teeth. It was not his to tell. He had not been asked about the plank, and a boy who told it unasked would be the sort of boy who told everything, which was a worse thing to be known for than almost anything he could think of.

"A ring." Lord Alaric leaned back in his chair. It creaked. He looked at Orryn for a long time, and Orryn kept his eyes on the third button of his lord's plain doublet, as he had been taught, and felt the water run down the small of his back. It was a very quiet room. Somewhere below them a door banged and was still.

"I know the kind of boy who crosses a yard at noon," said Lord Alaric, "having been struck at dawn, to a game he will not win, against the boy who struck him." He said it flatly, as he might have said that the granary leaked. "There are two kinds. I should like to know which I have bound to me. It is a plain question and you may answer it plainly."

His eyes did not move.

"Did you lose to my son, Orryn, or were you beaten?"

What does Orryn do?

1. Tell him the truth, whole: that he threw the last shoe wide on purpose, and why.

2. Say "I was beaten, my lord," which is true of the score, and leave his own last throw unsaid.

3. Decline to answer: say he would rather keep it between himself and Master Matthos, and take whatever follows from refusing.

4. Something else of your own.
