"You're wet," said Baldric Haye.

"I sat in a pond." Orryn gave the grey to a groom and found that his knees had gone soft under him. "Lord Alaric kicked the lock off. Or the peg. It was one of them."

"That's what the whole yard is saying. By morning he'll have kicked in the dam." Baldric did not smile; he never quite did. He held the folded paper in front of him in both hands as if it might be taken. "It's the lease. The Alder Mill, that Master Fallow mended for my father. Four sheets, his clerk wrote them out, and the middle of it will not come out the same way twice for me. You said you might look."

"I did." He had, on the ring-field in the dust, and had said he would ask Lord Alaric first, and had not yet found the moment. He looked at the four sheets, and at Baldric's long grave face with the sun-peel across the nose, and at his own hands, which were green to the wrists and shaking a little with the day. "Baldric, I can't. Not tonight. I'd read the same line eight times. Give me till after the dawn drill."

"It'll keep," said Baldric.

He did not say it as a man says it of a thing that will keep. But he folded the paper smaller, and put it inside his doublet, and said, "After the drill, then," and went away across the yard with his arms stiff at his sides, and Orryn watched him go with a small sour weight in his belly that did not feel like a fair price for a bath.

*

They ate in the squires' hall, cold mutton and bread and a bowl of last year's apples gone soft, and nobody talked. The heat had got into the walls and lay there. Perkin, who had been chased twice round the stables by Ser Tobin and had not yet been forgiven, ate with his head down. Piers had a wet cloth over the back of his neck. Osric's place was empty. Orryn was wiping the grease from his fingers on the bread when Alyn Merryweather appeared at his shoulder, smelling of pond, and said in his ear, "Come."

"Where?"

"The bend. Below the postern. Hob lifted his plank at dusk, I watched the boy go by on the road. It has a mile and a half of dry bed to drink before it gets to us, so it won't be here till moonrise, but it'll be here. Don't you want to see it come?" He grinned at Orryn's face. "Don't tell anyone. Look at him, he's thinking about the rules."

There was a rule against it, of the kind that everyone knew and Lord Alaric did not much trouble to enforce. He went.

The postern gave on a path through the old orchard, and below the orchard the ground fell to the brook. It was a clear night with no wind, and the air above the ground was still warm as breath. The heat came up through the soles of Orryn's boots. At the bend, where an old willow leaned over the bed with its dead leaves hanging like straw, there was a hollow in the stones where the water had stood deepest in spring, and they sat on its lip with their boots off and their feet in the dry, and the cracks in the mud were still warm.

"Longtable has a moat," said Alyn, to the stars. "It isn't much of a moat. We swam in it, my brother and I, in the summers. Our lady mother had a fit about it, because it had eels in it and because the steward kept the geese there. My brother used to say you can't drown in a place you can see the bottom of." He picked a stalk of straw out of the mud and put it between his teeth. "We found out the winter after that you could. Not us. A goose."

Orryn laughed, and then wasn't sure he should. "What did she do?"

"Had the moat drained, and the steward whipped, and the goose served at supper, since it was dead." He rolled the straw to the other side of his mouth. "You've seen her lists. She knows who ought to be at the table at every house between here and Oldtown, and who sits above whom, and who has been slighted in what year. I'd sooner be the man who holds the horses and eats at the long table and has nothing to ask of anybody."

"Lord Alaric might knight you."

"He might. Then it's a keep, and a wife, and a steward, and my sister's Ashfords at my elbow at every feast." He said it lightly. "What do you want, Orryn? Out of all this? Not the answer you'd give Ser Everard."

Orryn had a dozen ready. A seat at a lord's table. His father's name said without a pause after it. To be sent for. He turned them over in the dark and found them thin. "I don't know," he said, which was nearly true. "I'd like to be good at something. I'm not, yet."

"You were good with the water."

"I was lucky with the water."

"That's what lords call it, when it works." Alyn lay back on the stones with his hands behind his head. For a time there was only the sound of Alyn breathing, and the click of some small thing in the willow, and from the keep behind them a late dog. Orryn watched the stars come through the dead leaves. He had a feeling in his chest like a held breath that would not finish, and he knew very well what it was, and he put his eyes on the willow and kept them there.

It was not a new feeling. It had come before, in other seasons, for a stable-boy's laugh once, for a visiting knight's squire another summer; but it had never come for someone he would see at supper tomorrow, and the next day, and every day until spring. He knew what the septon said, and what the men in the yard said, and that it was a thing a boy folded small and kept inside his shirt, as Baldric folded his lease, until he was old enough to be rid of it or to learn how to carry it. Alyn, with a straw in his teeth, gave no sign of thinking of anything at all. That was the worst of it, and also the best. It meant he need not be careful. It meant he could be, for as long as the dark lasted, a boy on a stone beside his friend.

"Listen," said Alyn.

It was very faint, like a hand dragged lightly across a sheet. Then it was a whisper, then a mutter, the sound of water going over stones. Alyn sat up so fast that the straw fell out of his mouth. The first of it came round the bend in the dark, only a gleam at first, a long wet tongue feeling its way along the bed, nosing into the cracks, going gold where the moon had come up behind the willow, and it began to fill the hollow. It rose over their toes. It was warm, and then, at the bottom, surprisingly cold.

"Seven hells," said Alyn, almost reverently. They watched it for a long time, the hollow filling by inches while the frogs started up along the bank, and when it was knee-deep at the lip he began pulling off his shirt.

Orryn undressed to his breeches with his back half turned and his eyes on the bank, and found that his fingers would not work the laces. When he looked up, Alyn was already gone, in with a long clean noise and a spray that came up silver, and the pool was thigh-deep at the lip and over a man's head in the middle, black as ink. Orryn went down the stones after him to the knee. It took the heat out of his bones like a hand lifting a cloth.

"Come on, Grandview. You said you could swim."

"I can swim." He could, as a dog swims, with a lot of noise. He had learned in the shallow stream at home at nine, and had never been fast. He kicked out. The water slid over his shoulders and took the sting of the sun from his neck and the whole green stench of the afternoon off his skin, and he rolled over onto his back and floated with his ears under and heard his own heart, loud and slow.

Something took him by the ankle. He went under with a yell and came up spitting with Alyn's arm across his shoulders, Alyn shaking with laughter, both of them going down again in a tangle of limbs and wet hair and kicks, and Orryn put an elbow in someone's ribs and was dunked for it. For a moment, with his head under and someone's arm across his chest, he could think of nothing at all. It was only afterwards, surfacing, gasping, with the moon in his eyes, that he understood he was still holding very still, as a hare does, and that Alyn had already let go and was shouting something about the eels.

He swam to the bank and hung there with his arms on the warm stones, grateful for the dark. Alyn floated on his back with his arms spread, a pale shape in the black water, humming something tuneless that Orryn recognised, after a while, as the one Lord Alaric had hummed that afternoon.

"I am to go in the spring," Alyn said, to the sky. "Did I tell you? I asked Lord Alaric a week gone. He didn't say no. He said he would write to somebody."

"You said. At the ring-field."

"Did I?" He turned his head. "I thought I'd said it to Baldric." He floated for a while longer. "I'll miss this pool, anyway. That's a thing I didn't know until tonight."

It was not what Orryn wanted him to say, and it was not as bad as it might have been. He said, "It's mostly mud," and Alyn laughed, and the water ran on past the two of them, and the willow dripped, and for a long time neither said anything else.

*

They walked back through the orchard with their shirts in their hands and their hair dripping down their backs. The air smelled of green water and hot dust. At the postern Alyn clapped him on the shoulder, quite carelessly, and went off whistling toward the stair, and Orryn stood a moment with his own shoulder warm where the hand had been, and told himself that was all it was.

The squires' hall was dark but for one candle-end at the far bed-place. Baldric lay turned to the wall with his blanket to his ear, quite still, in the manner of a man who is pretending to be asleep. The four sheets lay on the floor beside him where he had let them drop, with the candle burning a hand's breadth from the corner of the top one.

What does Orryn do?

1. Pinch out the candle, take the lease, and read it by the light of the window until his eyes close, for the debt he owes Baldric.

2. Wake Baldric, tell him he is sorry, and read it with him now.

3. Leave it. Sleep. After the dawn drill is what he promised.

4. Something else of your own.
