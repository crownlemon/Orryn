Orryn had never used another man's name before.

He had used his own, often enough, and his father's, which opened some doors and shut others; and once, at nine, he had used his uncle's, to a miller's wife, to get a sack of bruised pears off her, which Alester had afterward found funny and Steffon had not. But another man's name, a name that belonged to a man he had met once, was something to be handled as one handles a borrowed knife: by the hilt, and carefully, and with a feeling that the owner might at any moment ask for it back.

"Ser Osmund," he said. "I am to be squire to Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield. These are his men, and this is his cart, and I am travelling on his errand and his word. He did not send me with leave to pay a toll. If the ford is owed anything, then it is owed by him, and he will be told how it was asked, and by whom."

He had meant it to sound like his father. It sounded, to his own ears, like a boy reciting a lesson in a voice a little too high. He kept his eyes on the knight's face and did not look at Wat.

Ser Osmund Sedgley considered him over the rim of the cup. The thin clerk, Harl, had lifted his pen. At the back of the shade the man with the spear had been joined by another, and the plum-eater, who had not yet stood, folded his arms.

"Alaric Meadows," said Ser Osmund, slowly, as if tasting the name for faults. "Lord of Grassfield. A very careful man, so they say. Writes letters." He turned the cup in his thick fingers. "I've never met him. I've never had cause. He sends his grain to Highgarden by the river road, and this isn't the river road, and so we've never had the pleasure." He smiled. "You tell me his name as if it were a key, lad. And I'm obliged. But look here. I can see the green on your cart. I saw it a quarter-mile off. A Meadows name's the very reason the rope's there. If Lord Alaric wishes to send to me, he knows where Sedgeford is. The toll is paid at the rope. It isn't paid by letter."

"Grassfield is two days from here," said Orryn.

"Then it's a long wait," said Ser Osmund, pleasantly, "for his answer. Or for your supper."

The men laughed again, not much. Orryn felt it as one feels a draught: not an insult, only a fact about the room. He had said the name, and it had been weighed, and it had come out light. Not worthless. Ser Osmund had taken it seriously enough to be amused. But it had not moved a rope. Perhaps, Orryn thought, Alaric Meadows was a great man in the Reach and a small one at a Stormlands ford; perhaps he was a small man everywhere, and had only the habit of exactness to make him look larger. He could not tell which. He thought, with a small cold lurch, that Alaric would want to know, and that he would not be the one to tell him.

He turned on the bench.

"Wat," he said. "Will you pay it?"

The elder groom did not look at him. He looked at the horse's ears. He sat so long without answering that Orryn began to wonder whether he had spoken aloud, and then Wat shifted the reins into his left hand, and with his right drew out from inside his shirt a small worn leather purse on a thong, the sort a carter keeps next to the skin. It had gone dark with sweat. He weighed it in his palm for a moment. Then he undid the knot.

"His lordship's purse is for the road," he said, to nobody. "You say this is the road, Master Orryn?"

It was not quite a question. It was the sort of thing a man says so that he can say later that it was said. Orryn understood that very well. It was the same thing Tam had asked of him over a barn full of hounds: I'll want it said I told you. He found that it made him angrier than the toll did, and also that he could not blame him.

"It is the road to Grassfield," he said. "And it's closed until the ford is paid. I'm asking you to pay it. If Lord Alaric wishes to know why, you may tell him that I asked."

"Aye," said Wat. "I'll tell him that."

He said it without any expression whatever, and that was the part Orryn kept turning over: that the man had agreed to carry his words to Lord Alaric, quite simply, as a fact, and had not said what he thought they were worth.

Wat counted the coin out onto the board between them. Two silver stags, thin and bright, with the old king's head worn smooth on them. Three copper pennies, dull as dock leaves. He did it slowly, one piece at a time, with a forefinger that had been broken once and set badly. Pate watched the coins go down with an odd expression, half pain and half interest, the look of a boy watching someone else's dog get put down. The sun had moved. Across the gravel the shadow of the toll-house eave had crept up to the rope, and now touched it, and the hemp lay half in light and half in shade.

"Harl," said Ser Osmund. "The book."

The clerk wrote. "Cart, one," he said, as if reading aloud from the page. "Horse, one. Persons, three. Two stags, three pence. Paid in coin at Sedgeford, in the afternoon, by order of the boy." The pen scratched. "His name?"

"Orryn Grandison," said Orryn, "of Grandview."

"Grandison," Harl repeated, and spelled it with a care that made it look like a word in a foreign tongue, and turned the book round on his arm so that it faced the cart. Orryn leaned across Pate to look. The hand was small and upright and quite clear, and the entry sat under a column of others, going back down the page: Reach carts. Reach pedlar, mule. Two Tyrell riders, no toll (by courtesy). A drover. There were, he saw, not many. A ford that had guarded its rope two hundred years had caught perhaps six travellers in a week. He found this a small, sour comfort. Ser Osmund was not growing rich. He was only growing stubborn.

"Well done, lad," said Ser Osmund, who had been watching him read. "You'd make a steward." It was meant as praise, and was in its way a friendly thing to say, and Orryn disliked it more than anything else that had been said. "You'll find the Reach doesn't think much of ours, I'm afraid. It's all the same river. But I've no quarrel with you. Tell Alaric Meadows that Osmund Sedgley sends his regards, and that the toll is two stags." He lifted two fingers to his temple in something that was not quite a salute. "Harl. Lower the rope."

Two of the soldiers went to the posts. The great hemp cable came off its hook and fell slack across the gravel, and lay there like a thing that had never meant any harm.

Wat gathered the reins. The grey horse put its head up and took a step, and the wheels began to turn.

Orryn sat back against the canvas, with his mother's bundle still on his knees. The sum was two stags and three pence, and he had been right about that. He had been right about the rest, too, he supposed: that the name had not carried, that the purse would have to, that it was no good trying to talk a man out of what he meant to have. It had gone as a cart goes over a rut. Nothing was broken. He told himself that, and found it did not help in the least, because the thing he had spent was not in the purse. He had put a boy's word into a groom's mouth, to be carried to a lord he had not met. It would be there when he arrived, waiting for him. Alaric would turn it over and look at it from every side.

Pate was looking at him again. It was the fourth time. He did not say anything, but he moved his right hand, the one with the scab, a little on his knee. It might have been to settle his whip. It might have meant, well, that's done. It might have been nothing at all.

They were halfway across, with the water slapping the felloes and a heron, a real one, grey, rising off the shallows with a creak of wings, when Pate turned his head.

"Rider," he said.

It was the first word he had spoken since Grandview. His voice was surprisingly deep.

Orryn twisted on the seat to look back. Behind them, on the road they had come down, a plume of dust stood against the afternoon, and under it, coming fast, two horses: a big chestnut courser flecked with foam, and a smaller bay behind. The man on the chestnut rode as if the road had been laid for him, and he wore a coat of crimson silk with two small crossed quills at the breast, and as he came down the slope toward the toll-house he put up one hand in a cheerful wave, a long way off, at nobody in particular.

On the bank, with the cable still in his hands, Ser Osmund Sedgley said, "Well. There's a thing," and began, slowly, to smile.
