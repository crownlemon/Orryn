Goldengrove, the Reach, 208 AC

Lord Rowan heard his people in the mornings, and he heard them standing.

He did it in the great hall under the golden tree of his house, with the trestles stacked against the walls, his steward at his elbow and a dozen of his knights along the walls. Ser Lucan Marwick, the Grassfield household knight who had ridden with them, said that the old lords of the Reach had judged that way before there were so many chairs, and that a lord who sat to hear a man’s trouble was halfway to falling asleep over it. He had told Orryn so on the stair.

Orryn was fourteen, a Grandison of Grandview in the Stormlands, and for most of a year he had been squire to Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep, in Grassy Vale, three days’ ride to the south. They had come to Goldengrove because Lady Arlanna, the wife of Lord Leo Tyrell, who ruled the Reach from Highgarden, had offered a boy in the keeping of Lord Rycherd Rowan of Goldengrove as a husband for Lord Alaric’s daughter Elyce, and had asked neither Lord Rowan nor Lady Jeyne first. Lord Rowan had sent word that if a lord of Grassfield wished to ask him anything, he might come and ask it as a lord asks a lord. Lord Alaric had come, and asked, and been answered in the night. This was the morning after.

The knights along the wall were talking about the south. Everyone talked about the south that year, since riders had come down out of the Dornish hills and burned a steading below Horn Hill, Lord Tarly’s seat on the border, and half the Reach had been riding south or arguing about it ever since.

“Found a burned camp in the hills,” said a grey knight with a scald on his neck. “Ashes still warm, and Dornish leather in them. A wine man out of Horn Hill told the cook.”

“Found a goatherd’s fire and a dead goat, more like, and called it a camp.” The knight beside him was younger and had a lisp he did not seem to mind. “My cousin rode with Tarly’s men a year back. He says every cookfire looks like a war camp to a knight who wants a war.”

“And what does your cousin want?”

“His supper. Same as me.”

The steward struck the floor once with his staff, and they were quiet.

Ser Tom Costen’s wife came first. Orryn knew whose wife she was before her name was cried, because the boy beside her had Ser Tom’s face: the same red, sun-cooked cheeks Orryn had seen in Lord Osmund Leygood’s hall at Highgarden, though none of the weight yet. The boy was thirteen or so, and stared at Orryn as though a squire his own age were the strangest thing in the room. The woman was broad, in a brown gown let out at the seams. Behind them came the reeve of Cress Hill, an old man with a voice like a gate that wanted grease.

“They’ve kept him, my lord,” Ser Tom’s wife said. She did not waste anything on courtesies. “They filled our trench and gave the spring to the widow, and fined him, and then they kept him for the knife, and they keep him still. Two hundred people on that hill, and the water going to Lady Ormesby’s cows, and we carry it up from the brook in buckets, and the brook is half mud.”

“We’ll be carrying it in our mouths next,” said the reeve.

Lord Rowan heard them out. He asked how deep the old well was, and the reeve said it was dry at forty feet, and Lord Rowan said, “Then dig at sixty,” as if that settled it, and turned to his steward. Two men who had dug the new well at the home farm were to go to Cress Hill with a windlass and rope and whatever else they asked for, and stay until they struck water or rock.

Then he looked at the woman.

“Your husband drew steel in another man’s hall,” he said. “That was a fool’s work, and he’ll answer for it. But he’ll answer for it here, to me, in front of the men whose quarrel put the knife in his hand. Not at Highgarden, to a man who has never seen Cress Hill and would not know which way the water runs on it. I’ll bring him home.”

The woman nodded, once, as though she had been promised a sack of flour and meant to count it.

When they had gone, Lord Rowan turned his heavy head. “You were in Leygood’s hall, Meadows. You saw it.”

“I saw it,” said Lord Alaric.

“Then you saw a Rowan knight judged without his lord, by a man of Ser Garth’s.”

“I saw a knight draw a knife on a bailiff in a court of law.”

“That too.” Lord Rowan did not seem troubled. “Say both, when the time comes.”

Orryn remembered it better than he wanted to: dust standing in the slanted light, the bailiff’s arm laid open, Septon Emmon kneeling to bind it with his sleeves pushed up. Ser Tom had shouted Lord Rowan’s name with a guard’s knee in his back, and the bailiff had shouted Ser Moryn’s, and afterward Lord Alaric had said quietly, And not Lord Leo. Not once.

*

Lord Rowan rode out after that to look at his river, and he took Lord Alaric, and Ser Triston, his heir, and two of his knights, and Ser Lucan, who was not asked and came anyway. He took the Lord of Old Oak as well. Lord Harys was not asked either. He was told.

“The boy rides with us,” Lord Rowan said in the yard. “Let him be seen.”

Lord Harys Oakheart, thirteen and as thin as a lath, sat his long-legged grey a length ahead of the rest of them with his back as straight as a spear, and did not look round once. Orryn, riding behind his lord on Rook, watched that back all the way down the hill and could not tell whether the boy was angry or only being a lord.

The Mander was low, with bars of pale sand standing up out of it like the backs of beasts and the reeds dead and white along the banks. Still, it ran. Carts went up from the ferry stair with casks lashed in them, and men with yokes and buckets went from cask to tree in the young orchards, a bucket to each tree, as if they were feeding horses. The air smelled of dust and windfalls.

Lord Rowan rode with his reins in one fist and talked as he rode, as plainly as if he were telling his steward where to put the casks.

“You want to know what I’ll say to Leo,” he said. “I’ll tell you, and you can tell your lady wife, since you’ll tell her anyway. A lord judges his own people. Not Leygood, not any of Garth’s men with a chain round his neck. Mine, in my hall, where they can see me do it. That’s the first thing. The second: no clerk walks into a lord’s barns and counts his sacks without the lord’s leave. Garth wants a tenth of every granary in the Reach in his depots. As a loan to the Reach, he says, because forty riders burned a farm at the far end of it.” He made a noise in his nose. “The Reach has never paid anything back to anyone. The third: a grant a man’s grandfather was given stands until the man has been heard. It isn’t read away in the night by a Redwyne with a candle. And the fourth you know. A guardian is asked.”

“That last is mine,” said Lord Alaric.

“It’s all yours, if you have a court and a barn and a grant in your chest. Armond tells me you told Leygood to his face that you’d give grain in a dry year and not give up your own court.”

Lord Alaric did not deny it.

“Who says it with you?” he asked.

“Armond gave me his hand at your girl’s wedding. You were there.” Lord Rowan counted on the fingers of his rein hand. “Ashford let none of Garth’s clerks past his gate. You’ll have had that from your Ser Androw. Gormon Peake lost two castles after the rebellion on the strength of what somebody wrote down about him; he’ll stand with any man who hates a clerk. And Moryn will speak for us at his father’s table.”

“Moryn spent two nights in your closet.”

“Telling me to be patient.” The heavy face did not change. “Moryn wants it asked for. At supper, quietly, with the wine going round and his lady mother smiling at her frame. I want it said. In the hall, in front of every lord who’ll come, where Leo can’t pretend afterward that he never heard it.”

Ser Garth Tyrell was Lord Leo’s heir and did most of Highgarden’s governing, with his wife’s Redwyne kin at his elbow; Ser Moryn was the second son, and the lords who disliked Ser Garth’s clerks took their troubles to him. Orryn had seen Ser Moryn at Highgarden, warm and easy, remembering everyone’s mother. He could very well imagine him telling Lord Rowan to be patient.

“And Garth?” said Lord Alaric.

“Garth reads accounts, and Redwyne pays for the candles.” Lord Rowan shrugged. “I don’t lie awake over Garth. I lie awake over what he’ll have written down before he’s lord. A thing done twice in the Reach is called custom, and custom is called law.”

“And there’s this,” Lord Rowan said. “I’ll not wait for the year to turn. When the rain comes, the fright goes. Every lord in the Reach will be out looking at his wet fields and forgetting he was ever afraid of Dornishmen, and Garth’s tenth will go with the fright, unless his clerks have it written into the rolls first. So I want my names before it rains.”

“And Olyvar?” said Lord Alaric.

“Olyvar went south to win himself a name, and he took my brother with him. Now the Hand has taken the whole business into his own hands, and Olyvar is sitting at Horn Hill with nobody to hit.” Lord Rowan looked at the river. “Tarly’s feeding them, I hear. Tarly will not thank him for it.”

“Uncle Nyles will come home with a Dornish horse,” said Ser Triston, “and not a Dornishman in sight.”

“If he comes home on the horse he went on, I’ll be content.”

Ser Lucan had ridden all this way without a word. Now he said, to no one, “The law is plain enough. No lord may take his levies over the mountains without the king’s leave. A knight who rides without it rides at his own risk, and his lord’s.”

“Then my brother’s risk is mine,” said Lord Rowan. “I let him go.”

They had come out on the green ground by the water where Orryn had walked Rook at dawn. Lord Rowan reined in. For a while he watched the men with the buckets.

“This is what I ask,” he said. “Stand beside me in Leo’s hall. When they come to Cress Hill, say what you saw. When they come to the boy, say you’re a father who wasn’t asked, beside a guardian who wasn’t asked.”

“And if I don’t?”

Lord Rowan did not lower his voice. Lord Harys was a length ahead, no more.

“The boy is mine until I knight him,” he said. “A knight’s a knight when I make him one. Sixteen, for a friend’s girl. Nineteen, for a man who stood with Highgarden.”

Ahead of them the grey jinked sideways as if somebody had touched it with a spur. Lord Harys gathered it again. His back did not move at all.

Orryn found that he had done the sum before he could stop himself. Elyce was twelve. Sixteen, and she would be fifteen. Nineteen, and she would be eighteen, and three years of her life would have gone into a quarrel between two lords about who was asked.

“You’d keep a boy three years longer to punish me,” said Lord Alaric.

“I’d keep him until he’s fit. I’m the one who says when.”

“That’s a price.”

“Everything is a price. You came to ask mine.” Lord Rowan turned his horse. “Think on it. You have until we eat.”

He rode on ahead to speak to the boy, or perhaps only to ride beside him. Ser Triston did not follow his father. He let his horse drift back until it walked beside Lord Alaric’s, and for a while said nothing at all, which Orryn was beginning to understand was how the Rowans began things.

“My father is Ser Moryn’s friend,” Ser Triston said at last. “Ser Garth will be Lord of Highgarden. I’ll be Lord of Goldengrove a long while after this, gods willing, and I’d sooner not spend it on the wrong side of his hall.”

“Then why tell me?”

“Because you have a son at Highgarden. If you stand in that hall, they’ll remember your face as well as my father’s.” He said it pleasantly, as he had asked after the wells. “They’ll remember mine anyway.”

At the ferry stair an old groom was holding the horses of the men who had gone down to look at the casks. He lifted his face as they passed and sniffed, the way a hound sniffs.

“Rain before morning, my lord,” he said. “My knee says.”

“Your knee has said that every day since the dry began,” said Lord Rowan.

“It means it today.”

On the way back up the hill Ser Lucan put his horse beside Lord Alaric’s and spoke low, but not so low that Orryn, a length behind, could not hear him.

“My lord. Stand at a lord’s shoulder once, and every hall in the Reach will call you his man.”

“I know what they call me, ser,” said Lord Alaric.

Lady Clarice sent for him at midday.

Her maid found him in the passage with Lord Alaric’s riding boots. Her lady’s rider would leave for Parchments at first light, she said, and if Master Grandison had a letter for Lady Alysanne it might go too, if it was written. It was not written. The maid seemed to have expected that. She took him up to a bright room over the orchard, where Lady Clarice, Lord Rowan’s wife, sat at a table with a letter of her own half finished in front of her.

“Sit,” she said, and moved her inkpot across the table to him. “Write it here, where I can tell my sister that it was written.”

He wrote to Lady Alysanne first, because it was hardest. Lady Alysanne Penrose was Lady Clarice’s younger sister and kept Parchments, the Penrose seat in the Stormlands, while her goodbrother Lord Ronnel sat on the king’s council. Her son Ser Addam had married Orryn’s cousin Elenei a year ago, and at that wedding she had not seemed sure that a Grandison boy was worth a second look. He wrote that he hoped she was well, and that the dun Rook, whom Ser Jon had lent him, was fed and sound. He wrote that his duties kept him from writing as often as he ought, which was not quite a lie, and that he was her ladyship’s servant. He thought it was the dullest thing he had ever made, and that this was probably correct.

Elenei was easier. Elenei read her letters aloud at table to anyone who would sit still for it, and left them in window seats for servants to find; anything he put in a letter to Elenei he might as well cry from the gatehouse. So he wrote her about the apples, and the salt dish with the lid made like an apple, and Lady Joy’s hair, which was nearly silver. He did not write about anything else.

The room talked over his head while he wrote. He was furniture with a quill.

Lady Joy Penrose had come in with Myranda Webber, Ser Wendell’s daughter, close behind her, and Lady Roslin, Lord Rowan’s daughter, was already in the window with an embroidery frame she was not embroidering. Lady Joy was to marry Ser Triston, and was a princess’s daughter, and wanted the wedding at court.

“Mother says the Great Sept,” she said, “with the king’s leave, and the High Septon to say the words.”

“Goldengrove has a sept,” said Lady Clarice, without looking up.

“A small one.”

“I was a princess’s daughter,” said Lady Clarice, “and I was married in it, by our own septon, and I do not recall that anyone noticed the size of the sept.”

Lady Joy decided not to have heard that. She sat down in the window by Lady Roslin, whose needle stopped.

“At court they say the Dornish eat out of one dish with their fingers,” Lady Joy said, “and their princesses keep their lovers at table where their husbands can see them.”

“Then they are more honest than we are,” said Lady Roslin.

“Princess Jena says we are all one realm now.” Lady Joy laughed, a pretty laugh, as though it were a thing she had heard laughed at and was laughing at in its turn. “Everyone says it after she’s gone out. One realm. Ser Manfred Dondarrion says it at dice.”

“Uncle Nyles is in the one realm now,” said Lady Roslin, “sitting at Horn Hill with no one to fight. I hope he’s enjoying it.” She said it sharply, and her needle went into the cloth and stayed there, and Orryn, who had a sister, thought she was more frightened for her uncle than she wanted anyone to see.

Myranda Webber, who had been examining Lady Joy’s sleeve as though it were a holy relic, looked up from it.

“Ser Gwayne Oakheart only rode south to win himself a name before Harys is old enough to take Old Oak off him,” she said. “Everyone knows it. He fights Lady Ceryse for every sack of barley. If a Dornishman kills him, Harys should send the Dornishman a horse.”

“Myranda,” said Lady Clarice.

“It’s what my father says.”

“Your father says a great many things.” Lady Clarice laid down her quill. “Lord Jon Hightower has sent grain to Horn Hill, under the white tower, with a Mullendore knight to see it arrive. That was well done. It was not meant for the hungry.”

“Who was it meant for?” said Lady Joy.

“Everyone who saw the carts.”

Lady Clarice turned to him as he sanded the second letter. “When is Lady Jeyne to be brought to bed?”

“Before the year turns, my lady.”

“And Matthos. Is he content with Ser Moryn?”

“I haven’t seen him since Highgarden, my lady.”

“He was here with Ser Moryn. He split his lip on one of our pages, and asked no one to pity him for it, and I liked him.” She folded her own letter. “And Grassfield. Did Lord Alaric let Ser Garth’s men count his oats?”

Orryn hesitated, and then thought that it was hardly a secret, since half of Grassy Vale had stood in the yard to watch. “He let them count, my lady, in front of witnesses. He sent nothing.”

“He let them count.” Lady Clarice said it over to herself, the way Maester Perrin repeated a remedy he did not believe in. She held out her hand for his letters and put them with hers. “I shall tell my sister you are well, Master Grandison, and that you have written. She will not believe the second part.”

Behind her, Lady Joy had finished admiring her own sleeve.

“You shall come to court with me when I’m married,” she told Myranda, “and see real sleeves.” Myranda Webber bent her heavy-lidded eyes to the silk and said she would like that above anything, in a voice so flat that only someone who had heard her at supper would have known it was the first true thing she had said all day.

Lady Roslin caught him in the passage outside.

“You were at Highgarden,” she said. “You saw Ser Harlen Tyrell.”

Ser Harlen was Ser Garth’s son, and heir to Highgarden after him. Orryn had heard at Highgarden that Lord Rowan’s daughter was being weighed for him against another girl, and he thought now, too late, that Lady Roslin had heard it too.

“Yes, my lady. In the yard.”

“Is he tall?”

“Tall enough, my lady.”

“That is a squire’s answer.” She did not smile. “What did he do in the yard?”

He thought about it. Ser Harlen at the rail with his arms folded, and the face of a man who had carried a message and been struck for it. “He asked the master-at-arms to let the squires sleep, my lady, because they’d been drinking. Ser Luthor said he might apply to his lord grandfather in writing.”

“So he’s kind, and nobody does what he says.” She looked down the passage, toward the window where her mother sat. “They’re weighing me against a Blackbar girl at Highgarden, like two mares at a fair. I should like to know what the buyer looks like.”

“He pays his friends’ debts, my lady, and gives wine to strangers. Lord Alaric said so.”

“Then he’ll be poor.” Lady Roslin looked at him again, and whatever she had been about to say she did not. “You’re no use at all, Grandison. Go and pour something.”

The stables at noon were the only place in Goldengrove where it was cool.

Lord Rowan’s squires had made a table of an upturned barrel in the shade of the long stable, and four of them sat around it at tiles with their shirts open. Bryce Webber, Ser Wendell’s second son, whom Ser Alyn knocked off his horse every morning, stood by with his arm in a sling he had not had at dawn and watched as though he might be allowed to play. The eldest Webber boy, older and quieter, sat on a saddle with his back to the wall and played with his purse. Robin Penrose owed him nine silver stags, and everyone in the castle knew it.

They were talking about Dornishmen.

“A hundred of them,” said a red-haired squire.

“Forty. Tarly’s sergeant counted.”

“Forty, then, and every one with a spear like a ship’s mast.”

“They say the Dornish girls go about in silk you can see clear through.”

“Webber’s seen one.”

“Webber’s never seen a girl he wasn’t kin to.”

The table howled. Bryce Webber went red from his collar up. “I’d have gone south if my father had let me.”

“Your father would’ve sold your horse before you got to the gate.”

“They’d have hanged you for a horse thief,” said the red-haired one, “and Lady Mina would have said you were a knight of the realm.”

They howled at that too. Orryn, who had come to see that Rook had water, kept his face to the dun’s shoulder and laughed into the horse, and was ashamed of it, and laughed anyway.

Robin Penrose came in out of the sun with a cup in his hand and the flush of noon wine already on him. He was Lady Joy’s brother, twenty or so, heir to Parchments, and he played tiles the way he talked, loudly and badly. He sat down at the barrel without being asked, pushed one of the squires off his place with his hip, and lost three hands in a row to the eldest Webber boy, who did not seem to enjoy winning.

On the third hand he looked up and saw Rook.

“That’s one of ours,” he said. “That’s Cousin Jon’s dun. I’d know that ugly head anywhere.” He stood. “Saddle him. I’ll take him down to the river and see whether Jon has lost his eye for a horse.”

The groom looked at Orryn. Orryn looked at the groom.

“He’s lent to me, my lord,” he said. “Under Ser Jon’s hand.”

“Lent by a Penrose. I’m a Penrose.” Robin Penrose waved his cup at the groom. “Well? Saddle him.”

Orryn did not argue with him. He had seen men argue with Robin Penrose at supper, and it had only made him louder. He went out of the stable at a run and found Ser Triston in the armoury door with a squire buckling a greave on him, and said what he had to say in a breath.

Ser Triston came as he was, with one greave on.

He looked at the groom, who had the saddle half lifted, and at Rook, and at Robin Penrose.

“Robin,” he said. “The dun is lent under Ser Jon’s hand, and Lord Alaric holds the slip. Leave it.”

“It’s a Penrose horse.”

“Then ask Ser Jon for it, from Parchments.” Ser Triston turned to the eldest Webber boy. “How much does he owe you?”

“Nine.”

Ser Triston took a purse from his belt and counted nine silver stags into the Webber boy’s hand, one at a time, while the squires at the barrel watched and pretended not to. When he had done he looked at Robin.

“There,” he said. “Now you owe me.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No,” said Ser Triston. “That’s what makes it worth something.” He nodded to Orryn as though Orryn had fetched him a cup and went back to his greave.

Robin Penrose said something under his breath about Rowans and stewards, and went out the other way.

It was a little after that Lord Harys came.

He came to see to his grey, or that was what he said to the groom; but he did not look at the grey. The squires at the barrel saw him and grinned at one another.

“My lord of Old Oak,” said the red-haired one. “Your uncle Gwayne’s off killing Dornishmen. What have you killed?”

“A hare, once,” said another. “It died of fright.”

“Out,” said Lord Harys.

They went. They went slowly, gathering their tiles and their cups and taking a long time about it, and that told Orryn more about what a hostage lord was worth at Goldengrove than anything Ser Cleyton had ever said. The Webber boys went last. Bryce looked back from the door.

Lord Harys turned to Orryn. His bitten hands were shut.

“You poured for them last night,” he said. “In his closet.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“This morning I heard him say nineteen.” His voice was quite steady. “Is that your lord’s price? Me kept until I’m nineteen?”

Orryn thought of a dozen things to say, and that every one of them would be a lie, and that the boy would know.

“No, my lord. It’s Lord Rowan’s. Lord Alaric told Lord Armond there’d be no betrothal while you’re anyone’s hostage. And Lord Rowan says when you’re knighted. So it’s Lord Rowan who says when.”

Lord Harys considered that for a long moment, very still.

“So he’ll sell my spurs to your lord,” he said, “for a place beside him in Leo’s hall.”

Orryn said nothing. There did not seem to be anything to say that was not yes.

“Then I’ll make him knight me.” The boy said it the way another boy might have said he would make a horse jump. Then, without any change in his voice at all: “What is she like?”

He did not say who.

“She’s twelve,” said Orryn. “She has freckles. She cries when it’s useful, and when it isn’t she makes lists of who’s been unfair to her.” He thought about it. “And she keeps getting back on.”

“You said that before.”

“It’s still true, my lord.”

For a moment something moved at the corner of Lord Harys’s mouth. Then it was gone, and he went past Orryn to the grey, and Orryn heard him begin to talk to it, low and quick, before he was out of the door.

By evening the heat had thickened until the hall was like the inside of a loaf. The high windows stood open and let in nothing but moths. Over the hills to the west the sky had gone the colour of a bruise, and twice during the fish course Orryn saw the far clouds light up from inside without any sound at all.

The knights below the dais watched the windows and talked about the south.

“Raiders,” said the grey knight with the scald. “Dornish raiders, and the Hand writes to Sunspear to ask them very civilly whether they’ve burned anything.”

“Asking the fox about the hens,” said somebody.

“Tarly asked us for ten spears apiece,” said the knight with the lisp, “and Leo said the Reach was not at war, and Dondarrion and Caron rode south with a hundred horse between them and asked nobody’s leave at all, and nobody has hanged them for it. So which of them is the fool?”

“Tarly,” said three men at once, and laughed.

“My brother rode for the Reach,” said Lady Mina Webber from the dais, “which is more than some men did.” She was Lord Rowan’s sister, and Ser Nyles’s, and she had wanted Old Oak for her own daughter.

She did not look at Lord Alaric when she said it. She did not have to.

Ser Wendell, her husband, slapped the board. “If I’d been twenty years younger I’d have been at Olyvar’s stirrup. I’d have had the truth out of the first Dornishman I met.”

“You’d have sold him his own horse, Wendell,” called the grey knight, and the benches roared.

One of the younger Rowan knights, flushed with cider, leaned out from the end of his bench. “And what did Grassfield send south, my lord? Prayers?”

The hall went quieter than it had been. Orryn, with the flagon, felt his own face go hot, as if it had been said to him.

Lord Alaric did not raise his voice. “I kept six riders saddled nine days for Lord Tarly,” he said, “and stood them down when Lord Leo did not call. I would do the same again.”

Some men nodded at that, and some looked at their cups, and one or two looked at Lord Rowan to see what he thought of it. Lord Rowan went on eating.

Orryn thought of Sergeant Hollis and his hungry men a long way from home, and of a princess nobody listened to, and kept it behind his teeth. Nobody in that hall wanted to hear it from a Stormlander with a jug.

When the swans had been carried out, Lord Rowan stood.

He did not have to call for quiet. He held up his cup, and the benches stopped.

“To the lords of the Reach,” he said, in his slow grey voice, “every man of them lord in his own hall.”

The knights pounded the boards until the dishes jumped. Lord Rowan let them, and drank, and did not sit. He turned a little toward his right hand.

“To my friend of Grassfield.”

The hall waited. Orryn could feel it wait, the way a hall at Grandview waited when his uncle Alester had said something at supper that somebody would have to answer. Ser Lucan, below, had gone still as a post. Ser Triston was looking into his cup.

Lord Alaric stood and lifted his own.

“To every guardian,” he said, “who is asked.”

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Lord Rowan drank, and the benches drank after him, and someone laughed and someone pounded the board again, and the talk came back up like water after a stone. Orryn did not know what had happened. He knew that it had been watched. He saw the grey knight say something behind his hand to the knight with the lisp, and the knight with the lisp nod. He saw Ser Lucan’s face close like a shutter. He saw Lord Rowan sit down at last, looking, for the first time since Orryn had known him, almost pleased.

Lady Mina Webber stood up then too.

She did not go to her brother. She came down the high table, past Lady Joy and past Ser Triston, until she stood across the board from Lord Alaric, with her long heavy face very pale.

“My brother promised Old Oak to my girl,” she said, “before your wife’s stepmother had ever heard the boy’s name. Take your toast home, my lord, and let your daughter wait for her lord until he is nineteen.”

“Mina,” said Lord Rowan.

“I have said it.” She went out by the door behind the dais, and did not look back.

Ser Wendell looked after her with his mouth open. Then, because he was Ser Wendell, he leaned along the board toward Lord Alaric as though nothing had happened at all.

“Now, my lord,” he said. “My second boy. Bryce. He’d make you a fine squire, a very fine one, he has a seat on him—”

“When Ser Alyn stops knocking him off it,” said Lord Alaric, “ask me again.”

The end of the dais heard it and laughed, and Bryce Webber, down on the squires’ bench with his arm in its sling, heard the laugh if not the words, and looked at his trencher.

Orryn filled his lord’s cup. Down the hall Robin Penrose was telling a knight that at court they were laying wagers on whether the Prince of Dorne would answer the Hand within the moon or within the year. Over the hills the clouds lit up again. Myranda Webber’s place on the first bench had been empty since the fish, and somewhere between the swans and the toast Ser Alyn’s place among the knights had emptied too, and Orryn had not seen him go.

*

“The wind’s turned,” Lord Alaric said, when the hall was breaking up. He was standing at the open door with his face lifted, as if he could smell it as well as the old groom. “See the horses are in, and the stable shutters fast. If it comes, I don’t want Rook standing in it.”

The quickest way to the stables was along the river wall. Orryn took the stair two steps at a time, with the wind pushing at his back and the air gone strange and close, and heard the laugh before he reached the top.

It was a woman’s laugh, low, and he knew it, because he had heard it say You belong to my uncle over a dish of trout.

They were in the angle of the wall where it turned above the river, half in the shadow of the tower. Ser Alyn Merryweather, who had been senior squire at Grassfield until Lord Alaric knighted him and sent him away, sat on the parapet with a wineskin, and Myranda Webber stood between his knees, and her golden-brown hair, which had been pulled so tight all day that it seemed to hurt her, had come out of its pins and hung down her back. She did not spring away when she saw Orryn. She stepped back, taking her time, and put up her hands to her hair.

She passed him on the stair close enough that he could smell the wine on her.

“Tell your lord’s girl she’s welcome to the boy,” she said, and went down into the dark.

Orryn stood where he was. Something had happened in his chest that he did not have a name for. It was hot and stupid and entirely about her: her hair down her back, her low laugh, the slow way she had stepped back, as if nothing in the world could hurry her. He hated her for it. He had not known that you could hate somebody for standing in a place.

“Don’t look at me like a septa, Grandview.” Alyn held out the wineskin. “Drink, or go down.”

Orryn did not drink. He did not go down either. He came and stood at the parapet, a little way off, and looked at the river, which was black and low and smelled of mud.

“Nyles will come home with a tale,” said Alyn, after a while. “Some hill he rode up, some Dornish arrow he heard go by. He’ll tell it at every table in the Reach for twenty years. And I’ll have Webbers.” He drank. “Thirty-two tomorrow.”

“You could ask Lord Rowan to let you go south.”

“There’s no south left. Didn’t you hear them? The Hand has it in his hands.” Alyn laughed, without much in it. “Is she happy? Leonette.”

Orryn thought of the sept at Grassfield, and Leonette on her father’s arm, glancing once toward the back where the Rowan knights stood.

“I was holding the candles,” he said. “I didn’t see.”

Alyn looked at him for a moment as though he knew exactly what that was worth. Then he looked away.

“My mother wants to know why I left Grassfield,” he said. “If I write to her I’ll have to lie to her in ink, and she keeps letters. That’s why. Now you know, and you needn’t make that face at Androw.” He put the stopper in the wineskin. “Say nothing about tonight. About her.”

“I won’t.”

“Good lad.”

It was the second time, and it went into the same place as the first. Orryn heard himself say it before he could stop it, in the voice the squires had used at the barrel.

“Does she charge double too?”

Alyn went very still on the parapet.

“Mind your mouth, Grandview,” he said, quite quietly. “She’s a knight’s daughter, and Lord Rowan’s niece, and you’re a guest in his house.”

Orryn would have given anything he owned to have the words back. He did not own very much.

The first drop hit the back of his hand.

It was so big and so warm that he thought for a moment somebody had spat on him. Then another struck the stone by his foot, and left a dark mark the size of a coin, and another, and then the smell came up off the hot stones all at once, dust and iron and something green he had not smelled in so long that he had forgotten it was a smell at all.

Below them, in the yard, someone shouted.

By the time Orryn reached the bottom of the stair the yard was full. Cooks and grooms and knights and serving girls had come out of every door, and they stood in the rain with their faces turned up and their mouths open like fledglings. The rain came down harder, warm and heavy, rattling on the stable roofs and hissing on the hot flags, and somebody began to laugh and could not stop.

“That’s Olyvar’s war finished,” shouted the grey knight with the scald, with water running off his nose. “They’ll all be home for the ploughing!”

Lord Rowan stood in the middle of the yard without a cloak, his grey hair flattening to his skull. Lord Alaric was beside him, and Lord Alaric was laughing, his head back and his hands open, as Orryn had never once seen him laugh at Grassfield.

“By morning nobody will remember who rode,” Lord Rowan said, to Lord Alaric or to the rain. “Or why they were frightened. I’ll go to Highgarden sooner.”

Orryn looked for Rook, and remembered the shutters, and did not move.

Across the yard, on the wet flags by the sept door, Osric Dunmere was on his knees. He had his seven-pointed star in his fist and his lips were moving, thanking the Mother, or perhaps the Father, or whichever of the Seven had sent it. Osric was Ser Lucan’s squire and the most pious boy at Grassfield, and his aunt was Septa Lenora, who, the squires said, told Lady Jeyne everything she heard. He had stopped praying. He was looking up at the river wall, at the stair, at Orryn standing at the foot of it with the rain running into his collar, and at Ser Alyn Merryweather coming down behind him.
