It was the third week without rain, too hot to think, and Ser Everard Blythe had decided that it was therefore the right day to fight.

"A battle is fought in the weather the Seven send," he said, "and not in the weather you would like. You will thank me when you are old and alive." He stood in the middle of the yard at noon with his silver hair tied back and his doublet, worn and spotless as ever, buttoned to the throat. He was forty-nine, tall, once handsome and now running to fat, with a nose the wine had mapped in red, and it was clear to every squire that he had dined well. That was why the squires arranged their lessons for the morning. Nobody had asked them about this one.

The sun stood straight overhead. There was no shadow in the yard at all. The cobbles burned through boot-soles, and the padded jacks they had been told to wear stuck to Orryn's back like wet hides. Along the north wall, where the gallery threw its stripe of shade, the knights sat on a bench with a bucket of cider cooling in the well-trough and the air of men at a play.

"You will boil us, ser," said Alyn Merryweather.

"A boiled squire is a tender squire," said Ser Everard, and Ser Tobin Whitlow, who was thirty and lay full-length on the bench with a hat over his golden curls, laughed so hard that he had to take the hat off.

"Ser Tobin has a hat," said Perkin Fenwick, who served him, to nobody.

"Ser Tobin has a knighthood," said Piers Fallow, to Orryn, low. "Same as a hat. Better for sleeping in." He had been the colour of a boiled beet since the second bell.

"Even money Grandview is the last to finish and the first to complain," Piers added, louder, for the benefit of the yard. "Three to one on Perkin dropping first. Osric never drops. I'll lay a hundred to one on that, and I'll lose nothing."

"I've the chalk," said Baldric Haye, and made a mark on the wall by the stable door, as a man marks a tally of sheep. "It's only fair that someone keeps the book."

Ser Everard took no notice. He paired them like a man pairing hounds. Alyn with Osric, who would not be hurried. Baldric with Perkin. "And Fallow with Grandview. Two wet sacks of meal. I want to see which one splits first."

Orryn did not mean to be a sack of meal, but he had a practice sword that weighed as much as a small child and a helm that had been left in the sun, and the first time he lifted it his eyes watered. Piers's face, behind the bars, was shining and furious. They circled once. They struck, slowly, as men beat a rug under water. Orryn landed a clumsy cut on Piers's ribs that meant nothing, and Piers hit his shoulder, and Orryn did not feel it until the next breath. Then neither of them could lift his blade, and they stood in the sun with their hands on their knees, drawing in air like bellows with holes in them.

"Oh, that is shameful," said Ser Everard, with great affection.

"Draw," said Ser Tobin, from the bench, to roars from everyone. "Both to the pump. Bring them wine."

Alyn, who had finished with Osric in a series of quick, bored blows and then stood pouring water over his own head, came across the yard, and without warning tipped the whole of his bucket over Orryn's. It was like being struck by a wall. Orryn gasped and spat and heard himself laugh, a high, startled, breathless noise that he did not recognise. "Grandview," said Alyn, grinning, "you were disgusting. You were nearly as bad as Fallow." And he went off to Ser Tobin's bench, where the gentleman lay asleep again with the hat over his face and his beautiful boots set side by side, and Orryn, who was wiping his eyes, did not see what he did next until Ser Tobin shrieked.

Alyn had filled the boots from the trough, with Perkin's bucket, and set the bucket down again at Perkin's feet. Ser Tobin, who had put his feet into them without looking, hopped about on the stones cursing in a high clear voice, with water running out of his cuffs, and every man on the bench was bent double. "Fenwick!" cried Alyn, a long way off and wide-eyed. "Fenwick did it! Fenwick, you wretch! I saw him with the bucket!" Perkin stood open-mouthed over the bucket, and the gap in his teeth showed. "That's not—I only—" and Ser Tobin, scarlet and laughing, started after him across the yard in one boot.

Orryn watched, and said nothing, and was not proud of it.

*

It was Ser Lucan Marwick, Osric's knight, who stopped the fun. He had not sat all morning. He had stood in the full sun at the end of the gallery with his hands behind him, and when the laughter died, he said, "That was a very pretty display." Everyone looked at him. He was tall and square and exact, with cropped dark hair, and he did not seem to sweat. "You have fought for a quarter-hour in the shade of your own conceit. A man who cannot run in harness cannot fight in it. Gambesons on. Hauberks on, for those who have them. The lane, to the beech and back. Three times."

"Ser Lucan," said Ser Wyman Latham, getting up from the bench. He was Piers's knight and the master of horse, lean and weathered, and he looked at the sky first. "It is past the hottest hour, but it is not much past. The horses have not been out since dawn, for this."

"The horses are not squires."

"Aye, and a squire has less sense than a horse, and is a good deal harder to replace."

"A joke, Ser Wyman." Lucan's tone gave nothing away. "Noted. Run."

The squires complained, Alyn loudest, about the heat and the harness and the knight who had thought of it. They did it with all their breath, which was a mistake, because there was no breath to spare. "It is all very well to say Stormland breeding will stand it," said Ser Lucan, to the air, and it was a moment before Orryn understood that this was for him. "I have seen the Stormland idea of discipline."

"My lord grandfather held the Reach border with fewer men than you've got squires," said Orryn, before he could think, and his voice was very small in the yard. Nobody laughed. Ser Lucan looked at him for a long, flat moment, as at a thing in a ledger.

"Then you will run well," he said.

They ran. The lane was a hundred and fifty paces to the beech, and the dust rose up past their knees. Orryn was last by the end of the first lap, as Piers had said he would be, and by the end of the second he had stopped being able to think of anything but his feet. The gambeson was a furnace. His head sang. Ahead of him, a long way ahead, Osric ran with the exact, upright, unhurried stride of a man who has been told to and will not be seen to hurry, and Orryn watched his back, because there was nothing else to do. Alyn dropped out at the beech and was sick behind it, and came back, grey and laughing, and finished. Perkin stopped, and Ser Tobin shouted "Fenwick, for the love of the Seven," and Perkin went on. Baldric ran like a man doing a sum.

On the third lap Osric stopped sweating. Orryn saw it, in the way that you see a thing you have no business knowing: the shine went off the back of his neck, and the hair on it stood dry and pale in the glare. He did not slow. He ran on, and then he began to run a little crookedly, as the road bends, and then he ran into the beech, and sat down with great care against the trunk, and fell over.

Orryn was there first. He did not decide to be. He was already on his knees in the dust with his own pulse hammering in his ears, and Osric's face under his hands was the colour of a boiled cloth and dry as paper, and hot, much hotter than anything living should be. "Osric." Nothing. The pale eyes were half open and not looking at anything. "Osric, you've stopped—"

"Have I finished?" said Osric. It was quite clear, and very polite, and addressed to nobody. "I did not hear the call."

"Water!" Orryn shouted. It came out a croak. "Get his jack off, get it off, somebody—"

Alyn was there, and it was Alyn's hands, strangely, that got the buckles undone, steady for all that he was shaking. "Don't pour it all at once," he said, in a voice Orryn had never heard from him. "Down the neck. Wet the cloth. Under his arms. It is how we did it with my brother after the hunt." Baldric was on the other side with the cloth. Ser Wyman came up the lane at a run, which Orryn had never seen, and Ser Everard behind him with his doublet unbuttoned at last and his face, quite suddenly, sober.

"Shade," said Ser Wyman. "Carry him. Not the sun—"

"The well's low," said Baldric, in an odd flat voice, to nobody. "I measured the rope at dawn. It came up two hands shorter than on Monday."

"How many buckets?" said Orryn. It was the only thing he could think to ask.

"Four before it fills again," said Baldric. "Five, if the Mother is kind."

"Then two for Osric, one for the horses, one for whoever's next. Nobody else drinks from the yard well till dusk. Send to the brook for the rest." It came out in a voice he did not know he owned, and he could not have said where he had got it, unless it was from listening to Master Verrall. Ser Wyman gave one short nod, which was all the order it needed, and Alyn looked up, and said nothing, and went.

They carried Osric to the gallery and laid him on the bench in the shade where the cider had been. Ser Tobin was not laughing now. "Seven hells," he said, softly, over and over, with a wet boot in his hand. Ser Bertram Corliss, Baldric's knight, a lean grizzled man who had not said a word all noon, had taken off his own cloak and was fanning with it. Ser Lucan stood where he had been, very straight, with his hands behind him.

"He did not call a halt," said Ser Lucan.

"No," said Ser Wyman, without turning round. "He wouldn't. That is the fault of an order that cannot be refused." Ser Lucan's face did not change. But he did not speak again.

Piers had come up behind the others, white under his burn, with the chalk still in his fist. "I had him at a hundred to one," he said, to Baldric, not loudly. Baldric took the chalk from him and put it in his pocket, and they stood there together.

It was a long quarter-hour. The colour came back to Osric's face slowly, and it was Orryn who was holding the wet cloth on his neck when the pale eyes found his and knew him.

"Orryn," said Osric. His lips were cracked. "Did I stop?"

"At the beech. You fell over."

"I did not call halt." He said it exactly, as a man dictates a fact. "That was mine to call. It was mine."

"Osric—"

"I am bound to say so," said Osric, and shut his eyes.

*

Lord Alaric came through the stable arch with Master Verrall trotting at his elbow, and he did not stop at the edge of anything. He was a young man still, hardly past thirty, and in the heat he looked it: no doublet, his shirt open at the throat and plastered to his shoulders, his sleeves shoved to the elbow, dust to the knees from the brook road, and a sunburn across his nose that was going to peel. He had been in the middle of saying something about a sluice. He saw the bench, and the knot of men about it, and left the sentence where it lay.

"Who's down?"

"Osric, my lord," said Orryn.

Lord Alaric crouched beside the bench and put two fingers under Osric's jaw, and then the back of his hand against his forehead, and thumbed up an eyelid, with the brisk, unbothered manner of a man who had done it to hay-hands. "Hot as a loaf," he said. "Who stripped him?"

"Alyn did," said Orryn, since Alyn had not come back. "And Baldric."

"Good. Keep the cloths wet and keep him out of the light. When he can hold it down he drinks, a little, often. Not a bellyful, or he'll lose the lot on your boots."

Osric's eyes had opened again. "My lord. I did not call—"

"You fell over, Osric. Nobody calls halt after that." Lord Alaric stood, dusting his knees, and for a moment he looked almost amused. "Shut your eyes. I have seen men go down like felled trees at the haying, and the ones who tell me about it afterwards are the ones who get up."

He turned to the bench. Ser Tobin held up the wet boot, as one holds up evidence, and opened his mouth, and Lord Alaric looked at the boot, and the puddle, and the chalk on the wall, and the cider, and appeared to decide that he did not want to know. "Three laps in harness," he said instead, to Ser Lucan, quite mildly. "At noon."

"A knight must be able to bear it, my lord."

"So he must. And I must be able to feed the horses, and I would as soon not write to Ser Marlon that I have cooked his son in my yard. Dawn, ser, from now until it breaks. At noon the squires sit in the shade and do nothing, which is what the rest of us have been doing." Ser Tobin laughed, once, and stopped. Ser Lucan bowed, a small exact bow, and his face showed nothing at all, which Orryn thought was worse.

Then, to Orryn's alarm, the lord's eyes came round to him. "Who put the water on a ration?"

Orryn's mouth had gone dry. "I did, my lord. Baldric counted."

"Then keep to it. Verrall says the well is down two hands in a week, and the brook is not much better, and I have just walked up it to find out why." He wiped his face on his sleeve and left a streak. "It's the pond at Fallow's lower mill. Hob Marsh has been filling it since midsummer and he won't open the sluice without my word, and I do not mean to go and give it with my shirt like this, so I am going to have a drink, and then I am going down to look at it myself. Orryn. With me. Find Alyn, and a cart, and every barrel that will hold water."

It was an order, and there was no sense in it of being tested. Orryn thought that was the first time Lord Alaric had asked him along to something that was a lord's work and not a squire's.

What does Orryn do?

1. Go with Lord Alaric, find Alyn and the cart, and see the brook and the lower mill for himself.

2. Ask leave to stay until Osric can sit up. He will want to talk, about Ser Lucan, and about what a lord's word at noon does to a knight's good opinion of his squire.

3. Square it with Perkin and Ser Tobin about the boots, even if it costs him Alyn's good humour.

4. Something else of your own.
