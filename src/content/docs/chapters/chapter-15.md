Orryn said nothing, and it was the hardest thing he had ever done.

Every part of him wanted to speak. His mouth had already opened; the first words had gathered behind his teeth like water behind a sluice. My lord, there is something more. But he had told himself in the long ride down that he would not, and a promise made to oneself on a horse seemed a very frail thing now, with the yard gone still and the cart rolling to a halt across the cobbles. So he shut his mouth. He folded his hands behind him, where no one could see them shake, and looked at Wat.

Wat climbed down. He did it slowly, one careful leg after the other, and shook out his coat. Then he came to stand before the lord with the little book held flat against his chest, in both hands, as a septon holds a holy text, and waited to be told.

"Not here," said Lord Alaric. He glanced round the yard: at the stable-lads pretending to curry a horse that was already clean, at the grey-haired woman who had forgotten her goose, at the man with the paper beside the grain-cart, who had not yet been given leave to go. "Verrall. Clear the yard, if you will. The grain can wait until morning, and so can the man who counted it wrong." His eyes came back. "Upstairs. The two of you."

So they went up.

The upper room was not what Orryn had imagined. He had pictured a solar, with tapestries and a fire, such as Alester kept at Grandview. It was a long, low, whitewashed chamber over the gatehouse with three narrow windows, and it had in it a table, two chairs, a shelf of ledgers bound in red calf, and nothing else at all. A tall desk stood under the western window, with a stool in front of it; on the desk lay a pen, a knife, a sandbox, and a single sheet of paper ruled in careful columns. Through the window, the last light was going from the orchards, the long even rows turning from gold to grey.

Alaric did not sit. He went to the window and stood with his back to them, looking out, and Orryn understood that this was how he listened.

"Read," he said.

Wat opened the book. He did not clear his throat, or smooth the page, or do any of the things a nervous man does. He simply began, in that soft, flat, level voice, as if he were reading out the price of oats.

"Departed Grandview at the third hour, the day after the wedding. Master Orryn Grandison on the bench between Pate and myself, no horse having been provided at Grandview for him." There was the very slightest pause on that. "Arrived at Sedgeford ford at noon. Ser Osmund Sedgley. A rope across the road. Toll two silver stags and three pence, Reach-livery rate. Master Orryn named your lordship; the name was of no avail. Master Orryn directed me to pay the toll from your lordship's purse, which I did on his word. The clerk entered it: by order of the boy."

Orryn's cheeks went hot. He kept his eyes on the whitewash.

"Ser Jon Penrose came to the far bank. He offered a horse from Ser Osmund's stable, by order of his mother. Master Orryn asked why. Ser Jon said Lady Alysanne had wished it. Master Orryn accepted the dun gelding, Rook. A slip was signed. Master Orryn sent a message of thanks to Lady Alysanne by Ser Jon's hand." Wat turned a page, with a small dry sound. "Rode on alone ahead of the cart for a mile and a half. Overtaken at the boundary stone. Master Orryn said nothing of it, nor did I ask."

He had not known that Wat had seen him laugh. He had thought it a private thing, out on the heath with the wind. Now it lay in the book, and it did not seem to him quite so private.

"At the Pear and Plough, an inn in the Reach. We came in at dusk. Master Orryn was talking with an old knight at the bench. Ser Wystan, eighty-one, one eye clouded, a scar across the head. Sworn man to the late Lord Gawen Grandison. I did not hear the start of it. I heard the second half."

By the window, Lord Alaric did not move. But something in the line of his back changed, as the line of a wire changes when a weight is hung on it.

"Master Orryn told me the rest on the road next morning, freely, without my asking, and at his own offering. As he was told it. The old knight said that Lord Gawen waited nine days at Grandview. That Ser Roland Penrose rode in with forty men. That two letters went to Storm's End, and none came back. That Lord Alester wished to ride on the second day, and said so aloud at the high table. That Ser Steffon stood at the foot of the stair and counted. That Ser Wystan himself carried the second letter. That one letter burned and one was kept. And that the one which burned did not go out of Grandview, but came in."

He read it all in the same voice. He did not soften anything.

"Master Orryn told me, when I asked, that the old knight was in drink. He said he did not say it was so. He said he told it as it was told. That is what I have written."

He closed the book. The little click of it was very loud in the empty room.

For a long time no one said anything.

Lord Alaric stood with his hands behind him, looking out at the dark orchards, and Orryn had the strange impression that he was not seeing them at all. When he spoke it was in a voice that Orryn had not heard from him before: not cold, but very quiet, and rather careful, as a man walks across a floor that might be thin.

"My aunt Ellyn never spoke of nine days."

Orryn did not answer. He did not think an answer was expected.

"After Redgrass she wrote to my father four times in the one winter. The last said only that she had no more to say." He turned from the window. His face, in the failing light, was as composed as ever. But there was something at the corner of his mouth that Orryn had seen before, once, on the face of a groom whose horse had gone lame: a stillness held on purpose. "I always took it that she had said all she meant to. I did not think there was an arithmetic in it."

"My lord," said Orryn. His voice was a thread. "I am sorry. I did not know it was—"

"Do not apologise." The lord's eyes found him, and held. "You were told a thing and you told it as you were told it. You did not embroider. You told the man who would write it down before you told me, which was a risk, and told him why, which was another." A pause. "And you did not put it in your account for me tonight."

Orryn's heart stopped.

"I said all of it," said Lord Alaric. "In order. Every part. I watched you decide to leave it out, in the yard. I could not tell whether it was fear, or a plan, or only that you did not like to say it in front of the grain-men. I mean to learn which." He came forward a step; he was taller than Orryn had thought, and it seemed that all the light in the room came with him. "Master Orryn. This is a question of my own house. It will not be said at table. It will not be said in the squires' hall, or to my wife, or to my steward, or to Wat's mule. You will not repeat it, to any man, until I give you leave."

"Yes, my lord."

"Yes." He stopped. His eyes did not leave Orryn's face. "Now tell me why it was not in your account. Tell me, and tell me true. If you have a reason, I should like to hear the reason. If you have only a fear, I should like to hear that."

What does Orryn say?

1. That he was afraid: of what the story would do to Alaric's house, and of being the one to carry it into Grassfield.

2. That it was not his to tell first: Ser Wystan had asked it be kept from anyone who would write it down, and he had already broken that once, to Wat.

3. That he did not know whether it was true, and did not wish to put an old drunk man's story in a lord's book as if it were fact.

4. Something else of your own.
