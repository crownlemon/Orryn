The sept at Grandview had been built for a smaller house than the one that was now crowding into it.

Orryn made it a hundred and sixty, give or take the dog: seated on benches built for eight that now held ten, and standing along the walls and in the door and out in the court beyond, where the heat lay on the cobbles like a hand. He had stopped counting at the door. A count that cannot be finished is only a worry with numbers in it. He stood at the end of the third bench on the bride's side, between his sister's elbow and a pillar carved with the face of the Crone, and the Crone's stone eyes looked past him to the altar as if she had seen all this before and expected nothing of it.

It was the hottest day since the haymaking. The seven windows had been propped open and let in no air at all, only the smell of the court, hot horse and trodden straw, which mixed with the beeswax of a hundred candles and the lavender the maids had strewn on the floor and the sweat of people in their best wool. Somebody's lapdog was panting under the bench ahead. Orryn's collar was borrowed. It was one of Arlan's, taken in at the seams for a boy who was a hand shorter and a stone lighter, and it had a way of climbing his neck whenever he turned his head.

He turned his head anyway, because there was more to see than the altar.

Septon Hollis stood before the Father's panel with his arms spread. He was a round old man with a voice built for a larger room and a nose that went red every summer with the hay, and he had served the sept at Grandview since before Orryn was born. He had married Lord Alester to Lady Lynesse, and christened Arlan and Elenei both, and buried Lord Gawen, and was said to remember every one of them with the same mild surprise that they had all turned out so much like themselves. Today the voice was full, and it carried over the benches to the back wall and out through the door to the court, where it seemed to give up.

At his right hand stood the groom. Ser Addam Penrose had come to his wedding in dark blue, and had stood quite still through all of the septon's opening, the way a man stands who has been told to and has not been told what for. He was twenty, broad without being large, with chestnut hair someone had made him comb and a plain open face. Orryn had liked him at the gate, where he had been the only one of the Penrose party to look at the horses before the house. He looked now at nothing, with his hands folded in front of him, and when the septon said his name he started very slightly, and then said, "Here," as if it were roll call.

Somebody on the groom's side coughed. It might have been a laugh.

That would be Ser Jon, Orryn thought. Ser Jon Penrose was Addam's elder brother, twenty-two or twenty-three, and stood at Addam's shoulder as a groomsman in a doublet of crimson silk slashed to show cloth of gold. He was the handsomest man in the sept. He had a jouster's shoulders and thick dark-auburn hair that took the candlelight, and a way of standing with his weight on one leg as if the floor were an audience. When he caught Orryn looking, he winked. It was a very friendly wink, and Orryn looked away from it, having learned that friendly things from handsome people were seldom free.

Behind the brothers, in the first bench on the groom's side, sat their mother.

Lady Alysanne Penrose did not fidget, or fan herself, or look at the windows. She sat with her back not touching the bench and her hands in her lap, a woman near fifty in grey silk with the silver tower at her throat, and the clear stone in it caught the candles and gave them back colder. She had pale blue eyes in a narrow face, and silver in the auburn of her hair, and Orryn had been told in the days since the gate that she was a daughter of Garmund Hightower and of Princess Rhaena, so that she had Oldtown's blood in one half of her and the dragon's in the other, and had never been known to forget either. She had slept two nights in the garden chamber.

That was Orryn's doing, and he did not look at her for long.

He had stood in the passage on the night of the arrival with the iron key in his fist and three ways in front of him, and by midnight he had chosen the one that was least his. He had gone to his father. Ser Steffon had heard the whole tangle in the little room he kept off the steward's court, with a candle and the account books he had brought from home, and had said, without looking up from a column, "Your uncle gave his word at the gate to a guest, before her sons and her steward. A word given at a gate stands. Put her in." And then, because that was apparently all the counsel he had to give, he had gone back to his figures. Orryn had put her in. He had given the key back to Meg, who had taken it with the same silence she had used to give it, and Lady Alys, told in the morning that the green room was taken, had said only, "Of course, dear. How could it not be?" and gone away smiling. He had liked the smile less than he would have liked a shout.

Since then Lady Alysanne had said nothing of the chamber at all, either to praise it or to complain. Orryn had decided to take that for approval.

The bride came last.

They sang her in, the sept's six singers, with a seventh voice from the court coming in a little behind and a little flat. Lady Elenei Grandison, who was sixteen and the most easily liked person at Grandview, came up the aisle on her father's arm in a gown of cream and green. Her brown hair had been brushed until it shone, and she was smiling with her whole face at everyone she passed, at the lords and the knights and the maids at the door and the dog under the bench, and she looked, Orryn thought, like a girl on her way to a very good party. He loved her for it a little. He also noticed, as he had noticed at the gate and in the yard and at supper, that she did not look at the groom until she was nearly before him.

Lord Alester Grandison, Orryn's uncle and the lord of Grandview, walked her up as if the sept were his to give. He was tall and broad, with the dark brown hair going grey and the beard trimmed that morning. He nodded to people on both sides as he came, with that warmth that made grown men straighten when he looked at them, and the whole sept, even those who had been uneasy over the cost of this day, seemed to lean toward him by a finger's breadth. Orryn felt it too. He felt his own spine straighten. Beside him his sister made a small sound through her nose, and he knew that she had felt it as well, and was displeased.

"Who comes before the Seven," said Septon Hollis, "to take this woman?"

The answers were all in the right order. Addam said his name and his house, and the septon said the gods' names, and Alester gave his daughter's hand to the groom and stepped back. Ser Addam took the green maiden's cloak from Elenei's shoulders, which was lighter than it looked, and folded it, and Ser Jon took it from him and held it as if it might break. Then Addam put round her shoulders the cloak of his own house. He fastened the clasp with great care and some difficulty, because his fingers were not quite steady, and when it was done she looked up at him for the first time and said, quite audibly, "There."

The sept laughed, kindly, and Addam did too, and his shoulders came down by an inch.

They said the old words, each to each. Orryn knew them, as he knew his prayers and the order of precedence, and he let his mind go with them until it caught on a thing it had been sitting beside for three days: the sound of the groom's voice. He spoke the vows in the same plain, careful tone in which he had said nine words in the yard, a voice that seemed afraid of spending too much at once. Elenei's came after, bright and quick and a shade too loud, as if the vows were a story she was telling.

When Septon Hollis bound their hands with the ribbon, Lady Alysanne Penrose, in the front bench, closed her eyes. It was not long. Orryn would not have seen it if he had not been looking, and when she opened them she was looking straight at the cloak on Elenei's shoulders, as a woman looks at the price of something.

"Father, Smith, Warrior," said the septon, "Mother, Maid, Crone, Stranger."

"Bless them," said the sept.

Then the singers sang, and the bells rang in the keep, and the whole crowd rose as one, and Orryn's collar climbed his neck, and he found Alyssa's hand on his sleeve. "Stand still," she said into his ear. "They have to come down the aisle and there are two hundred of them. If you go for the door you'll be trampled by Lord Peasebury."

"I wasn't going for the door."

"You were counting the way to it."

He had been. He did not say so. At the front of the sept the bride and groom were turning together, and she was already laughing at something.

\* * *

Outside, the sun hit like a thrown plate.

The court had been swept at dawn and was now a spill of silk and steel and noise. Orryn went at once to the gate, where his work was. A crowd, he had found, was a different creature from a list, and while the list said a hundred and forty to dine in the great hall, the crowd said something that grew when you looked at it.

The first of the late guests had come in during the vows. He counted them as they came under the arch, in ones and twos and a cluster of four, red-faced and dusty from the south road. They were the border riders, Lord Peasebury's people. Lord Peasebury himself was a big-bellied, ruddy man with small bright eyes and a coat that had been let out twice. He was Lord Alester's oldest friend in the hunting field, and he came through the gate with both arms out, roaring greetings at a groom who had never seen him before. Behind him came cousins, so many of them and so like one another in the face that Orryn gave up trying to tell them apart and counted noses instead. Seven Peasebury cousins. Two of old Gawen's men, grey-headed, who had served Lord Alester's father and took off their caps at the sight of the keep. Ser Wystan, who was so old that the groom lifting him from the saddle was frightened of him, and who proceeded to tell the groom so. Four others who said they had been told there would be a place.

Fourteen, and four who might be called a place. Orryn wrote it on the back of the list with the stub of pencil, and wrote beside it two stalls, and then rubbed the last out and wrote three, because the old man's mule was a mule and would not be shifted into a stall already holding a horse.

"Told you," said a voice at his elbow.

It was Tam, the head groom, stooped and silent and shod, it was said, before he was born, with hay on his sleeves and the nearest thing to a smile that Orryn had seen from him since the arrival. "I said. I said there'd be more than the raven said. Didn't I say, young master? Because they always say less. They never count the cousins."

"You did say," Orryn said, "and I will say you did, if anyone asks."

Tam seemed to consider that the best answer he had ever been given. "The hounds are still at it in the old barn," he reported. "Ser Jon's man wants them moved. I told him to ask you."

"Tell him they will be moved after the feast."

"He won't like it."

"No," said Orryn. "But Ser Jon's hunters are in the south stable and Lord Peasebury's will be in the north paddock, and if I move his hounds into the paddock they'll be among the horses, and that will be worse. Tell him I said so. Tell him politely." He thought. "Tell him the hounds will be quiet when the feast starts. Dogs know the smell of a roast."

Tam's mouth moved, which was as near as he came, and he was gone. It was, Orryn reflected, the first of the day's small lies that might also be true.

By the time he had shifted Alester's three idle coursers to the north paddock to make the stalls, and sent the mule round to the old barn where it could be disliked by hounds in peace, the court had begun to flow toward the hall. He went with it, a boy in a collar that did not fit, carrying a list in his belt.

\* * *

The great hall of Grandview was the oldest room in the castle and the proudest, and for once it was too small.

There were four long tables down the floor, with trestles and benches filled to the wall, and the dais at the head with the high table spread under the oriel. Someone had hung the walls with green, and someone else had hung the green with ribbon, so that the old banners looked like drowned things in a ribbon pond. Orryn counted tables and seats until he was sure, and the sum was a hundred and fifty-one places for a hundred and forty-six people, the extra five being the Peasebury cousins who had been fitted in with all the grace of a boot in a shoe. He did not say so. It had worked. That, Maris would have said, was all that could be asked of a table.

He was seated, to his own mild surprise, at the second trestle, a long way from the dais but not quite at the foot, between his sister and a quiet, sandy-haired knight called Edric Wagstaff.

Ser Edric was the heir to Greenward, twenty-three years old, betrothed to Alyssa and soon to be her husband. He was a big sandy man, broad, freckled, with enormous hands and an expression of unalterable patience. There was dog hair on his sleeve, a pale curling hair that had clearly been there since the morning and would be there when he was buried. Orryn had known him for six years, in the way one knows weather. He was a decent man. Everyone said so, and said it in a tone that made Orryn wonder how decent a man could be before it became an accusation.

"Orryn," said Edric, with pleasure, as though the sight of him had improved the day. "I was telling Alyssa. Did she tell you? About the hound that went into the ford after the otter?"

"She told me," said Orryn.

"Did she tell you what the hound did?"

"Yes," said Alyssa, in a voice that had been stretched thin. "Three times, Edric. I have told him. I have told everyone."

"Ah," said Edric, with a great peaceful smile, and began to tell it.

Orryn listened, because it would have been rude not to, and looked over Edric's shoulder at the dais.

They were all there, laid out like a pageant, and he took them in as he had learned to take in a room, from the near end to the far. Lord Alester sat at the centre in a green doublet stitched with gold, with his wife on his right. Lady Lynesse had the soft brown hair under a net of pearls and a gown that was modest and elegant and, Orryn suspected, cost more than she had told her husband. She was already pleased with everyone. On Alester's left sat the bride and groom, Elenei laughing with her head tipped back and Addam smiling at the table cloth. Beyond them, in a descending line of rank and wine, were the Penroses, and the Hertsons of Hartsfold, Lynesse's kin, who had come down from the north in a body: her brother Lord Wilmar Hertson, slim and fair and handsome in a worn way, in a coat mended with such skill that only his sister would have known where, and the old dowager, and Ser Dermot Hertson, Alester's hunting companion, who was already on his third cup. Lord Cafferen of Fawnton, a thin man in a good coat who regarded the benches as though he were estimating the price of them, and his wife, who regarded Lady Lynesse's gown in the same way. And between them, so quietly that Orryn only saw her when she moved to reach for the salt, a small girl of twelve with a long fawn-brown braid and a green gown that had been made for someone larger. That was the Lady Alysanne Cafferen, Arlan's betrothed. The family called her Sanne, because Grandview already held enough Alysannes. Orryn had counted them, privately. There was Lady Alysanne Penrose at the high table. There was his aunt, Lady Alys, who was Steffon's and Alester's sister, and was married away to the Musgoods and had come home for the wedding. There was Sanne. There was Alyssa, who was not an Alysanne but was close enough to be confusing.

Arlan was beside the child, at the far end of the table. Arlan was Alester's heir, fourteen like Orryn and a full hand taller, athletic and dark-haired, with the grin of a boy who has won something. He had won, in fact, the ring-tilting in the yard the evening before, against Ser Jon's squire. He was telling the story of it to the Cafferen girl, who watched him with her large eyes and said nothing at all.

The first dishes came in.

It was a wedding feast, and Alester had been right about the capons. They came in on their long boards, golden, with the bones sticking up like masts, and the pastries behind them, and the great salmon from the river with its skin gilded and its eye replaced with a pickled plum. There was a boar's head. There were the lampreys that Ser Dermot had sent for, in a sauce that nobody was sure of. The wine came round, the Arbor gold in its green bottles, and Orryn watched his father's face when the first bottle was opened and the cork was held up for admiration.

Ser Steffon Grandison sat at the third trestle, as he always did, with his wife on his left and an unobtrusive space where his brother's favour should have been. He was a sturdy man in dark wool, with a trimmed beard and grey-blue eyes, and he ate slowly and counted. Orryn could see him counting. His lips did not move. His eyes went from the capons to the guests to the capons, and rested, for a very little while, on the Arbor gold. Then Steffon looked up, and for the first time since they had left the sept, his gaze found Orryn's down the length of the hall.

He did not nod. He raised one finger from the table, and put it down again.

It could have meant anything. It could have meant well done with the stalls. It could just as well have meant you see what I told you. Orryn took it for a sort of salute, because he wanted to, and was irritated with himself for wanting.

Alester stood.

Alester had a way of standing that stopped a room. He raised his cup and waited, and the hall, at its own pace, went quiet.

"My friends," he said, "my kin, and my lords and my lady guests. I will not keep you from the capons."

There was a roar of laughter, and Lord Peasebury hammered the table.

"Today my daughter has gone from my cloak to her husband's," said Alester. "I have not the words to tell you what that means to a father. I will tell you only that when Lord Ronnel Penrose and I first spoke of this match, three years since, I said I should give her to no house I did not honour. And there is none I honour more. I give you the Penroses of Parchments!"

They drank. Ser Jon, who had been waiting, stood at once with his own cup in his hand and his handsome head thrown back. "And I give you Grandview," he said, "which has held this valley longer than any man here has been alive, and has never, my lord, been in a hurry to make up its mind."

It was said with a smile. It was said, in fact, with such a charming smile that for the space of a breath no one in the hall knew what to do with it.

Then Lord Peasebury began to laugh, enormously, and half the hall went with him. But Orryn saw Alester's face go quite still. He saw Ser Steffon's hand close on his cup. And at the high table he saw that Lady Alysanne Penrose had not smiled at all. She had inclined her head, a very little, toward her elder son, in the manner of a woman acknowledging that a sentence had been delivered as intended.

They had chosen at once, the Penroses, when the Blackfyre banners went up. Grandview had waited. Everyone in the hall knew it. Everyone in the hall had been taught from childhood not to say so at weddings.

"That was well said," said Alester, after a moment, in a voice so cordial that it took Orryn a moment to hear the cost of it. "A man should be quick in a good cause, Ser Jon. I have always said so. And slow in a bad one."

"My lord is wiser than he looks," said Ser Jon.

It was a second small insult, laid over the first so smoothly that the hall mistook it for a compliment and laughed again, and Alester laughed too, a beat late. Then the singers struck up and the pies came in, and the moment was drowned in the noise of a hundred and fifty people eating.

"Well," Alyssa said into her cup. "He's very handsome, isn't he? That's the dreadful thing."

"Who?" said Edric, turning.

"Never mind," said Alyssa, and patted his arm. "Tell me again about the otter."

\* * *

Orryn was sent for in the middle of the second course.

It was a page of the household, a girl in green with ribbons in her hair who ought not to have been there. She did not curtsey. "My lady Penrose asks for the boy who keeps the rooms," she said, with a great show of memory. "At the high table, if it please him. She says he may bring his plate."

Alyssa raised her eyebrows. Edric said, "That's a great honour," in his earnest way. Orryn left the plate, and wiped his fingers on the cloth, and went up the whole length of the hall under the weight of the eyes of a hundred and forty people, as far as the dais and the three steps up.

Lady Alysanne Penrose sat beyond the bridal pair, with Orryn's aunt Lady Alys Musgood placed at her right hand, an act of kindness that Orryn did not trust. Lady Alys was Steffon's and Alester's sister, late thirties, brown-haired, with lively grey-green eyes and fashionable gowns and tasteful jewellery. She was the sort of woman who always seemed to be in the middle of something, whatever the something was, and when she saw Orryn she smiled with such a lightness of pleasure that he remembered, with a start, that she had once given him a honey-cake at six and told him the whole of his mother's quarrel with Lord Gawen's steward over it.

"My lady," he said, and bowed to both.

"Master Grandison," said Lady Alysanne Penrose.

She had a low voice, with nothing in it. She did not smile. She looked at him for a while with the pale eyes, as one looks at a column of figures that is likely to be right but has not yet been checked, and then she said, "I wished to say that I have slept well."

"I am glad, my lady."

"The chamber is better than I had looked for." She lifted her cup and set it down. "A guest does not often find the best room in the house given up to her at the gate. It was handsomely done."

"It was my lord's gift, my lady," said Orryn carefully.

"So it was." The eyes did not move. "Tell me. Whose was it before mine?"

It was a question of a kind he did not understand, and he answered it with the truth because he could think of nothing else. "I could not say, my lady. It was kept for guests. I think my lady Lynesse had it aired in spring, and Meg keeps the key."

"Kept for guests," said Lady Alysanne.

"Yes, my lady."

"And the green hangings. They are very fine. Are they a part of the room?"

He had no notion why she was asking. "I believe the maids hung them new for the wedding, my lady. Lady Elenei chose the colour."

There was a very small silence, in which Lady Alys, on the other side, drank a little wine.

"Did she," said Lady Alysanne Penrose.

She turned her head away. It was not exactly a dismissal, but Orryn felt that his time had been spent, and bowed, and went back down the three steps with the odd sensation of having handed over something without knowing what it was.

\* * *

It was Elenei herself who told him.

She came down from the dais between the second and third courses, with half the hall's attention going with her, flushed with wine and happiness and the heat of the hall, and a wreath of white roses askew on her brown hair. She took a turn the length of the tables, stopping at every bench, kissing a cheek here and a hand there, and when she came to the second trestle she put her arm round Orryn's neck from behind, like a sister, and he smelled wine and rosewater and sweat.

"Thank you," she said in his ear. "I saw you at the lists. You've been wonderful."

"I haven't done anything, Elenei."

"You've done everything. Mother says so. I said to Addam, I said, he's a clever one, my cousin, he counts things." She laughed. She had a big warm laugh, quite without malice, that made people feel cleverer than they were. "I'm so pleased about the hangings. Did you see them? You must have seen them. I chose them myself, in the spring. I told Mother, I said I'd have my green or I'd not be married, and she said I should have it, and I said it had to be that room because it has the south window, and it's so lovely at night with the moon — and they've done it all, haven't they, they've hung it, and the bed's been brought, hasn't it, Meg said she'd see to it—"

Orryn did not move.

"The bed," he said.

"The bed. The big one, from the old west range, the one Grandmother had. Mother had it taken up after noon. I wanted to see it done, but she said I mustn't, it's ill luck, the bride mustn't—" She laughed again and swayed. "Oh, I'm so happy, I could cry. You're not to tell Addam that I'm silly."

"I won't," said Orryn.

She kissed his cheek and went on up the table, a bright figure in cream and green. He sat with the cloth under his hands and the capon cooling on its board and a feeling, quite precise, of having been given the last number of a long sum.

The garden chamber. A room with a south window and green hangings. Lynesse's list in his belt, with its thirty-four guest chambers and its careful pencilled marks, and not one of them a bridal bed. He had gone through that list five times in the last three days and he had noticed that there was no bridal chamber and had put it down to oversight. It was not an oversight. It had not been written down because it had never been a guest chamber at all, and everyone in the household, from the lord down to the dairy-maids, had known it so well that nobody had needed to say.

Alys, green room (temp.). That was what Lynesse had written. Not the garden chamber. The green room. Alys had been given the green room for the time being, and Alys had been told it was Alester's promise. Alester had promised it to her, and then to Lady Alysanne, and had meant it both times, as he meant everything he said. And Lynesse, who said yes to everyone, had said yes to him and yes to Alys and yes to Elenei about her curtains, and had never once set the three sentences side by side.

And he had given the room to Lady Alysanne with his own hand.

He put his hand flat on the table. It was a very large table and the oak was cold, which was a surprise in that heat.

"Orryn," said Alyssa. "You've gone white."

"Is the bedding at midnight?" he asked.

"After the dancing. When the hall's drunk enough. Why?" She stopped. She had a quick mind, his sister, and it had seen the shape of the thing before he had finished speaking. "Oh, Seven," she said softly. "Not the green room."

"I must find Meg."

He was gone before she could answer, along the wall behind the benches where the servants went, past the pantry and the scullery passage and the stair to the buttery, all of it hot and loud and smelling of fat and of men sweating over spits. Meg was in the stillroom with a tray of honey wafers on her arm, an old, silent woman with a face like a dried apple, who had been at Grandview since before his father was breeched. She saw his face and set the tray down.

"The bed," he said. "In the garden chamber."

She looked at him for a moment.

"Lady Lynesse's word, young master," said Meg. "After noon, while the sept was on. The old bed from the west range. The hangings were hung last week. I did say to her ladyship that the Penrose lady's things were in it. Her ladyship said they were to go to the east room, and the east room was to be got ready, and was I not to trouble the lady with it till she'd been at the feast." She paused. "The chests went an hour ago. Three of the girls and two of the men. Her woman went with them. Her woman said nothing."

"She has not been told."

"I'd not know, young master." But her eyes said she knew.

Orryn stood in the stillroom with the smell of honey and bruised herbs and tried, as he always tried, to find the number that would make it come out. It was a quarter past ten by the bells. The dancing would begin in a half hour. By eleven the bride would be taken up. And the Lady Alysanne Penrose, who was the groom's mother and a Hightower and a Targaryen's daughter, who had been put in the best room in the house by the lord's own word and who had asked a boy, in front of the high table, whose room it had been before hers — she would walk up the stair behind her son's bride, and open the door.

He went to find Lady Lynesse.

She was in the passage behind the dais, the one that led to the solar stair, with her pearl net askew and a cup in her hand, giving instructions to a maid in the soft, pleased voice that Orryn had come to know the sound of. When she saw him she smiled at him with real warmth.

"Orryn, dear. Has anything gone wrong? It's all gone so beautifully."

"My lady, the garden chamber."

"Oh, it's lovely, isn't it? The south window — I said to Alester, it's a pity to put it to anyone else." Her smile was untroubled. "Elenei did so want it. Her grandmother was married in that bed, you know."

"Lady Alysanne Penrose is in it, my lady."

"Was in it," said Lynesse. "I've had her things moved. The east room is quite as good, better in some ways, there's the privy and the little —" She stopped. She looked at him with her head slightly on one side, the way she looked at a maid who has said the wrong thing and may need to be forgiven. "Oh, dear. You didn't — Orryn. You didn't think I meant her to keep it?"

"Lord Alester told her it was hers."

"He says things," said Lynesse. "You mustn't mind it. He says things at gates." She patted his arm. "She'll be quite comfortable. I shall tell her myself, after the dancing. I only didn't want to spoil her supper."

"My lady, someone should tell her before—"

"You tell her, dear," said Lady Lynesse, brightly, the way one passes along a dish. "She's taken to you. I could see that. I shall be so grateful."

And she was gone, with a rustle of silk and a smell of lavender, to find someone who would say yes to her.

Orryn stood alone in the passage. Down the stair, the hall roared. Somebody had begun the first of the dances, a loud rushing country reel that shook the floor. He thought of his father, three tables from the dais, with his finger raised. He thought of Alaric Meadows, whom he had met once and did not remember, whose letter said you will be exact. Then he went back to the hall to find Lady Alysanne Penrose and tell her, in front of a hundred and fifty people, something he did not entirely understand.

He was a quarter of the way there when the music stopped.

Ser Dermot Hertson was on a bench with a cup in his hand and his hair in his eyes, bellowing something about the old law and the long night, and Lord Peasebury was on the table beside him with one boot in a dish of lampreys, and the bellowing was taken up by the whole of the lower hall, the way a field of dogs takes up a bark. It was the cry for the bedding. It came before midnight, which was far too soon. By the time Orryn had pushed his way to the foot of the dais, Lord Alester was on his feet with his arms spread, laughing, saying yes, yes, he would not keep them from their sport, and the knights were going up the dais steps to take the groom's arms, and the women were rising to take the bride.

He saw Lady Alysanne rise, among them.

She rose slowly. She had understood that she was expected to go up with the women, since she was the groom's mother, and she was going. She had her hands folded and her head high, and the silver tower at her throat threw a sharp cold fleck across the wall. Her sons were on either side of the dais, Ser Jon with a grin as wide as a sword-cut, and Ser Addam being carried up by Peasebury cousins with an expression of helpless mildness.

"My lady," said Orryn.

She did not hear him.

"My lady Penrose—"

But a Peasebury cousin had him by the shoulder, large and merry and smelling of ale, and was saying that the boys went at the back, boy, with the torches, and had a torch put into his hand, and the whole river of them was moving, and there was nothing to do but go with it.

\* * *

The stair up to the south range was narrow and old, and the torches turned it into a throat of fire.

They went up in a body, nearly forty strong, with the knights roaring and the women shrieking and Ser Jon Penrose singing a very bad song at the top of his voice about a miller's daughter. Lady Lynesse's maids went ahead with candles. Elenei was in the middle of the women with her roses falling out of her hair, laughing so hard that she had to be held up. Lady Alys Musgood walked at her right hand, one arm linked in the bride's, and as the stair turned Orryn saw her bend her head and say something to Lynesse, and Lynesse laughed and nodded.

He could not hear it. He was at the back with the boys and the torches and the sweating pages, a boy among boys, jostled on every side. The crowd carried him up the stair and along the passage with its low oak beams, past the windows that looked down on the court, and brought him to the door of the garden chamber at last, where it halted, in the crush, in an eddy of torchlight and noise.

The door was open.

He saw it in pieces, over shoulders. The south window, wide, with the moon in it. The big old bed, with its carved posts and its heavy hangings, green, brand new, the colour of a leaf held up to the sun. Lavender in a bowl. Candles on the sill. A bride's room, made ready by hands that knew their work. And the floor of it bare, quite bare, where yesterday there had been chests and a travelling-desk and a pair of silver-backed brushes laid out on the table like weapons.

Lady Alysanne Penrose stood in the doorway.

She had stopped on the threshold. The women had gone past her, shrieking, to surround the bride, and the men had come after with the groom, and she had not moved. She stood in the light of the candles with her hands folded, quite upright, and looked at the room. She looked at the bed. She looked at the empty table where the brushes had been. Then, very slowly, she turned her head and looked at Lady Lynesse.

"Where," said Lady Alysanne Penrose, "are my things?"

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The noise in the doorway dropped like water through a hole.

"Why, in the east room, my lady," said Lynesse, and she was still smiling. "Quite comfortable. I had meant to tell you after the dancing, only the lords were so eager—"

"This chamber was given to me. At the gate. In your lord's own words."

"Of course. And with the greatest — we were all so pleased to have you — but it is the bridal chamber, my lady, you see, it has always been—" Lynesse stopped. She had finally seen it, a day too late, the way she saw everything. "Alester," she said faintly. "Alester, my lord—"

"And well given, my lady!" Alester was there, huge and cheerful, coming through the press with Ser Dermot at his heel and a cup in his fist. He seemed entirely unaware. "Well given and well kept! Did you rest? I told Lynesse, I said, the Penrose lady shall have the best of what we have, and I'd have you know, it is the best. It was my mother's marriage chamber. She was married in that bed. And Lynesse, and now my girl. I could think of nothing better to do you honour." He beamed. "And it did you honour, did it not, my lady? Two good nights' sleep. I could hardly lend it out for the wedding night itself, now could I! Ha!"

Lend. The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well. Orryn felt it, with a sort of cold clarity, in the soles of his feet.

Lady Alysanne Penrose looked at Lord Alester Grandison for what seemed a very long time.

"You lent me," she said, "your daughter's bridal chamber for two nights. And did not say."

"A jest, my lady—"

"I did not laugh."

It was not said angrily. That was the dreadful part. She said it as she might have given the date, or the name of a flower. The pale blue eyes were quite steady, and the blood of Oldtown and the blood of the dragon stood behind her in the narrow doorway, silent, and looked at the lord of Grandview, and the lord of Grandview, for perhaps the first time since Orryn had known him, did not know what to say.

"Dear me," said Lady Alys Musgood, into the stillness, in a voice of perfect lightness. "Lynesse, I did say it was the green room, did I not? That day at supper. I distinctly remember. The green room. And you said of course." She turned, with her bright grey-green eyes, to Lady Alysanne, and her smile was full of sympathy. "These things get so confused in a house at a wedding. Nobody ever quite says."

Lynesse's face went through three colours.

It was as if a hand had touched the surface of something. Orryn saw it, and did not know what he was seeing. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps an aunt trying kindly to ease the awkwardness. But he remembered Alys, green room (temp.) in Lynesse's careful hand, and he remembered who had told Alester at table, over the cheese, with a smile, that the green room was so much the prettiest in the house.

"We shall be glad to put right whatever—" Lynesse began.

"There is nothing to put right," said Lady Alysanne Penrose. "My son is married. That is the business of the day." She inclined her head to the bride, who had stopped laughing at last and was standing in the circle of women with her hand to her mouth like a child who has dropped a dish. "Lady Elenei. You have a very handsome room. I hope you will be happy in it."

"Thank you, Lady Mother," said Elenei, in a small voice.

"The east room, then," said Lady Alysanne, to no one. She turned, and the crowd in the door parted for her without anyone seeming to decide it. She went through the lanes of torch and silk like a ship through a harbour. She did not hurry. And as she came level with the end of the passage where the boys stood with their torches, she turned her pale eyes on one of them, and Orryn knew that she had known he was there all along.

"You counted well, Master Grandison," said Lady Alysanne Penrose.

She said nothing else. She walked on, and the dark swallowed her.

Behind him the noise came back, a great ragged wave of it. Ser Jon laughed, somebody began the miller's daughter again, and the men carried the groom into the room with a roar. The women shut the door in their faces with a bang, and for a while there was only wood and shouting and the old ceremony going on behind it, the one the boys were never allowed to see.

Orryn stood in the passage with a torch in his hand and wax on his wrist and a taste of copper in his mouth, where he had bitten the inside of his cheek.

"Boy," said a Peasebury cousin kindly, "you've gone the colour of a candle. Go and get some air."

He went.

\* * *

The wall walk above the north gate was the quietest place at Grandview, which was why Alyssa had it.

She was sitting in the embrasure with her back against the merlon and her knees drawn up under her blue gown, which she had undone at the neck, with a cup in each hand. Below her the court was a bowl of torches and tumbling shadows, and beyond the curtain wall the valley lay under the late moon, silver and still. The hounds in the old barn had gone silent at last. The bells had struck past midnight some time ago. She did not look round.

"I thought you would come here," she said.

"Why?"

"Because it's where you went when you were nine and Arlan broke your wooden horse." She held out a cup. "Drink. It's Arbor gold. I took it from the end of the high table when nobody was looking. Father will be one cup short on his count, and he'll never be able to prove who."

Orryn sat. The stone was still warm from the day. The wine was cold and sweet, and tasted of grapes in a land he had never seen.

"It was Aunt Alys," said Alyssa.

"I don't know that."

"You do. So do I." She turned the cup in her hand. "She knew about the bed. She had to. She's been in this house three days and she knows what Lynesse had for supper. She sat at table and told Uncle Alester the green room was the prettiest in the house, and she told Lynesse she'd seen it and was sure it would suit her, and she told Lady Alysanne—" She stopped, and her crooked smile came. "She told everyone something, a little, and nobody else. And then she sat there tonight with a face like the Maid, and said Dear me."

"It might have been an accident."

"Oh, Orryn." But she was not unkind. "It's what she does. She likes to be needed. If there's trouble, she's the one who explains it. If there's none, she makes some and then she's very sorry." She shrugged. "I don't say she meant a Hightower to be insulted. I say she meant something. She wanted the green room herself, did you know? She said so at table, the first night. She wanted it, and was given a smaller one, and now Lady Alysanne has been given a smaller one too. They are even. I shouldn't wonder if she thinks of it as justice."

"Lady Alysanne said I counted well."

"Did she? Seven." Alyssa drank. "She means you should have known."

"I did not know."

"I know you didn't. That is what she means." She looked at him, then, with her grey-green eyes steady in the moonlight, and for a moment she was not teasing. "Orryn. It's what they do. When a thing goes wrong in a great house, someone is to blame, and the only question is who is nearest. You were the nearest. You were fourteen and holding the key. If you want to be of use in the world you will have to learn to be the nearest and not mind it, or else learn to stand further off. I don't know which is better."

"Which would you do?"

"Stand further off," said Alyssa, instantly. "But I'm a bad judge. I am not nearly as good as you at being useful. It's the only way to be liked, and I never wanted to be liked, only listened to."

They sat. Below, a servant crossed the court with a basket and a torch. In the keep the shouting had thinned, the ordinary noise of drunk men going to bed. Somewhere a door banged. Orryn thought, with an odd flatness, of Elenei in the green bed with the moon at the south window, and Addam beside her, and could not make himself think anything beyond that.

"Edric told it again," Alyssa said. "The otter. At the table. You were gone, so he told it to me twice."

"He means well."

"He always means well. It's a quality of mind, like freckles." She tipped her head back against the stone. "I am to be married in two years. Mother says a girl should have her first child within two years of the bedding. She says it as if it were a recipe. I shall live at Greenward with a man who is kind and never lies to me, and I shall run it better than his mother does, and I shall be dead of boredom by thirty." She said it lightly. "Don't look like that. It's not as bad as it sounds. It sounds quite bad."

"You could—"

"I could nothing. You are the one who is going somewhere." She turned, and her eyes had a sharp brightness in them that he did not want to look at. "Tomorrow. Don't think I haven't been counting too. Father has said you leave at the third hour, and Mother has been packing your shirts since breakfast. You're going to Grassy Vale to serve Lord Alaric, and I'm to marry the otter hound, and you'll be a knight in five years, if you live, and I'll be a mother of two."

"Alyssa."

"I'm not sad. I'm cross." She pressed her cup into his hand. "Write to me. Not to Mother. Not the letter she'll read aloud at supper. To me. Tell me what he's like, the man. Tell me what the food is. Tell me what a squire does all day. I'll want to know."

"I will."

"And Orryn." She looked at the court below, at the dark windows of the south range. "Don't be so good at it that they keep you. Counting. Rooms. They'll make a steward of you if they can. They'll say the boy's so useful, and you'll be forty and still holding the key."

He laughed, which he had not expected to do. It came out cracked. She smiled with her mouth and not with her eyes, and drank the rest of the wine, and they sat on the wall until the torches in the court had been put out one by one and the moon had gone round behind the keep.

\* * *

He read the letter again by a candle-end in the room over the guard hall, with the shutters open and a smell of smoke still in his hair.

It was three pages in a hand he did not know, narrow and upright and very black, in numbered paragraphs. 1. You will be ready at the third hour on the morning after the wedding feast, and not before. 2. Two grooms and a covered cart will come for you. 3. You will bring your own sword. 4. You will bring two clean changes of riding clothes. 5. You will come dressed. There was nothing in it of welcome. There was nothing of unkindness either. It was a letter from a man who had decided in advance what he would need to know and would not write a word beyond it. The last line read I shall find out how well.

Orryn had read it perhaps twenty times. He thought, now, that it was a very good letter. He thought that there was no way to be wrong in it. He thought that it asked everything.

He folded it along the creases, and put it back against his ribs, and lay down in his clothes. He did not sleep.

\* * *

Morning came up hot and white.

Maris Grandison had packed his shirts, as Alyssa had said, and then had unpacked them, and packed them again. She stood by the cart in the inner ward, Orryn's mother, a woman of near forty in plain good wool with light brown hair under a cap, and her kind blue eyes were very bright. She had two bundles. One was his. The other, he discovered, held a loaf, a wheel of cheese, and eight of the small honey cakes that Alyssa had hated as a child.

"You've written to him that you have been given enough clothes," said his mother. "Have you? Because I could—"

"Mother."

"I only ask." She smoothed his collar, which was his own that morning, and a size too small, so that it did not climb. "It is such a long way. And he doesn't know you. And Alaric has always been a cold boy, even when he was a child, even at Ellyn's funeral. He never cried, you know. He stood there. I said to your father, there's a boy who'll be exact all his life." She stopped. "That is not a thing I should say to you. Forget it. Come home at harvest."

"If he lets me."

"He'll let you," said Maris, with sudden fierceness, as if daring the world. "You are a Grandison."

Ser Steffon stood at the horse trough with his hands behind his back and did not come over until the carters had finished looking at the harness. When he did, he put one hand on Orryn's shoulder, briefly, and took it away.

"I have your uncle's account of the feast," he said. "Eleven oxen, sixty capons, forty sheep, twenty-two casks of ale and eighteen of wine. He has added the border guests, which I had told him to add, and he has not added the Arbor gold, which I had told him to add." His face did not change. "You counted the guests correctly. All but one."

"Which one, Father?"

"There was a fifteenth. A groom from Ser Wystan's, who ate and left. It's of no consequence." He paused. "A man who keeps accounts will be wrong by one a great many times in his life. The thing is to know the sum before the sum is known to you. Alaric Meadows is said to be a man who can do it. Learn from him, if he will teach you. If he will not, learn from watching."

"Yes, Father."

"And keep a tally of what he spends on you," said Steffon. "I shall want to know the cost."

It was the closest he came. He nodded once, quite stiffly, and went back to his brother's steward with his hands behind him, and Orryn understood that the conversation was over and had been, in its own way, a great deal.

The Meadows men were already in the gate.

They had come at the third hour exactly. The bell had not finished striking when the cart came under the arch, a covered farm cart with a patient grey horse in the shafts, and two grooms in green-and-brown livery on the box, who looked at nobody, and a lead-rope with nothing on the end of it. They were Reach men, brown and neat. The elder one climbed down, touched his cap to Ser Steffon and to Lady Maris, and said in a soft southern voice, "Master Orryn Grandison, for Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep. We were told the third hour."

"You are exactly on it," said Orryn.

The man seemed to consider this. "His lordship will be pleased to hear it," he said, which Orryn thought was not quite the same as saying he would be pleased.

He had his sword. It was the plain short one that had hung over the bed in the room over the guard hall, which Steffon had had the armourer sharpen the week before without saying why. He had his two clean changes of riding clothes, folded in the way Maris folded things, so that you could tell where each one had been. He was dressed. He stood in the middle of the ward, dressed, and looked at the cart, and at the grey horse in the shafts, and at the empty lead-rope. There was no horse for him. He noted it, as he noted things, and put it away with the other things that Lord Alaric had not said.

Alyssa had come down in a blue gown with her hair not yet done. She did not embrace him. She put a hand on his arm and squeezed it, hard, and said, "Remember. The otter hound's name is Garnet. Do not forget to ask."

"I won't."

Ser Edric Wagstaff came across the ward from the stables, patient and enormous, and shook his hand with such goodwill that his fingers ached for the next mile.

He climbed up beside the younger groom, who made room for him without a word. His mother was crying. Steffon stood with his hands behind him. The cart rolled forward through the arch, and Orryn turned on the seat for a last look at the keep.

The whole of the south range lay in the morning sun, grey stone and slate roof and narrow windows, all the shutters still closed against the heat. Only one stood open. It was on the second floor, at the end, a tall window facing south. Green hangings hung at either side of it, bright as a leaf, and a girl in a green robe was leaning on the sill with her chin on her arm, looking out at the valley. She was sixteen, married, and still smiling. After a moment she saw the cart and lifted one hand.

He lifted his.

Two windows along, the shutters were closed. But a curtain moved, as it might have done for the wind, and was still.

Then the gate swallowed the light, and the road went down into the valley, and Orryn Grandison faced the way he was going.
