*Alternative chapter text; canonical status not yet resolved.*

Goldengrove, the Reach, 208 AC

The woman had come to take back her scythe. The bale of hay was a different matter.

“It was under it,” she told the steward.

“I can see where it was.”

“Then let me have it.”

Orryn had stopped with Rook’s bridle over his arm. He had gone to fetch the horse after breakfast, when Lord Alaric’s men were beginning to buckle things they had unbuckled only the evening before. A cart stood across the stable entrance. The woman had her hand on a scythe haft that stuck out over its tail, and a man in a leather apron had his hand on the other end. He could have taken it away from her easily. He seemed to be waiting to see whether he would have to.

“Your husband can cut in the upper orchard,” the steward said.

“When?”

“When there is something to cut.”

She took her hand away. The man lifted the bale far enough to draw the scythe out. Seed and dust fell from the binding. Orryn could smell how sweet it was from where he stood.

“He'll be needing that,” she said when the steward made no sign to give it to her. “Whatever you decide about the grass.”

The steward looked over his shoulder. Ser Triston Rowan was coming out of the stable with two bridles, one in each hand, which made him look for an instant like another man come to work. He handed the bridles to a groom.

“Give her the scythe, Wat. We lent her the iron. The haft is hers.”

The woman put the blade over her shoulder. There was a rag around it that had once been blue. She thanked Ser Triston, a little too late, and walked out through the smaller gate.

“Did she steal it?” Orryn asked the groom who brought Rook out.

“The hay? Her husband cut it. He cut ours as well.” The groom picked a bit of straw from the dun's mane. “They were to have a share of the second cutting.”

“What happened to it?”

“Ask the river.”

Rook trod on the corner of the groom's boot. He pushed the horse's shoulder with both hands and told him what he thought of him. Rook shifted just far enough to let the boot out and put his head down to nose at the spilt seed.

Orryn had been a squire at Grassfield long enough to know what they would have said there about anyone who let a horse eat off a paved yard. Ser Wyman could see a boy doing it from the far end of the long stable, even with a man between them. He would say nothing until the boy had got the horse and the feed where he wanted them, and then he would make him begin again.

There had been thirty-one measures of oats when Orryn counted for him before the stand-down. Fewer when he and Baldric counted again. It had seemed a large number to a boy who had only ever had to fill two nosebags at a time.

“Grandison.” Ser Triston was still there. Without the bright coat he had worn at supper, Orryn could see how broad he was across the back. “Will you ask Lord Alaric if he has ever put a price on a cart of hay?”

“He has people who do, ser.”

“I expect he has. I was hoping to speak to him.”

“Shall I tell him what about?”

Ser Triston considered him. He had asked after the wells at supper, before he asked after anything else. Orryn had liked him for remembering that there were wells.

“Tell him we have more than we can eat,” he said. “That ought to do.”

Orryn led Rook round the cart. The steward was writing on a small board, with the flat of his hand over everything he had just written. Along the wall stood four more carts with the same green hay piled high above their wheels. A man with a hooked iron was drawing one bale down and pushing another into its place. The bale that came down was dark at one end.

“His own fault,” the man said, to no one Orryn could see. “Tied it wet.”

The cart had been so full that Orryn had thought there would be no way to make it seem less full by taking off one bale. He could see the gap all the way across the yard.

*

Roslin found him in the passage outside the room where Lord Alaric was waiting for his boots.

“Is he handsome?”

Orryn had been thinking about a smear of blacking on his thumb. “Who, my lady?”

“Ser Harlen. I asked if you had seen him. You said you had.”

He had not said any such thing. He might have said it to someone at supper, who had said it to her. There were people at Grassfield who could make a story travel between the kitchen and the gate before the man in it had finished his dinner. He did not know why he had expected fewer of them in a bigger house.

“He is fair-haired,” he said.

“So is my brother. Do you think my brother handsome?”

He did, which made the question much worse. Lady Roslin watched him discover that there was no good answer to it.

“Did he seem fond of his mother?” she asked.

“I didn't see them together, my lady.”

“But you saw him.”

“At Highgarden. In company.”

“Then what did he do in company?”

Orryn remembered Harlen making room for him when he had expected every place to be taken. It had mattered more at the time than any feat with a lance would have. He tried to imagine telling Lady Roslin so and hearing the story at supper afterwards in somebody else's voice.

“He made a place for me,” he said. “When he needn't have.”

“Did his mother tell him to?”

“No.”

She considered that. For the first time she seemed less impatient with him than with whatever he might say next.

Behind her, Lady Joy came through a door with Myranda Webber, discussing whether a grey gown would make a woman of thirty look older. Joy had seen a lady at court sent out of a room for wearing yellow when she had been told to wear gold. Myranda wanted to know who had sent her. When Joy noticed Roslin she stopped walking, but Myranda went on two steps before she did.

“We're to have breakfast in the orchard tomorrow,” Joy said. “I've asked Myranda to come. You may come too.”

“Which orchard?”

“The one below the wall.”

“The women go there for fallen fruit at dawn. My mother lets them.”

“Then we'll have it later.” Joy seemed pleased to have solved the difficulty. She looked past Roslin to where Orryn was standing with the boots. “Are those his best?”

Orryn did not know whether she meant Lord Alaric or whether they had already got back to Harlen without him noticing.

“They're clean,” he said.

Myranda laughed. Joy looked at her, surprised, then laughed as well. Roslin took the boots from Orryn, opened the door behind him and handed them back to him through it.

“Now they're indoors,” she said. “I believe you can take them the rest of the way.”

Lord Alaric was sitting at the little table by the window in his stocking feet. He had been close enough to hear all of it, but he looked up at the boots as if nothing had happened in the world since he asked for them.

“Will he eat it himself?” he said, when Orryn gave him Triston's message.

“My lord?”

“The hay. Was it hay he meant?”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Then we'd better see what he wants for it.” Alaric put his foot into the first boot and held the second across his knee. “Ask Ser Lucan to come. And when I say we are to leave after dinner, Grandison, see that we do. I don't want a bed made up again on my account.”

*

They went down to the yard instead of up to Lord Rowan's closet. The carts had been shifted into the shade, where there was room for a man to walk along them and put his hand in at the sides. Lord Rowan did this without taking off his rings. At the second cart he drew out a handful and pulled it apart over the dirt.

“There's some sedge in it,” he said.

“There's sedge in ours,” said Alaric.

“There's none in the oats. Triston says you ought to have both.”

Ser Triston had brought a stool for his father. Rowan had yet to sit. Triston set it by the wheel where it would do no harm and asked the steward how many sound sacks there were. The steward named a number. Orryn did not know whether it was a large one until he saw Ser Lucan Marwick turn his head.

“Two carts for Grassfield,” Rowan said. “Hay enough to make sending them worthwhile, and oats to go with it. I can put them on the road today.”

“Set a price, then.”

Rowan did. Alaric looked at the cart and asked whether the sacks were weighed before they went on it or after. Triston said before. They talked about the sacks and the measure and what would happen to the price if a wheel went off a bridge. The steward took out his board. His small white dog came out from under the cart where he had been lying and sniffed at Ser Lucan's spur. Ser Lucan moved his leg to give him room.

“After threshing,” Rowan said, when they had come round again to payment. “I don't need your silver this morning.”

“You have very little of it in your hand either way,” said Alaric.

“I have hay in my hand. Your man can look at it until we're both dead, if he likes.” Rowan dropped what remained of the handful and brushed the seeds out from between his rings. “There can be more of it moving before the month is out. I want Armond's word on the crossing. His own word, with his own terms. A man coming to his bridge ought to be able to reckon the cost before he leaves home.”

“A man can reckon it now,” said Alaric. “If he has silver.”

“If he knows whether the man at the bar will take his wax. Your father-in-law has had enough of other people's wax. It seems to me we might leave him his and still get a cart over the Mander.”

Orryn thought of Hedgefort, and the stag that had been paid on barley given away. He had rather liked the tollkeeper's way of saying that no man's seal filled a purse. He had liked it less on the other side of the bridge.

“You can write to him,” Alaric said.

“I can. I think you would have a better answer.”

“And then?”

“Ask Ashford what he would charge to take grain over the Cockleswent. Ask him what he would send if he knew what it cost to get it there. And let me know, so I needn't ask Lord Leo for a clerk to go and find out.”

Alaric pulled a loose strand from the cart. The horses stood with their heads low in the shade of the wall. One had a bald place under its collar. Orryn had been looking at it for some time, trying to decide whether it was an old sore, when his lord spoke again.

“Jon Hightower sent grain and asked nothing for it.”

“That you know of,” Rowan said.

“He knew where it was wanted. He put it on a road. So far he has got further than any of us.”

“Aye.” Rowan took the stool at last. It was low for him and put his knees up. “Suppose it's you who want it next. Would you rather go to him, or to Armond?”

“Depends what Armond has.”

Triston let out a short breath that might have been a laugh. His father looked up at him, and he bent to take the dog's ear between finger and thumb. The dog shut its eyes.

“What does the rain do to your price?” Alaric asked.

“If it rains before the carts leave, I'll put covers on them.”

Orryn looked down at his boots. His lord could be very good at asking a plain question that a man would rather answer with a joke. He did not like it nearly so well when another man knew to do it back to him.

“And the men who cut it?” Alaric asked. “Have they been paid?”

“Some in coin, some in the cutting to come,” said the steward. He looked at Rowan after he had said it.

“If there is one?” said Alaric.

The steward laid the board against his thigh. On his way out of the stables Orryn had passed the woman with the scythe again, standing beyond the small gate while she waited for a horse to go by. He had recognised her by the blue rag. He could see it now whenever he closed his eyes.

“Then I'll have the reckoning with them,” Rowan said. “They know who their lord is.”

Alaric took his hand out of the hay. “So does Tom Costen.”

Ser Lucan had been standing with both hands on the top of his sword belt. One hand moved.

“Tom Costen drew steel in a hall that wasn't his,” Rowan said. “I know what he did.”

“What will you ask Leo to do with him?”

“Send him home for judgment.”

“And if Leo sends to ask me whether I think he ought?”

“You may say what you think. I'll have asked him to send my man home, with or without you standing there. If he does, I mean to have Costen answer for the knife in front of the men whose quarrel put it in his hand.”

“Will the widow get her spring?”

“She has her spring.” Rowan leaned down to scratch his ankle. His boot had slipped a little and there was dust in the fold above it. “If Tom's people meddle with her again, she may come to me. I expect she will.”

Orryn remembered the blood on the bailiff's arm in the lesser hall at Highgarden, and Septon Emmon binding it. The widow had gone to Highgarden already. He wondered whether she would have to tell the whole thing from the beginning if she went to Goldengrove. Ser Lucan had once made him tell the story of a broken rein three times because the first two times he had begun with what the horse did.

“I can see to the carts myself,” Triston said into the quiet. “If Lord Alaric is willing, I'll send him a copy of the weights. His steward can send the same back to me when he has them. There needn't be a man of ours in his stable after the horses are unhitched.”

“Nor a man of mine to take a reckoning at your meadows,” Alaric said. He considered Triston for a while. “Your father has smarter sons than I do. Mine would have asked to ride with the carts.”

“You have one son,” said Rowan. “Give him time.”

Alaric gave a brief laugh and went to the far side of the cart. Orryn followed because his lord's gloves were lying on the wheel rim and he thought they might be wanted. They were not. Alaric stood where the shade ended and the sun shone through the space between the bales. His face had reddened along the bridge of the nose on the journey, and the skin there was beginning to lift.

“Two carts,” he said. “At the price you said, due after threshing. I will speak to Armond about passage for any loads to follow. I'll ask him what he wants. I won't promise it for him.”

“Nor for Ashford,” said Triston. “We can ask there ourselves, if it comes to it.”

Rowan held out his hand. “That will do for today.”

Alaric noticed the hand. For just long enough that Orryn noticed him noticing it, there was nothing more. Then he took it.

The steward put away his board. He had the same brisk look as the man who had come to count sacks at Grassfield. Orryn thought he would have no trouble remembering which matters had been written down and which had not.

*

Ser Wendell Webber was in the passage to the stable with Robin Penrose and a young man who wore Wendell's colours and had none of Wendell's manner. The young man was holding a leather purse open. Robin had two coins on the back of his hand. He turned his hand over and caught one. The other fell and rolled to the wall.

“There's one,” said Robin. “You may find the other yourself.”

Orryn stopped with the gloves under his arm. Robin saw him looking down for the coin.

“You. Grandison. Go on, then.”

The coin was under a bench. Orryn could see its edge from where he stood. He had started towards it before Robin said, “The dun is standing ready, is he?”

“Yes, ser.” He remembered too late that Robin was not a knight.

“He ought to come home with me. I'd forgotten how sound he was until I saw him trot out this morning. Jon always did lend the good ones.” Robin spoke with the bright, rather breathless pleasure of someone who had only just thought of a thing. “There you are, Webber. A horse worth forty of your little stags. Will that content you?”

The young man closed the purse. “Is he yours to give?”

“He's a Penrose horse. Whose would he be?”

Orryn straightened. He had the gloves crushed against his ribs. “Ser Jon lent him to me.”

“I heard you,” Robin said, though Orryn had said it only once. “No one asked for him this morning. If I want him, I'll ask.”

“No doubt,” said Ser Triston behind them. Orryn did not know how long he had been there. He carried a small purse of his own and tipped silver into his palm without looking at Robin. “How many, Wendell?”

“Nine.”

“Eight,” said the young man. “He paid one.”

Ser Triston counted eight. He left the fallen coin where it was. It took quite a time to count out eight stags when the men who had come to the stable for their horses fell about the passage pretending they were waiting for someone to move.

“There,” Triston said when he had done. “And the boy has his horse.”

“He hasn't,” Robin said. No one was looking at him now except Orryn. “He's got the use of it. There's a difference.”

He walked away before anyone answered. The others began to move. One of the Goldengrove grooms found the fallen stag and held it out to Wendell's son. The young man closed the groom's hand over it.

“He gave it to the wall,” he said. “You're nearer kin to the wall than I am.”

The groom laughed. He waited until the young man was gone to put the coin in his shoe.

Outside, Orryn gave the gloves to Alaric, who had been looking for them in the wrong bag. He wanted to tell him what Robin had said. There was no version he could make short enough to say as his lord was mounting, and he could not bear to begin it with he said the horse wasn't mine. So he held the stirrup and waited until Alaric was in the saddle.

Lady Clarice came down to take her leave. She brought Joy with her, and Roslin came a little later and stood by her mother. Nobody spoke of the girl at Old Oak, or the girl at Grassfield. Clarice sent her greetings to Lady Jeyne and told Orryn that if he had no ink at home she would lend him some. Joy was looking over their heads while she said it. Myranda had come as far as the stair with her and stayed there, discussing a strip of silk that had come away from the facing of her sleeve. They looked like girls who would have much to tell one another when the men were gone.

Orryn found Ser Alyn by the gate. Alyn had on a good coat with a tear where the sword belt rubbed, and was holding the head of a chestnut that wanted to get at one of the cart horses. Orryn thought he had come to say goodbye. Alyn was there because someone had asked him to hold the horse.

“Tell Ser Androw I will write,” Alyn said when the chestnut had settled.

“Will you?”

“I said to tell him so.”

Orryn looked at him. Once he could have told by Alyn's face whether he expected an answer. He tried now and could not do it.

“Your lord didn't ask me back,” said Alyn. “I suppose I thought he might ask how I liked it here.”

“What would you have told him?”

Alyn laid a hand up between the chestnut's ears. There was a lighter patch there where the hair had worn under the bridle. He rubbed it with the heel of his hand.

“That I have a horse to hold when somebody wants one held. And when Ser Nyles comes back he'll have a better tale to tell than I will.” He gave the rein a little slack. “I'd go if I were let. You'd come to see me off, wouldn't you?”

The horse swung its head. Alyn took the slack back and turned to it with a sudden sharp word, quite unlike his way of speaking to a horse at Grassfield. It was a relief to have something else to watch. When Orryn looked round again Ser Lucan was calling him from the road. He was late enough that there was no time to find an answer.

*

They had gone half a mile when the first cart caught them. Triston had got it out of the yard faster than anyone expected. It was a small thing to manage, Alaric said when the driver told him who had seen it loaded, and he said it twice.

There was no cloth over the hay. Two narrow shields hung from the front board, where a man in the road would see them before he saw the driver. One was the golden tree of Rowan. The other had the flowers of House Meadows, painted on wood so fresh that the pale edge showed all around it.

“My lord father ordered the first,” the driver said. “Ser Triston had the other brought down.”

Alaric looked at the shields. “And when the other cart is ready?”

“Same on that one, my lord.”

Alaric drew his horse to the side of the road to let it pass. He could have sent the man back. It would have been no great distance yet. Orryn thought he might do it when he saw him lift his hand, but his lord only pointed out where a stone stuck up in the rut under the near wheel. The driver edged round it and went on.

As they came down towards the river, Orryn saw the woman with the blue rag around her scythe again. She had stopped at the edge of the meadow to let the cart go by. There was a girl with her, perhaps eight or nine, carrying a shorter blade on a stick. The girl watched the wheel turn until the cart was down the hill, and then she and the woman went in through a gap in the hedge. From the hill Orryn could see where they were going. The grass beyond the hedge was brown to the roots.

He thought of the apple he had wrapped for Perkin. It was still in the bag behind his knee. When he put his hand back to make sure, he found a little hollow in one side where something had pressed against it. He moved it on top of his spare shirt, where it would bruise no further.

By then the cart was well ahead of them. He could see the flowers very clearly when the board swung in the sunlight. He had never seen the Meadows arms on anything bought on credit before.
