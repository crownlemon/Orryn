Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

By the ninth morning of standing ready, the oats were gone from the farthest bin in the long stable, and Ser Wyman Latham had begun to look at the other bins the way a man looks at graves he has not yet been told are his.

Orryn knew because he had been sent to count them. Ser Wyman had not asked him; he had said, to nobody, that it would be a fine thing to know how many measures there were, and Orryn had been standing nearest. So he stood in the brown dimness with a tally stick and the smell of dust and horse in his nose, counting with his lips moving, while through the open door he could see Ser Bertram Corliss under the beech with his arms folded and his scarred cheek turned to the east.

The six riders had taken to sleeping in their boots. Each dawn their horses were saddled, and each dusk unsaddled, and each dawn saddled again, because Ser Bertram would not have it said that any man of his had been caught with a girth loose when the word came. Nine days, and nothing had come up the road but dust. The lower well gave a bucket now and then, after a long wait for the mud to settle. The beech had begun to drop leaves, small curled brown ones, as though it too had given up waiting.

“Thirty-one,” said Orryn.

“Thirty-one,” said Ser Wyman, as a man says the name of a debt. “At six horses to the day, with the colts, and the rest of the stable, and the hawks eating meat that would feed—” He stopped. He was a lean weathered man with a close grey-brown beard and hands that went very still around animals, and he had never in Orryn’s hearing finished a sentence that might have been taken as a complaint against Lord Alaric. “Thirty-one will do. You may put it back.”

He had not got the stick down when the boy on the wall began to shout.

It was a kitchen lad called Wick, who had been sent up with a bucket to see whether the road was still there, and he bawled it down into the yard as if it were the best news in the world: riders, riders on the Highgarden road, a knight and two spears and a mule. By the time Orryn had crossed the yard they were through the gate.

The knight rode a tall bay as straight as a lance, and Orryn knew the horse before he knew the man, and then the man turned his head and he saw the patch of boiled leather over the left eye. He had thought of Ser Robyn Rhysling a good many times since Hob’s Cross, and always as a pale blue eye and a voice that said say it again; he had forgotten that the old man’s beard was grizzled iron and black, cropped close to a long jaw, and that he sat his saddle like a man who had been told to and had never once questioned it. The two spearmen behind him wore Tyrell green with a cluster of grapes stitched on the breast. Behind them a thin young clerk in a dusty black coat rode a mule, with a leather case on his knees that he held as another man might hold a baby.

“Grandison,” said Ser Robyn.

“Ser.” Orryn took the bay’s bridle. His face was hot already, and the day was not half begun. “You are welcome at Grassfield.”

“Am I?” The pale eye went round the yard, the beech, the six saddled horses, the cookhouse smoke, the boys gathering at a careful distance. It came back. “Then the bay will have water that has not been through a goat first. Four gates since Highgarden, boy, and not one of them had a clean bucket. You said it twice at the cross, and better the second time. I wrote down your stag at Hedgefort too, Dudley Bushy’s bridge and a stag paid on a lord’s wax. It was not my place to speak of it there. Somebody will, I expect.”

Orryn had no idea whether that was a kindness. “Yes, ser,” he said.

“Where is your lord?”

“In the small hall, I think, ser. I will show you.”

Ser Robyn swung down, stiffly, like a hinge that had been oiled seldom, and stood a moment with a hand on the saddle until his knee remembered its work. “Slowly, then,” he said. “It does not care for halls.”

*

They received him in the small hall, with the shutters half drawn against the glare and the smell of old rushes in the heat.

Lord Alaric sat at the high table with his shirtsleeves pushed up and a half-eaten heel of bread by his elbow, as though he had been interrupted at it, which he had. Lady Jeyne sat at his right, in a high-backed chair that Verrall had carried in, with her hands on the swell of her belly and her face composed. Ser Bertram stood at the end of the table, and Ser Lucan Marwick across from him, tall and square with his cropped dark hair and his shut-door face. Ser Tobin had been fetched from somewhere and had not had time to arrange his curls. Lyman Verrall, the steward, hovered with his warm hazel eyes and his smile at the ready, and Maester Perrin leaned in a corner like a long grey question, his chain of many metals pale against his robe. Orryn took his place behind Alaric’s chair and tried not to look at anyone.

Ser Robyn did not sit, though he was offered it twice. He stood at the foot of the dais in his mended surcoat with the clerk a pace behind him, and eyed the wine Verrall had poured for him as if it might be used against him later.

“You had my letter,” said Alaric. “I asked Lord Leo whether he calls the Reach.”

“He does not.” Ser Robyn said it quickly, the way a man pulls a splinter. “Lord Leo has seen your letter. He has not called the banners, and he bids me say the Reach is not at war. I was made to say it over three times before I left, so I’d not soften it on the road. That is the answer, my lord. It is all of it.”

Nobody spoke. Ser Bertram’s jaw worked once. Orryn thought of the six saddled horses outside, and the thirty-one measures of oats, and the nine days, and found that what he mostly felt was relief, and was ashamed of it.

“And Ser Olyvar?” said Ser Bertram, so low that it was hardly a question. “He is not a man to sit and wait on a letter.”

“No,” said Ser Robyn. “He is not.” The pale eye turned to him. “He asked it in open hall, before his lord father, and was answered as I’ve told you. Six days since, he rode south. No summons. He took what knights chose to ride with him, Ser Denys Tarly, grandson to the Lord of Horn Hill. Ser Nyles Rowan, brother to the Lord of Goldengrove. Ser Gwayne Oakheart, uncle to the young Lord of Old Oak. Ser Harys Graceford of Holyhall. Ser Garth Oldflower, among others.”

“Lord Rowan allowed it?” said Ser Lucan.

“Rowan didn’t forbid it,” said Ser Robyn. “That is all I know, and I’ll not make it more.”

Denys Tarly, Orryn knew. He had seen him at Bitterbridge, twenty and thin and hungry-eyed, asking for hay in a voice that cracked. The others were only names. A Rowan who was not the Rowan he had seen, a Graceford, an Oakheart—he thought, with a small cold start, of the boy at Goldengrove whose uncle that must be—and a second Garth, who was not Ser Garth Tyrell and had no business sharing his name. He tried to picture them riding, six or eight young knights in the dust with Olyvar Tyrell at their head, and could not make the picture sit still. What came instead was the thing Baldric had said in the dark, that a man was not made a knight at a trough.

He would have gone. He knew that. He would have gone in a moment, if anyone had asked, with the sun on his helm and Rook under him and a spear in his fist like a boy in a song. And he found, very quietly, standing behind a chair in a hot hall, that he was glad that nobody had asked.

“And Ser Garth?” said Alaric. “His brother has ridden to answer the Marches with a dozen boys. What does he do?”

“Writes,” said Ser Robyn.

It came out so dry that Orryn could not tell whether the old knight meant it as a jest, and then he saw that Ser Robyn was not sure himself, and had gone faintly red above the grizzled beard. “I beg your pardon, my lord. I do not say it to mock him. He has written to my kinsman Lord Theomar Redwyne, the king’s Master of Ships, that the burnings in the Red Mountains be laid before the king’s council. Let the council ask who did it and why, let the king’s justice find them, and let the peace with Dorne stand as it stood. No Reach spear across the mountains, and no Reach lord making a war of it. He does not want a quarrel with Dorne made out of a burned barn and a few hoofprints. Those are his words, not mine.” He cleared his throat. “I come in his name, and in my cousin’s, Ser Arron Redwyne the lord seneschal, who writes down everything, as you will have heard. Blackbar rides with the same message to other holds, and Beesbury to others besides. I have this one.”

It struck Orryn, standing there, as the cleverest thing he had heard in a moon. He could not have said why it should. He had only the boy of ten whom Hollis had not found in his head, and the black frame of a barn, and it seemed a very fine thing that somebody had thought to find out who had done it by asking, and putting the name in a letter that a king would read, before anybody else was dead. It would be slow; he understood that. But a letter did not need hay.

He saw Ser Bertram’s mouth twist, and did not understand that either.

“That is a good errand,” said Alaric. “It is not why you have a clerk on a mule.”

“No, my lord.” Ser Robyn looked at the floor, for the first time, and at the wine, and at last at Alaric. “There is a worse one. Ser Garth asks something of you, and my cousin with him. It isn’t spears. It’s grain. The clerk has it by heart, which I haven’t. Read it, boy.”

The clerk read from the leather case in a thin careful voice like a boy at a lesson. Ser Garth Tyrell and the lord seneschal, in this year of dry wells, asked every lord of the Reach to declare what grain and fodder and carts he held, to the last sack, to the counters of Highgarden, and to send a tenth part of the grain to the depots appointed, there to be kept in common and given out to the lords and towns and steadings that stood in want. A tenth, said the clerk, no more. It would be written down, and receipted, and carried by the lord seneschal’s own carts on the lord seneschal’s own roads. It was no tax. It was a loan to the Reach.

When he had done there was a silence.

“Gathered where?” said Alaric.

“At the depots appointed, my lord.”

“Which are where?”

“I have not been given the names.”

“And given out by whom, and to whom, and to whose measure?” Alaric took up the heel of bread and put it down again. “Carried over whose bridges, ser? Through whose tolls? You were at Hob’s Cross. You saw what a stag costs at Hedgefort.”

“I could not tell you, my lord. I carry the words, not the maps. Ask the boy.”

“I will.” He nodded at the thin young man, who looked as though the floor might open. “How many sacks does Grassfield owe, by your reckoning?”

“It is a tenth of what is held, my lord. It would depend on the count.”

“It would,” Alaric said, and Orryn could hear that he was not angry, which was worse. “I will tell you what I think of your tenth, ser, and then you may carry it. I am the lord of Grassy Vale. It has its villages and its mills and a good many farms, and when the dry is bad it is I they come to, not Highgarden. If I send a tenth of Grassy Vale’s barley out of it, it is Grassy Vale’s tenth I have sent. When its children are crying for bread, I cannot send to Highgarden to have it back. I do not say your lord seneschal is a thief. I say that a man who feeds a granary far off while his own people go short has no answer when they ask where it went.” He put both hands flat on the table. “Grassy Vale is the only commons I answer for.”

“Whether it is right I could not tell you, my lord,” said Ser Robyn. “I was sent to carry it, not to weigh it. If I weighed all I carried I should never get anywhere.”

“No more would I ask it of you.”

“And who,” said Lady Jeyne, in her soft clear voice, “holds the other end of the tally?”

She had not spoken until then. Every head in the room turned, and Orryn saw Ser Robyn’s pale eye find her and stay. She did not look at him. She looked at the clerk, with a faint pleasant smile, as at a cook who has brought her a doubtful fish.

“A count is a very fine thing,” she said. “But a list of what a house has is a very handy thing for a man who means to ask for it.”

“It is only a count, my lady,” said the clerk, in a small voice.

“Of course it is.”

“Highgarden may ask, my lord.” It was Ser Lucan, flat as a board. “I do not deny it. Ser Garth and the seneschal speak in the lord’s name, or near it, and a lord may ask his vassals what they hold; it is as old as the oath. I would say it may ask. I would not say it may take.” He did not look at the clerk. “A tenth sent out on a letter is a tenth sent. I do not know the law that makes it a loan.”

“You will not find one,” said Maester Perrin, from his corner, a little too quickly. “If I may, my lord—the usual form of reply in such cases would be a graceful acceptance in principle, with a reservation as to the particulars of quantity. I could have it drawn in an hour. A courteous letter costs very little and may buy a great deal.”

“No,” said Alaric.

“My lord—”

“Plain, Maester. I want it plain, in my own words, and I want it on one sheet.” He looked at Ser Robyn. “Tell Ser Garth and the lord seneschal this. Meadows will declare what Meadows holds, by its own steward, in its own granary, and your clerk may stand at his elbow and see every sack counted. Meadows will not send a sack out of Grassy Vale. When Grassy Vale is fed, and there is grain beyond it, I will sell what is beyond at a fair price to any man who comes with honest silver, and I would sooner it were a Redwyne cart than a thief’s. And tell them that Meadows gave eighteen sacks in the open before anyone asked. That stands. It was a gift. It was not a promise of the next.”

“I’ll write it as you said it,” said Ser Robyn.

He took, from inside his coat, a small book bound in cracked brown leather, no bigger than a man’s hand, and a stub of charcoal. He wrote in it slowly, with his tongue showing at the corner of his mouth, and Orryn, standing behind the chair, saw that his lips moved as the charcoal did. When he had done he read it back, and it was Alaric’s own words, near enough, and flatter.

“I carried the ask, my lord, and I’ll carry the answer back, and no one will hear it from me but the men it’s for. I’ll say you gave it straight. I can say that and mean it.”

“Say that I gave it,” said Alaric. “They will make what they like of plain.” He looked round the table. “Verrall.”

“My lord?” Verrall came forward with his smile ready. He was already nodding. “It will be the work of an afternoon. I shall have the granary open and the tallies fetched, and every sack counted to the half-measure, and the clerk can have the figures in his own hand by—”

“By dusk. And I shall walk the granary with you.”

Verrall’s smile held a heartbeat longer than it ought to have done, and then was exactly the same. “Of course, my lord.”

Orryn noticed it only because he happened to be looking, and thought nothing more of it.

“Ser Bertram.”

“My lord.”

“Stand them down.”

There was a pause in which nobody breathed. Ser Bertram’s hands, which had been folded at his belt, came apart and hung at his sides.

“Lord Leo does not call,” said Alaric. “You heard. I said I would wait for the word and I have it. The six may unsaddle and sleep in beds. Ser Wyman will want his oats.”

“As you say, my lord.” The voice was perfectly level. Ser Bertram turned and went out of the hall, and his boots crossed the flags in an even tread, and Orryn knew well enough that if he went to look he would find Ser Bertram under the beech again, with nothing in front of him.

“He said no,” Baldric Haye said afterward, in the dusk, in the lee of the long stable, as if it were something that had been done to him. “To the second ask. I said, if the six aren’t riding, then Olyvar’s lot are, and the Marches are still burning, and there’s a place for a squire who can hold a spear. He didn’t even stop walking.”

“What did he say?”

“That a squire who rides to a war with no lord’s leave is a man who dies for no lord’s thanks.” Baldric kicked the stable wall. He was sixteen, square and pale, and he had been like this since the oats were counted. “And that his lord told him to stand down, and he would stand down, and so would I. Then he said, ‘You’ll have your spurs for something better than being counted.’ And went. Like I’d been given a present.”

“Maybe he meant it kindly.”

“Maybe he meant it as a bloody no.” Baldric spat. “Denys Tarly went. Denys Tarly who can’t sit a horse and keep his teeth in. He went, and they’ll be riding through the hills with banners, and I’m here counting barley.”

“You’re not counting it,” said Perkin Fenwick, from the top of a pile of old hay that nobody had wanted. He was fifteen and wiry, with the gap in his front teeth, and he had been listening for some time. “Verrall’s counting it. And Lord Alaric’s walking round after him with a face like a bad smell. It’s a lovely sight. I watched through the shutter.”

He rolled over. “Seven hells, it’s a second Garth. They’ve got a second Garth. They breed them like hounds. And a Graceford—Osric, your lot’s gone to war.”

Osric Dunmere had been sitting on an upturned pail with his hands between his knees, saying nothing. He was seventeen, careful and serious, with the silver seven-pointed star of the Faith at his throat, and he did not look up. “Ser Harys is my brother’s wife’s brother,” he said. “I have met him twice. He was kind to me, which he need not have been.” He turned the star in his fingers. “I will say a prayer for him. It would be wrong not to.”

“Say two,” said Perkin. “For the horse.”

“Don’t.”

Piers Fallow, who had said nothing at all, lay on his back on the hay with his arm over his eyes. He was the miller’s son, broad and slow, and he spoke now without moving it. “They’re counting the barley,” he said. “All of Grassy Vale’s barley. Father’ll want to know the number.”

There was a short silence.

“He can ask Verrall,” said Baldric, quite gently, which was worse.

Orryn had been waiting for somebody to say it, and found that nobody was going to. “It was a good thing,” he said. “The letters. Ser Garth, I mean, and the king. That they’re asking who it was, before sending men into the hills. I thought it was good.”

Baldric stared at him. “Letters,” he said.

“It might stop a war.”

“It might. And the boy at the steading’s still dead, or sold, or wherever he is, and we’re still stood here, and Olyvar’s still got Denys Tarly.” He shook his head. “You’d make a very good clerk, Grandison. You’d count the sacks and not eat any.”

It was meant as a jest. It landed anyway, and Orryn heard himself laugh a little late, and say that it was better than being a dead hero, which was true, and was not at all the answer he had meant to give. He did not tell them the other thing, that he had stood behind the chair and been glad. He thought that Baldric of all people would have understood it, and would not have forgiven it.

Lord Armond Caswell came in at the third hour after noon, with a dozen men and a great deal of dust, to fetch his nephew home.

Orryn had seen him last in the solar, a fortnight past, with a cup in his fist and Lord Alaric’s whole attention on the mill-pool. He was Lady Jeyne’s father, and lord of Bitterbridge, and a big man gone heavy, with a red nose and a white moustache and a long scar down his left forearm that he would show, Orryn had learned, to anyone who stood still. He had fought for the king at Redgrass Field, and had been telling it since. He swung down in the yard with a great sound like a sack of grain falling and announced to the nearest groom that he had not been so thirsty since the year of the Redgrass; and Ser Cleyton Caswell, who had spent nine days at Grassfield looking for reasons to be somewhere else, came down the steps with his bundle looking like a man let out of a cellar.

The solar was as hot as the hall. Armond took the wine Orryn brought him, and the chair that Alaric gave him, and the second cup, and told them about the road.

It was a good road, he said. It was dry. A man could ride it in half the time, and there had been a mule in the ditch at the Stile, which reminded him of Redgrass, because there was a mule in the ditch there too, the morning of the charge, a grey with one white foot, and his own sergeant had said to him—

“Father,” said Lady Jeyne.

“—and I said to him, Sergeant, I said—”

“Father. Matthos.”

“What?” said Armond. He blinked at her. “Yes. Matthos. Fine lad. Very fine.” He drank. “Rode in behind Ser Moryn like a banner on a pole. Tall as a post. He’s grown. His voice has gone down. I told him so and he said that it had done it a moon since, and I was not to speak of it, and I said that I would not.” He laughed, wheezing. “He has a split lip.”

“From what?”

“A boy, I think. Or a door. He said a door.”

“Which boy?”

“Jeyne, it is a boy of fourteen. He was fighting something. He wears it well.” He took another mouthful, and caught his daughter’s eye, and put the cup down rather more carefully. “A Rowan lad. A page. Larger than he was, I understand, which was the whole of the trouble. Ser Moryn had them both up in front of the hall and was very good about it. Said that a squire should not hit a page, and a page should not be where a squire’s fist was.” He was pleased with that and said it again. “He is a fine man, Ser Moryn. A very fine man. He asked me about Redgrass for an hour.”

Lady Jeyne’s face did not change. “Did he.”

“He had read of it. He said he had read of it, and he had questions. Where the line stood when the Blackfyres came down the slope—” Armond stopped. His own face had begun, very slightly, to light. “It was a good talk.”

“I am sure,” said Alaric, in a voice that Orryn could not read, “that it was.”

“And the lad,” said Armond, with some effort, and Orryn saw that he had remembered what he was there to say. He shifted in his chair. “He asked after Grandison. The Stormlander. Whether he had grown.”

“And what did you answer?” said Alaric.

“I said that I did not know the Stormlander’s height. He laughed.”

Orryn’s ears were burning. He kept his eyes on the jug in his hands.

“Ser Moryn came in on the second night,” Armond went on, “with twenty men and a banner, and sat at Rowan’s right hand like a son. Two nights. They talked half the first of Highgarden’s counters until the candles guttered, and the second of nothing, so far as I could see, and then they went into the lord’s closet and shut the door. And Ser Moryn came out smiling.” He frowned. “And Rowan didn’t.”

“Rowan never smiles,” said Alaric.

“Ah, but he looked so, don’t you see. He had the look of a man who has been given what he asked for and finds the price higher.” Armond waved a hand. “I do not say it was bad. I say the one went in and the other came out and Ser Moryn took his boys and rode for home. And Nyles—”

“Ser Nyles Rowan rode south,” said Lady Jeyne.

“How did you know?”

“Ser Robyn Rhysling was here this morning.”

“Ah.” Armond looked a little deflated. “Well, he did. To join Olyvar’s riders, two days before Moryn came. Rowan stood in the gate in his best cloak—in this heat!—and watched him go. Said nothing. I heard him say after, to his lady, that his brother was a grown man.” He considered it. “He did not say he approved. I would not have said he disapproved.”

“Did he say anything of Elyce?” said Jeyne.

“Of—?” Armond blinked. He had, it was clear, forgotten that he had come for that.

“Of the match. The Oakheart boy. Of my daughter.” Her voice was exactly as soft as before, and the room grew quiet around it. “You promised me you would ask him honestly. I should like to know what he said.”

“Oh. Oh, yes. That.” He rubbed his moustache. “He says he will answer Alaric in his own hand. In his own time. He says—” He closed his eyes to get it right, and said it in a flat imitation of another man’s voice, so that Orryn could suddenly hear Lord Rowan very well, grey and slow and not at all warm: “If a lord of Grassfield wishes to ask me for a thing, let him come to me and ask it, as a lord asks a lord. He will not be told by the Lady of Highgarden what I have in my own hall.” He opened them. “And that I might tell you he was sorry for your trouble.”

There was a short silence.

“Well,” said Lady Jeyne. She sat quite still. Something moved in her face, so quickly that Orryn did not trust it, and was gone. “So Lord Rowan and I have the same complaint.”

“Jeyne,” said Alaric.

“I only say it is a thing in common.”

“And the boy?” said Alaric. “Harys. You saw him.”

“Little cock,” said Armond, with warmth. “Stands this straight.” He demonstrated, rigid in his chair, with his belly out. “Hardly said ten words. His uncle had ridden through two days before, to join Olyvar, and the boy did not speak of him. Not once. He asked me one thing in the whole of the week.”

Nobody said anything.

“He asked whether your Elyce rode,” said Armond. “I said I did not know.”

Orryn’s heart gave a small stupid jump, and he looked at Lady Jeyne. She did not look at him. She looked, for a long moment, at her father, as at a man who has handed her a letter without knowing what was in it.

“She is learning,” she said at last.

It was nearly dark when he got to the stable. He had been sent for a cloak, and had stood too long in the passage with it over his arm, and when he went to put it away he found that the lantern at the far end was lit.

Elyce Meadows stood on the upturned pail beside the big bay colt, working a brush down its neck in long even strokes. She did not look round. She had not, Orryn thought, looked round once since the day he had been in the room.

“Grandfather has come,” she said.

“Yes, my lady.”

“Did he say anything about me?”

He thought of lying, and of what it had cost him last time. “He said the boy asked whether you rode.”

The brush stopped. It stayed stopped for the space of a breath. “What did Grandfather say?”

“That he did not know.”

“He doesn’t. He has never once come to the stable.” The brush began again, a little faster. She was twelve, and small for it, with her soft brown hair coming loose and her freckles very plain in the lantern light, and for a moment she looked like nothing more than a child whose horse had bitten her. “I shall tell Mother that you told me,” she said. “Or perhaps I shan’t. I haven’t decided.”

Orryn did not answer. He could see that she meant it as a kindness and as a hook at once, and he could not have said which she meant more. He could not have said she knew herself.

“Go on,” said Elyce. “Wyman wants you.”

He went. In the yard it had grown dark enough that the first stars showed over the wall, and out under the beech the grooms were leading the six horses to the trough with no saddles on them, one by one, in no particular order. Nobody had told them to do it quietly, and nobody had told them not to. The hooves were very loud.

Ser Bertram stood a long time with his arms folded and his scarred cheek turned to the east, looking at the road. Then somebody called him to supper, and he did not go in.
