The hammering began before the cocks did.

Orryn lay on the straw tick in the little room above the guard hall and listened to it come up through the floor: the flat knock of mallet on peg, a man calling for another trestle, the long scrape of a bench dragged over stone. Grandview had been making ready for his cousin Elenei's wedding for a moon, and every morning it found some new thing to do with the great hall that it had not done the morning before. His father had brought the family up from their own house eight days ago. Orryn had learned to sleep through the benches by the third night. By the sixth he had begun to dream of them.

It was hot already. The one window, a slot no wider than his hand, let in light the colour of weak ale and not a breath of air. He kicked off the sheet, found his hose by feel, and sat on the edge of the tick with his bare feet on the boards, fourteen years old and not yet properly awake, trying to remember what day it was.

Two days before the wedding. Four before the morning he would ride west.

He had been keeping that count for a week, and it had never once come out the same size twice. Some mornings four days felt like a month. This morning they felt like the length of the stair.

He pulled on his boots last, because they were the worst of it, and went down.

The guard hall smelled of tallow and last night's ale. Two men-at-arms were eating at the long table with their elbows out, and one of them said "Morning, Master Orryn," through a mouthful of bread, and Orryn said good morning back and went out into the inner ward, where the heat met him like a hand.

Grandview stood at the end of a long ridge of grey stone, with oak woods falling away on three sides and the ridge road tied to the fourth, where the gatehouse and the dry ditch kept the world at a polite distance. Three towers, a great hall, a sept, a well, the broad square keep they called the Old Lion because nobody could remember what it had been called before. The black lion slept on its field of gold above the hall door, hanging so limp in the heat that it looked like a rug put out to air. Rouse Me Not, the words said, and in his whole life Orryn had never heard anyone at Grandview agree whether that was a warning or a request.

His uncle Alester was Lord of Grandview, and the bride's father. Grandview held its lands of Lord Edric Baratheon at Storm's End, as it had for longer than anyone had counted, and sent him men when he asked and wool when he did not. His own father, Ser Steffon, was Alester's younger brother, and held lands of his own a short ride off, so that Orryn had grown up near enough to Grandview to know every back stair in it and far enough away to know he would never inherit one. He did not mind that. Most days he did not mind that. And on the fourth morning from now he was going to a different house altogether, a long day's ride west over the Reach border, to serve as squire to Lord Alaric Meadows of Grassfield Keep, who was his father's cousin and a knight, and whom he had met once, so long ago that he remembered only a hand on his head and the smell of ink and horse.

The kitchens had been awake since midnight. A boy with flour to his elbows put the heel of a loaf in Orryn's hand without being asked, because Orryn had been coming into these kitchens since he could walk and the cooks had given up pretending not to notice. He ate it standing, scalding his fingers, and watched a whole side of beef go past on a plank carried by two men who were arguing about nothing.

His sister was sitting at the end of the long table with her riding boots on the bench beside her.

Alyssa was sixteen, two years older than he was and not at all slow to point it out, with the same golden hair and freckles that made strangers take them for twins until she opened her mouth. She had a cup of small beer and half a loaf already torn up, and she was looking at the door the way she looked at a horse she meant to take over a fence.

"You're up," she said. "Good. Sit. If Mother asks, I've gone to the sept."

"Mother knows where the sept is."

"Mother knows where I am supposed to be. It's a different thing." She tore the heel off her own loaf and pushed it toward him, which meant she wanted something. "Septon Hollis does the wedding blessing three times a day at the moment. If anyone asks, I've gone to hear it. Properly. With my hands folded."

"For how long?"

"Until the ridge gets too hot."

He ate the bread. It was very good bread. "There's a rider expected from Grassfield today," he said. "Father said so at supper."

"Your cousin."

"Father's cousin."

"Yours once removed. Father worked it out on a napkin. He says it's why you're to carry Lord Alaric's cup and not some stranger's." She drained the beer and set the cup down. "I wouldn't count on it making him easier to please."

"I'm not counting on anything."

"You are, though. Look at your face." She grinned, the crooked grin their mother said would cost her a husband if she ever let it out in company, and stood, stretching until her shoulders cracked. Edric Wagstaff, who was to marry her, had once asked her if she knew how to count hounds. She had told Orryn the story three times and he had never once seen what was funny in it. "The Penroses won't be here before noon. Aunt Lynesse is in the buttery counting napkins and going white. Go and be useful somewhere. You'll hate it if you're not."

She was gone before he could answer, boots in one hand and the last of the bread in the other, bare feet slapping on the flags.

Lady Lynesse was in the buttery, with a wax tablet in one hand and a stack of linen in the other, counting under her breath. She was Lord Alester's wife, a small, soft-voiced woman with brown hair coming loose from her coif and ink on her thumb, and she looked up at Orryn the way a person looks at a man who might have come to ask for something.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "Or thirty-seven. There's a cloth missing, Orryn, I can't think where. Your uncle says the Penroses are bringing their own, but I don't see how that's polite."

"My lady, would you like me to see to the guest chambers?"

Her face did something complicated. "Oh. Would you?"

"I could go through them with Meg, and match them to the list. If you tell me who goes where." He had thought about how to say it all the way across the ward. It sounded much more like an offer on the stair.

"Oh, you are a dear," said Lady Lynesse, and put the linen down on the nearest barrel and the tablet on top of it, and found him a smile. "Whatever you think best. I wouldn't mind in the least. The Penroses must have the best of the rooms, of course. Alester says so, and Lady Alysanne is so particular. And Alys has been so patient." She stopped. "Where did I put the keys?"

"Meg has them, my lady."

"Of course she does."

He waited a moment longer to see whether anything else was coming. Nothing was. Her list was folded in the tablet's wooden frame, in a small round hand with a good many crossings-out. Orryn tucked it into his doublet and went to find Meg.

Meg had kept the guest-chamber keys since before his father was breeched, and she wore them on a ring at her belt that clinked when she walked, so that you could hear her coming through any part of the inner ward. She was seventy at least, with a face like a dried apple and no patience for young men with lists. "Master Orryn," she said, when he found her. "Her ladyship's sent you. Well. You'd best come and look."

There were thirty-four chambers fit to put a guest in, if you counted the three under the east roof, and Meg counted them for him by door, with her hand flat on each one as though it might want reassuring. Twelve leaked. She said so without apology. Another six smoked when the wind was west. The Old Lion had three, the best of the lot, deep in walls two yards thick where the heat could not reach. The rest lay along the gallery over the inner ward, where the windows were bigger and the beds were softer and the walls had been dampened by every storm since Lord Gawen was young.

As he went, Orryn found himself using the old rules. No chamber stands unaired more than a week. Grandmother Ellyn had said that, standing in the doorway of the room next to this one while a maid wrung out a cloth, in the voice she used when she meant to be heard in the next county. Beds turned, rushes shaken, lavender renewed, and the window opened at noon. A house that cannot air a bed cannot keep a promise. He had been eight. He had understood perhaps half of it and remembered all of it, and it had followed him through every guest room since, the way a smell follows you out of a kitchen.

The garden chamber was the last on the gallery. It was the largest guest room in the inner ward and the only one with a window on the enclosed garden, a south window, set deep, with the sun already lying across the floor in a long square. The hangings were green and had been brushed. The bed was made with the good linen. A bowl of lavender stood on the sill, fresh. Someone had been in that morning to open the casement.

"That's the best room in Grandview that isn't the lord's," Orryn said.

"That's what they say," said Meg.

"The Penroses should have it. It's for Lady Alysanne, if she's as particular as my aunt says."

Meg looked at him, and he had the strong impression that she was deciding how much of the truth he was old enough for. "Garden chamber's Lady Alys's," she said. "When she's done sulking in the green room. Lord Alester said so at table, the night she came. Said she should have the sun for her bones."

"Lady Alys is my aunt."

"She's a good many people's aunt."

"Did Lord Alester say anything of the Penroses?"

"He says a great many things," said Meg, with perfect courtesy, and rattled her keys. "Her ladyship's list will tell you, if it's on it."

It was not on it. Orryn took out the folded list and went down it with his finger, twice. Lady Alysanne Penrose, with sons and women: good rooms. Nothing about the garden chamber, and nothing about Alys but a line at the bottom in a different, hastier ink: Alys, green room (temp.). He could read temp. two ways, and could not tell which his aunt Lynesse had meant.

He wrote garden chamber beside Lady Alysanne's name in pencil and left the bowl of lavender alone.

The head groom was a stooped, silent man called Tam who had been shod, it was said, before he was born, and he took the news of twenty-eight horses the way other men took bad weather. "Twenty-eight," he said. "On top of the hunters. On top of what your uncle's friends bring."

"That is what their raven said. Plus whatever Lord Alester's friends bring." Orryn showed him the figures. He had done them twice in the shade of the gatehouse arch with the list on his knee, and they had come out the same both times, which was reassuring. "If we put the hounds in the old barn, and the hunters in the south stable together, we free eleven stalls in the east. That gives us the room. We'd move them this afternoon."

Tam looked at the paper. He did not look at the barn, the stable, or Orryn. "Hounds don't like the barn."

"They'd only be there four days."

"They'd be howling four days." He scratched his jaw. "I can do it. But I'll want it said I told you."

"Said to whom?"

"To whoever asks." Tam handed back the paper with a fingertip. "Somebody always asks."

Orryn took the paper, and was a little disappointed, and told himself the hounds would settle.

By the time he came out of the stables the sun had climbed over the east wall and the whole outer ward lay shimmering. The ward stank of horse and hot dust and the green tang of cut rushes drying in a pile by the barracks wall. Carts were coming up the ridge road and unloading at the gatehouse, bakers and wine merchants, a man with live geese in wicker pens that shrieked at every jolt. He stood for a moment in the shade of a wagon with the sweat running down inside his doublet and thought, with an uncomplicated pleasure he did not examine, that he was good at this. The counts came out. The pieces fitted. Nobody had told him to do it, and it was going to be done.

Then he remembered the hounds, and went to find something cold to drink.

He found his father and his uncle in the great hall.

The trestles were up on the long side, the boards laid, the benches fixed; the dais had been swept and a faded carpet laid over its steps, and somebody had taken down the oldest of the shields and put up the newest, which gave the whole wall the look of an old man with a very new hat. Lord Alester sat at the middle of the high table with his boots up on a stool and a cup in his hand, and across from him sat Ser Steffon Grandison, with a sheaf of parchment in front of him and a face like a closed door.

It was always strange to see them together. Alester was tall and broad and loud, with dark hair going grey at the temples and a laugh that made hounds look up from the other end of the castle. Steffon was a sturdy, quiet man with a trimmed beard and grey-blue eyes, who read a room the way he read an account, from the bottom up. They were four years apart and might have been born in different countries. Arlan lounged beside his father in a chair too small for him, a tall, tanned boy of Orryn's own age with a blade of straw in his mouth, bored in the thorough, contented way of a boy who has never once been told that boredom has a price.

"... eleven oxen," Steffon was saying, reading. "Sixty capons. Forty sheep. Twenty-two casks of ale and eighteen of wine, of which four are Arbor gold."

"It is my daughter's wedding," said Alester.

"It is also my brother's roof. The steward tells me the Arbor gold alone is the price of the south barn."

"Then we'll tell the south barn it can wait." Alester's grin took the edge off it, as it always did, and Orryn felt it too, the pull of it, and was annoyed. "Steffon. Steffon. A man does not marry off his only girl with small beer and a cheese. Our lord father gave Alys a better feast than this, and we were half as many."

"Father also had a better harvest that year."

"There he is!" Alester had spotted Orryn at the end of the table and flung out a hand. "The very man. Steffon's boy, with a list. Come here and settle this. How many guests are we to put in beds?"

Orryn walked up the hall with the paper, aware of Arlan watching him with his eyebrows slightly raised. It was a long walk. "Thirty-four beds, my lord. Twelve of them under leaking roofs. The Penrose party will be twenty-four, by their raven, and Lady Alysanne has two sons with her. If your friends from the border come, as you said, that is another dozen or fourteen. Perhaps more. It would be useful to know the number."

"A dozen, fourteen," said Alester, with a vague, pleasant wave. "Peasebury's cousins. Old Ser Wystan, who will not forgive me if he's not asked. A few of Gawen's men."

"Then we have to put some of them in the hall."

"Then they'll sleep in the hall, and be glad of it. Do you know, I slept in this hall when I was fifteen, with a hound across my feet, and I never slept better." He drank. "You see, Steffon? The boy does his sums, and nobody's weeping."

"He's a better counter than you are," said Steffon, not unkindly, not looking up.

"He's a better everything than I am. It's what I say about him." Alester laughed again and Arlan laughed with him, too late and a little too hard, and Orryn stood with the list, unsure what he had been used for. "Raymont writes," Alester said, picking up a letter, "that Storm's End cannot spare him for a niece's wedding, and sends his love, and a cask of Dornish red. I call that a poor swap. I'd have taken the man."

"He'd have taken the red," said Steffon, "and left before the toasts."

"That's a hard thing to say of a brother."

"It is a hard thing to say of a brother who's earned it."

They went on like that, and Orryn, who had meant to ask about the chambers, found that there was no space in the conversation to put a question. It had the shape of an old argument, worn smooth with handling, each of them taking the same turns without having to look. He had heard it all his life in a hundred versions, and had always thought, privately, that his father was right and that Alester knew it and that it did not make a particle of difference. It seemed to him today that he had never once heard either of them finish a sentence the other had begun.

"If you will excuse me, Father," he said, and was not heard, and went out through the side door into the glare.

Elenei found him before he had crossed the ward. She was coming out of the buttery door with a seamstress at each elbow and a length of blue ribbon wound round her wrist like a bandage, and she hailed him across the stones as though he were a ship.

His cousin was sixteen, Lord Alester's daughter and the bride, with brown hair that had been pinned up three times that morning and was coming down for the fourth, bright hazel eyes, and a lively, open smile she wore as easily as other people wore a cloak. She made any room warmer by coming into it, and forgot she had left the door open. She had been kind to Orryn all his life, in the loose, unconsidered way of a person who is kind to the weather.

"There you are," she said. "Is it true you're doing the beds? Mother says you're doing the beds. She says it as though it were a great honour, so I suppose it is. Look, would blue or green be better with a garden? I've decided green, but Dorrie says blue."

"Better for what?"

"For Lady Alysanne's chamber, silly. The garden room. I had Meg take down the old hangings and put up the green ones I had made for my own room, because Lady Alysanne has a green gown, and I thought it would look as if she belonged there. As if we'd planned it all along. Mother says you can't simply tell a woman you want her to feel welcome. You have to do it with curtains."

Orryn kept his face as still as it would go. "When did you do that?"

"Oh, days ago. Meg grumbled. Don't tell Aunt Alys." She laughed. "She thinks the room is hers, and I'm not supposed to know. Everyone thinks I don't listen. I do. I just forget what to do about it afterward."

"Elenei—"

"Do you think he'll like me?" The lively face went almost still, and for a moment she looked younger than he was. "Ser Addam. I've only met him twice, and he said perhaps nine words. Mother says quiet men are restful. I should like to be restful. Do you think I could?"

He had no idea. "I suppose it depends on what you mean to do with the nine words."

"That's no answer at all," said Elenei, delighted, and a seamstress said her name for the third time, and she held up the blue ribbon in farewell. "Write me from the Reach. Tell me what the ladies wear. Don't say it's pears. Everyone says pears."

She was swept off toward the sept, trailing ribbon. Orryn stood on the hot stones, looking at the place where she had been, and thought of green hangings, brushed and hung, and the sun lying across the floor of a room that had been, as far as he could tell, promised to two women and given by curtains to a third.

The rider from Grassfield came at noon.

He came up the ridge road at a walk, as any man would in that heat, a long-faced man in Meadows colours with a leather case across his saddle, and he dismounted in the outer ward and asked for Ser Steffon Grandison with the particular weary politeness of someone who has been told twice what to say and wished to say it once. Orryn, who had been sitting on the well-rim eating an apple and watching a bored mare lick a bucket, was fetched by a stable boy at a run.

His father read the letter in the lee of the Old Lion with his shoulders against the stone, and his mother, Lady Maris, stood a half step behind him, her light brown hair tucked under a plain cap, her blue eyes on the page over his shoulder. She had come out of the kitchens with flour on her sleeve and had not stopped to brush it off. Lady Maris Grandison, born a Gower, had a way of arriving in a place just as it began to need her and giving the impression she had always been there. "Read it aloud," she said. "The boy should hear."

"The boy can read."

"Then he can read it after."

Steffon read it aloud, in his flat voice, with no particular expression.

"To my cousin Ser Steffon Grandison, greeting. I have your letter of the ninth. I will send two grooms and a covered cart to Grandview on the morning after the wedding feast, to arrive at the third hour, and will expect Orryn to be ready at that hour and not before. He is to bring his own sword and two changes of riding clothes, both clean. He will be lodged in the old keep, in the chamber adjoining the squires' hall. I will receive him in my solar on his arrival. He is to come dressed. I understand he can read and keep accounts, which is more than I can say for most boys of fourteen who arrive here; I shall find out how well. My wife sends her regards to Lady Maris and asks that you send his measurements for a livery coat. Respectfully, Alaric Meadows, Lord of Grassfield."

He folded it into thirds. "He's numbered the paragraphs," he said.

"Has he?" said Maris.

"In his head. You can see it."

"I'd like to know," said Maris, "what clean means."

"That you should ask," said Steffon, with something that might almost have been a smile, "is exactly what he'd expect."

Orryn looked at the folded letter, and his mother, and the shadow of the Old Lion's corner on the dust, and found nothing that seemed to belong to him in any of them. He will be lodged in the old keep. I shall find out how well. He thought of the lean man smelling of ink and horse and a large, dry hand setting down on his hair, and could not make it say anything. It was strange to think of that hand as his. It was stranger still that it had known what shirts he owned.

"What is he like?" he asked. "Father. Truly."

Steffon considered the question for longer than Orryn expected. He looked at the letter and not at his son. "He has been Lord of Grassfield since he was younger than you'd like to think about," he said at last. "And he has done it by seeing to everything himself. I think you'll find he knows the price of every nail in the keep. I think you'll find that's not nothing."

"Is he kind?"

"He is exact," said Steffon, and handed him the letter. "You'll want to keep this. He will ask whether you have."

There was no more to say. His mother put her hand flat against his cheek, briefly, the way she checked a loaf, and went back inside to see about measurements. Orryn put the letter in his doublet, against his ribs, and felt its edges all through dinner.

The Penroses came a little after the heat of the day broke.

The lookout on the gatehouse sounded his horn, and a ripple went through the outer ward: grooms looking up, women stopping with baskets, a clatter of boots on the barracks stair. By the time Orryn reached the gate there was a crowd, and Lord Alester was in the middle of it in a green coat he had not worn that morning, and Lady Lynesse beside him with her hands clasped, and Steffon a pace behind, with Maris, with the air of two people who were there because someone had to be.

From the top of the ridge road a cloud of dust was coming, red-brown and slow, and through the dust Orryn could make out horses, a baggage wagon, another, and a banner on a tall staff. The banner was russet brown, the colour of dry leaves, and across it were two white quills, crossed.

He had been taught those, the way he had been taught every device within a day's ride. Penrose of Parchments. A big house, sworn to Storm's End, on the north edge of the Stormlands where the roads ran toward the Kingswood and the king's own coin. Lord Ronnel Penrose kept that coin at court, as master of coin to King Daeron, and his wife was a Targaryen princess, and the wife was said to do the sums. His brother was dead, and his brother's widow kept Parchments in his absence. The quills were supposed to mean we write it down, and the words beneath them, if you believed the heralds, said so outright: Set Down Our Deeds. His father said the Penroses had never once let anyone forget who bled. His uncle said Penroses had a right to say it. In that, and perhaps only that, they had always agreed, though they gave different reasons. Eleven years ago, when Daemon Blackfyre had called his banners and half the Stormlands had waited to see whose way the wind blew, the Penroses had chosen at once, and paid. Grandview, under Lord Gawen, had waited.

Prudent, people said at Grandview. In Parchments they probably said something else.

The first rider came through the gate at a canter, well ahead of the rest, on a big chestnut courser flecked with foam, scattering a pair of grooms and a dog. He hauled it up in the middle of the ward and swung down in one motion and turned, smiling at the whole assembly as though it had been got up expressly for him. He was a broad-shouldered young man of twenty or so, with thick dark-auburn hair, hazel-grey eyes, and a well-cut riding coat; the coat bore two small crossed quills at the breast, worked in white thread.

"Ser Jon," said Alester, delighted. "Ser Jon Penrose! You'll have ridden all night."

"All morning, my lord. My mother set the pace." Jon tossed his reins to the nearest groom without looking at him. "I've ridden worse roads, but never one so bent on delaying a man."

A ripple of laughter. Arlan, at his father's elbow, laughed loudest. Orryn did not, because he was watching the rest of the column come in: eight men-at-arms in russet with quills on their shoulders, a baggage wagon, a wagon of women with their veils pulled forward against the dust, grooms leading spare horses, a very upright old man on a mule whom he took for the steward. And in the middle, a grey palfrey ridden sidesaddle by a woman in dark blue wool too heavy for the weather, whose back was as straight as a lance.

She dismounted without waiting for a hand. She was a narrow-faced woman in her late forties, with silver threaded through her auburn hair and eyes so pale a blue that they seemed to see through whatever she looked at, and at her throat she wore a small old piece of silver, a tower, set with a single clear stone. Lady Alysanne Penrose, Orryn thought. A Hightower, on one side. On the other, Targaryen. He did not know how a woman carried that, and found himself watching to find out.

Beside her horse, a younger man dismounted more slowly. He was a head shorter than Jon and a little narrower, chestnut-haired, with a calm, plain, honest face that looked as though it had been made to be agreed with. He was holding the bridle of his mother's palfrey and, with the other hand, a leather satchel she had passed him without looking. Ser Addam, Orryn thought. The bridegroom. He wondered if Elenei had said a word to him that was not about ribbons.

"Lady Alysanne," Alester said, and came forward with both hands out, and Lady Lynesse beside him bobbed as though on a spring. "You do us honour. You do Grandview honour. You will want rest and wine and a bed. We have the finest room in the castle aired for you, madam, the finest we have: the garden chamber, with the sun in it, my wife has seen to it herself. I'll hear no thanks."

The crowd murmured. Lynesse's smile became something fixed and bright. Orryn, three rows back with his list in his hand, felt every part of his stomach go cold and light, as though he had missed a step on a stair.

"I am obliged, my lord," said Lady Alysanne. "Your letter said as much. I trust the chamber faces south."

"South and into the garden," said Alester. "You'll never want another."

"I do not doubt it."

He presented his household in a long, warm, ragged line: his wife, his brother, Lady Maris, his son; his niece Alyssa, who had been fetched from somewhere with her hair wild and bits of oak leaf in it and who curtseyed with perfect, startling grace; and Orryn, last, with a hand on his shoulder: "Steffon's boy, who sets out on the morrow of the wedding for Grassy Vale and the house of our kinsman Meadows, and in the meantime has taken the whole business of the guests upon himself. He has a better head for beds than I." There was laughter. Lady Alysanne's pale eyes moved to Orryn and rested there for the space of a breath.

"Then he will know where my sons are to sleep," she said.

"Yes, my lady," said Orryn, who did not.

The next hour was all horses. He found he was glad of it. The Penrose steward, whose name was Master Aylmer and who treated Orryn with the elaborate courtesy of a man addressing a pup he was waiting for the owner of, turned out to have a list of his own: twenty-six souls and thirty horses, which was two more of each than the raven had said, and left him two stalls short. Orryn wrote it down. He walked the Penrose grooms to the east stable himself, past the old barn where the hounds had begun, at last, to howl, and at the end of the row found Ser Addam already there, holding a lame roan by the headcollar and running one palm down its off fore.

"He took a stone on the ridge road," he said, not looking up. "He'll mend. If someone could spare a bucket and a clean cloth, I'd be grateful."

"I'll send a boy."

"There's no need. I'll fetch it." He straightened, and gave Orryn a grave, kind, slightly puzzled smile, as if wondering what they were both doing there. "You're the cousin who's off to Grassy Vale. Elenei wrote of you. She said you were clever with figures, and that I should like you."

"Did she?"

"She said it twice, so I took it to be true."

Orryn felt his ears go hot, and could not have said whether he was pleased or ashamed.

It was dusk by the time he reached the inner ward again, and the heat had begun to give up the stones. Someone had lit the torches along the gallery. In the long grey light the Old Lion looked like a thing left out in a field. Penrose maids were hauling boxes up the gallery stair, the boxes bound with straps and smelling faintly of cedar.

He had the key. Meg had pressed it into his hand an hour before at the foot of the gallery stair, without a word, and gone off with her ring clinking. It was a small iron key and had grown warm.

"Nephew," said a voice at his shoulder.

Lady Alys Musgood had come across the ward from the well. She was Alester's sister and Steffon's, a woman in her late thirties with brown hair and quick grey-green eyes and a gown of green silk that was a little more than the day had called for, and she carried it as though the evening had been got up to admire it. She had arrived two days ago, with two maids and a great many opinions. "I hear you have been busy."

"Yes, my lady aunt."

"Alester's been busy too, I gather. He said the sweetest thing to the Penrose woman at the gate. I heard it from the wall." She smiled. "You've a face like your mother's. It tells me everything. Have you been to see the garden chamber?"

"I aired it this morning."

"Meg aired it. I watched her." She said it gently. "I've had that room promised me since the night I came. Alester said so at dinner, before the whole table. I've been in the green room since. It smokes. I haven't mentioned it, because I'm not a woman who complains. But I do wonder what you intend, now."

"I haven't decided," said Orryn, which was true.

"No, of course not. You're a sensible boy. I'm sure you'll do what is right." She touched his cheek with two fingers, lightly, as one would test the heat of a loaf. "Whatever that is. I shall be in the hall."

She went away across the ward, her silk whispering, and Orryn stood with the key in his fist.

There were three ways he could see, and they came to him in a sort of ordered line, like stones set out on a board. He could go to his aunt Lynesse, who would be sorry, and agree with him, and be sorrier than ever by morning. He could go to his father, who would listen, and say very little, and add it to a long account that Orryn had never been shown, and that he suspected would be balanced one day in a way nobody enjoyed. Or he could do it himself, quietly, tonight: move his aunt to the east tower, find something to say to the Penrose steward, and trust that his uncle would not remember what he had said at the gate, which was a thing Orryn had been trusting his uncle not to remember all his life.

He did not like any of them.

The letter was warm against his ribs. He took it out, in the torchlight, because there was nothing else in the world to do with his hands, and read the paragraph about the chamber adjoining the squires' hall. Lodged in the old keep. A solid man's sentence. A man who had said clean and meant it, who knew the price of every nail, and who would, within the week, know Orryn's too.

Somewhere in the great hall a horn sounded for supper, long and sour, the way it always did. Up on the gallery the Penrose maids had stopped with the boxes at the head of the stair, waiting, he realised, for someone to tell them which door.

He was still holding the key.
