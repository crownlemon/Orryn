"I was afraid, my lord," Orryn said.

It came out as a whisper. He made himself say it again, louder, to the narrow window and the dark orchards beyond. "I was afraid of what it would do. To your house, and to mine. And I did not know if it was true. Ser Wystan was in his cups, and I did not think a drunken man's tale should go in a lord's book as if it were fact."

Lord Alaric regarded him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, as a man nods who has found a figure in a column that agrees with his own.

"Fear is honest," he said. "Doubt is better. You are fourteen, and you have learned the second one faster than most men do." He did not say it warmly; it was a measurement, not a kindness. "Sit down, Master Orryn. There is a stool by the desk. You will want to hear this, since it concerns your lord grandfather's name."

Orryn sat. The stool was hard and a little too tall for him, and his boots did not quite touch the floor. His lord grandfather. He had never once thought of Gawen Grandison as a man who had done things, only as a name carved on a tomb in the sept at Grandview, between two others, with a sword laid across the stone. And his lady grandmother beside him, Ellyn of the Meadows, whom he remembered only as a cold white hand with rings on it, and a smell of lavender and old paper.

"Wat, you may go. Leave the book. Fetch Verrall."

Wat laid the little book on the table and went out. He did not look at Orryn as he passed. Orryn thought that a man could carry a great deal and look at nothing.

Lyman Verrall came in a few minutes later with a tray: bread, cold mutton, a jug of small beer, three cups. He set it down with a soft clink, took in the room with a single sweep of his friendly sunburnt face, and poured without being asked. He gave the first cup to Orryn, with a wink so small it might have been a flicker of candlelight.

"Eat, young ser," he murmured. "He thinks better when other people are chewing."

Lord Alaric did not sit. He walked: three paces to the door, three back, his hands clasped behind him, so that the boards creaked at the same two places each time. When he spoke it was not to Orryn, nor quite to Verrall. It was to the room, in the dry level voice of a man going down a column of figures.

"Redgrass Field. The year I was nineteen. You will remember it, Verrall. The Blackfyre pretender raises his banner, and the king calls every lord of the realm to ride against him. In the Stormlands the call goes out from Storm's End, and each lord is told to bring his men to a place and a day." A turn at the door. "The lords of the Marches ride at once. Penrose rides at once; Roland Penrose is at the muster with forty men while Grandview is still reading the summons. He is seen. It is remembered that he was first." Three paces back. "Your lord grandfather rides on the tenth day."

"The tenth," Verrall said gently.

"The tenth. And the field is already won, by the grace of someone else's sword, when he arrives. Nobody says he was a coward. They are too clever for that. They say he was prudent." He said the word as one might pick up a dead rat by the tail. "A careful man. A man who waits to learn which way the wind blows. It costs a man nothing to say it and it costs a house everything to hear it. Eleven years on, they still say it at Parchments, and they said it at the ford, and they will say it at my table if I let them."

Orryn found that the bread had turned to ash in his mouth. He remembered Ser Jon's toast, never in a hurry, and Ser Osmund laughing in the rain with his hand on the rope. He had thought it a small cruelty, a bit of ordinary Penrose insolence. He had not known that it was a thing with a hundred years of weight behind it, or that it came to this: a house made small by a single word.

"Now," said Lord Alaric. "Consider what the old knight has told us. Not whether it is true. Consider it as a sum, and say what it adds to." Pace; turn. "Nine days of waiting, not ten. Nine, and then the road. If that is so, Lord Gawen did not dawdle. He was ready on the ninth day and rode the next morning. Why wait nine days? Not for fear. A man afraid does not write twice to Storm's End. He writes once, and then he stays in his bed."

"He was waiting for an answer," Verrall said.

"He was waiting for an answer. Two letters went west. One came back." A pause. "And the one that came back was burned."

The room was very still. Somewhere below, a door banged, and a woman laughed, and was shushed.

"Let us suppose, as three men might suppose in a counting-room," Alaric said, and held up a finger, "that the answer said ride. Then Lord Gawen disobeyed it, or was prevented, and the nine days are his fault, and a man would burn that letter to save the house from its own shame. That is the first sum."

A second finger. "Suppose it said wait. Hold at Grandview until the call is renewed. Then Lord Gawen did exactly as he was bidden, and the nine days are not prudence at all but obedience, and every man who has called Grandview prudent for eleven years is a fool, and Storm's End is the cause of the shame, and a man who wished to keep the peace between Grandview and its liege might burn that letter, too."

A third. "Suppose it said neither. Suppose it said some third thing, which I cannot guess at, and which no one has ever mentioned, and which a man could not bear to have known. The numbers do not tell me. They tell me only that three answers fit, and that a man who burns a letter must have known which of the three it was."

"And the kept one?" Verrall said.

"The kept one is the one that went out, or so I would think. A copy of the question. A thing that proves he asked." Alaric stopped at the window. "I would give a great deal to know who put his hand to the fire. My lord uncle Alester shouted from the second day that they should ride, if this is to be believed. A man who wished to ride would have burned a letter that said wait. He would have had every reason, and he is a man who acts before he reasons. And Ser Steffon stood at the foot of the stair and counted."

Orryn flinched. He had not meant to. His father's name was a small cold stone laid on his chest.

"What did he count, Master Orryn?" Lord Alaric said, without turning.

"I— do not know, my lord."

"No. Nor do I." The lord turned, and for an instant his face was not composed at all, but naked and fierce and rather tired, the face of a man of thirty who had spent his life being exact in a world that would not hold still. "It is the whole matter, do you see. Not the nine days. Nine days is a small thing; I could forgive a man nine days. It is that there are two stories in this house, and one is a lie, and I do not know which. My aunt wrote four letters and then she was silent. Your grandfather died and said nothing. Your father counts. Your uncle shouts. And a drunken old man in an inn in the Reach knows more of it than any of them will say."

Verrall, quietly, put down his cup. "Shall I write to Grandview, my lord? Ask Lord Alester—"

"Lord Alester would shout." Alaric said it flatly, with no scorn. "He would shout in a letter, and my wife would hear of it, and by harvest-end it would be at Highgarden. No. I will not ask a man who has a reason to lie and a worse reason to be asked." His eyes came to Orryn, and rested. They were hazel, and calm, and very tired. "But there is a boy in my room who has been told that the old man has a second thing to give. I heard it in Wat's reading. For a price. And that he will not give it in front of a groom."

Orryn swallowed. "Yes, my lord."

"You are my squire. You are also your grandfather's blood, and a boy, which is to say a thing no one fears." He spoke quite slowly now, weighing each word, as he weighed everything. "I do not tell you to go. I do not say I shall let you go, or when. I say only this: if the price were one you could pay, and one you would be willing to pay, would you pay it, and would you bring me what you were given? Or would you keep it?"

What does Orryn say?

1. That he would pay what he could, and bring everything to Lord Alaric, and no one else, until Lord Alaric said otherwise.

2. That he would want to write to his father, Ser Steffon, first, since the letter is his family's, and ask what it was that he counted.

3. That he cannot promise: he gave Ser Wystan his word to ask nothing in front of a groom, and he will not trade the old man's trust for a place at Lord Alaric's side.

4. Something else of your own.
