Bitterbridge, 208 AC

The first thing Orryn heard that morning was somebody asking whether the horses had been fed.

He lay still beneath his blanket, listening.

“Twice,” a man answered.

“Twice?”

“The first time they ate it.”

There was a short silence, followed by the sound of a bucket being put down rather harder than necessary.

Orryn opened his eyes.

The little chamber above the gatehouse was already bright. The Caswell page who had shared it with him was sitting on the edge of his bed, pulling on a stocking. He had tied his hair back with a strip of cloth and was making a face at his foot.

“Your lord's awake,” the page said.

“How do you know?”

“He sent for hot water.”

Orryn sat up.

“And my lord's son?”

“Still asleep when I passed.”

That seemed unlikely. Matthos had spent much of the previous evening talking about the knights of Highgarden, and had declared twice that he intended to be ready before any of the household.

Orryn pulled on his breeches and discovered that the seam beneath his left knee had begun to open. He had meant to have it repaired at Grassfield. Marya had packed him a spare pair, but those were in the baggage beneath the stairs, and he did not intend to send a servant searching for them before breakfast.

He put on his tunic and went to the larger chamber.

Lord Alaric stood beside the window in his shirt, shaving with a small knife and a basin of water. Matthos was sitting in bed, eating bread.

“Grandison,” Alaric said, without turning. “Have they saddled the gelding?”

“Not yet, my lord.”

“Then see that they do. And tell Bertram I want the packhorse weighed before we leave.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“Not with a balance. I mean I want him to look at the baggage. They've put the heavy chest on one side again.”

Orryn nodded.

Matthos swallowed.

“Father, will we be at Highgarden tomorrow?”

“We shall be there when the road brings us there.”

“I only wanted to know.”

“Then ask a question I can answer.”

Matthos took another piece of bread.

Orryn collected his lord's boots from beside the bed. There was a pale smear of dried mud along one heel, which he rubbed away with a cloth.

“Leave them,” Alaric said. “I can wear muddy boots.”

“They're nearly clean.”

“Then you may finish.”

Orryn finished.

*

The stable court was crowded with carts.

Some belonged to merchants who had lodged at Bitterbridge overnight. Others bore the centaur of House Caswell. A wagon filled with woollen cloth was being backed toward the gate, its driver shouting at two boys who were attempting to turn a mule in the opposite direction.

The great bridge across the Mander lay beyond the walls. Orryn could not see much of the river from the yard, but he could hear the water beneath the distant clatter of wheels.

He found Ser Bertram beside their packhorse.

The knight was holding two bags, one in each hand, and wearing the expression of a man who had just discovered that neither belonged to him.

“Which is heavier?” Orryn asked.

“Whichever one I'm holding last.”

“Lord Alaric said the chest was wrongly loaded.”

“Lord Alaric has an eye for such things.”

Ser Bertram put down the bags.

“His lordship may inspect it himself if he wishes. The smith has put the spare shoes in one of those sacks, and if we put them anywhere else they'll rattle all the way to Highgarden.”

Orryn helped redistribute the baggage.

They had nearly finished when Joffrey Caswell came into the yard with a lute case beneath one arm and an apple in his hand.

“Are you leaving already?”

“After breakfast.”

“Then you've still time.”

“For what?”

“To eat something worth eating.”

He offered Orryn the apple.

Orryn took it.

“Is this worth eating?”

“I haven't tried it.”

There was a bruise beneath the skin. Orryn turned the fruit in his hand.

“Do you always give away things you don't want?”

“Only to people I like.”

“That seems an odd custom.”

“Bitterbridge has many.”

Joffrey put down his lute case upon a mounting block.

He was wearing the same fine blue doublet he had worn the day before, though someone had apparently persuaded him to have it brushed. There was a small hole near the cuff that Orryn had not noticed before.

“Matthos says he'll have a better horse at Highgarden,” Joffrey said.

“He may.”

“And better armour.”

“He says that too.”

“And four squires to help him.”

“He would be the squire.”

“I told him. He said Moryn Tyrell would make an exception.”

Orryn laughed.

Joffrey seemed pleased, but not surprised.

“What do you think it will be like?” he asked.

“Highgarden?”

“Yes.”

“Large.”

“So Father says. He also says there are more knights than a man can count, which I should think makes the steward's work difficult.”

Orryn had seen the great Caswell hall, the bridge, the busy yards and the stables. He had thought Bitterbridge enormous when they arrived.

It was strange to imagine a place so much greater that the people of Bitterbridge spoke of it with wonder.

“Are you coming with us?” he asked.

“No. Mother says I have lessons.”

“With your master-at-arms?”

“With everyone. It depends who finds me first.”

Joffrey opened his lute case and felt among the pegs.

“My father says I ought to be learning from Ser Cleyton. Mother says Ser Cleyton is an excellent knight to watch and a poor one to imitate. I don't know what that means.”

“Nor do I.”

“I expect I'll learn.”

He closed the case.

“There was another verse to that song last night.”

“The one about the ferryman?”

“Yes.”

“You said it was finished.”

“It was. Then I remembered another.”

Orryn looked toward the gate, where Ser Bertram was now arguing with a groom about a missing girth.

“Play it.”

Joffrey considered the sun.

“Perhaps after breakfast.”

He took up the case again.

“Come along, then. Mother dislikes keeping guests waiting, especially when they're leaving.”

*

Lady Brenna Caswell was already at the breakfast table.

She sat near the open window of a small chamber overlooking the river passage, with two books, several folded letters and a plate of bread beside her. The bread was untouched. A clerk stood nearby, holding a narrow strip of parchment.

Lord Armond occupied the chair at the head of the table.

He had eaten most of his breakfast and was explaining to Ser Cleyton Caswell why a man ought never to purchase a horse without looking at its teeth.

“Unless it is a very good horse,” he added.

“Then why look at them at all?” asked Cleyton.

“That is what I'm telling you.”

Lady Brenna wrote something in the margin of a letter.

Orryn entered behind Lord Alaric, carrying his gloves and belt. Matthos followed, looking rather more awake than he had upstairs.

“Alaric!” Armond called. “Come and eat. We've bread enough for twice your company.”

Alaric bowed to Lady Brenna and greeted his father-in-law.

Orryn took his place behind his lord's chair.

He was accustomed to standing while Alaric ate. At Grassfield he would have brought the water, attended to the cups and carried away the plates. Here the Caswell servants performed most of those duties, and he found himself holding his lord's gloves for lack of anything better.

Brenna noticed.

“Master Grandison, there's a stool by the door. You needn't stand over his shoulder all morning.”

“Thank you, my lady.”

He took the stool.

Joffrey came in shortly afterward and sat beside Matthos. A servant brought both boys eggs and a dish of stewed fruit.

Brenna finished writing.

“Armond, I shall need your seal before noon.”

“For what?”

“The answer to Goldengrove.”

Armond made a dissatisfied sound.

“Again?”

“They have written again.”

“Then let them read the first answer.”

“They objected to it.”

“I remember. I objected to their objection.”

Brenna set down her pen.

“Lord Rowan's steward claims that his master's grain carts are exempt from the western bridge toll by ancient grant.”

“They have been exempt since before my father was born.”

“The Redwyne factors say their carts are charged in full for the same road.”

“Then they ought to ask Lord Rowan how he got his exemption.”

“They have. He told them it was none of their concern.”

Armond laughed.

“That sounds like Rycherd.”

Brenna did not laugh.

“And now the Arbor's men want a copy of the grant, which nobody at Goldengrove appears willing to produce. Garth's officers have asked for our toll books.”

Alaric lowered his bread.

“Garth's officers?”

“His clerks,” Brenna said. “They have been asking for copies of old exemptions throughout the Reach. At least that is what the letters say.”

“By whose command?”

“That is precisely what Lord Rowan wishes to know.”

Armond reached for his cup.

“I've no intention of having my father's tolls decided by men who weren't born when he collected them.”

“Nor have I,” Brenna replied. “But the Redwyne men are paying what the Rowan men are not, and they have discovered the difference.”

“Let the Redwynes pay. They've money enough.”

Orryn looked down at the table.

He had been taught that a toll was owed for the use of a road or bridge. At Sedgeford, Ser Osmund Sedgley had kept a book recording every charge, and Orryn had spent a miserable afternoon learning how the wrong payment could remain in a ledger long after the traveller had gone.

He had never considered that a great lord might pay nothing because one of his ancestors had persuaded another lord to promise it.

Brenna turned one of the letters toward Armond.

“I have not told our collectors to change anything. The disputed amounts are recorded separately, and I've sent for the oldest rolls. But Lord Rowan expects us to defend his privilege, while the Arbor's factors expect us to defend theirs.”

“Then we shall defend our own,” Armond said.

“And what are those?”

“The right to collect my tolls.”

“From whom?”

Armond looked at her.

“From anyone who owes them.”

Ser Cleyton bent over his plate.

Brenna held her husband's gaze for a moment, then turned the letter back toward herself.

“Very well.”

Alaric had been listening closely.

“Rowan is right to object if the grant is his,” he said.

Brenna looked toward him.

“You have not seen it.”

“No. But neither have Garth's clerks, from what you tell me.”

“Nor have I. That is why I'm asking for it.”

Armond pointed a piece of bread toward Alaric.

“There's the difficulty. Every man has a grandfather who promised him something, and nobody can produce the promise.”

“Some promises are older than parchment,” Alaric said.

“Some promises are older than the men who remember them,” Brenna answered. “And others were never made.”

Alaric accepted that without replying.

Armond drank.

“I shall take the matter to Leo when next I see him. He knows Rowan, and he knows Redwyne. He can tell them both to leave my bridge alone.”

Brenna resumed writing.

“You may tell him. I doubt either lord will be satisfied.”

“They needn't be satisfied. Leo is their lord.”

There was a brief silence.

Orryn had heard men speak of Lord Leo Tyrell often enough. The Longthorn was an old and celebrated knight, lord of Highgarden and ruler of the Reach. His sons were famous men in their own right, and his eldest, Garth, would inherit after him.

At Grassfield, Garth's name had become common whenever accounts or new demands were discussed. Moryn's was heard more often among visiting knights.

Armond, however, spoke as though the three sons were still boys running through their father's hall.

Alaric set down his cup.

“With respect, my lord, it may be foolish to wait for Leo to settle everything.”

Armond's eyes narrowed.

“Foolish?”

“He cannot rule forever.”

“No man can.”

“And Garth already governs much of what his father leaves undone. If Rowan waits for Garth to become lord before he concerns himself with Redwyne influence, he'll discover the matter has been settled without him.”

Brenna stopped writing.

“Has Lady Ashford persuaded you of that?”

Alaric turned toward her.

“She persuaded me of nothing I had not begun to see myself.”

“Then she was fortunate.”

It was not clear whether she meant the lady or Alaric.

Armond pushed aside his plate.

“What's this about Ashford?”

“They visited Grassfield,” Alaric said. “Lady Odessa Merryweather brought Wylla and her promised husband, Ser Androw Ashford. His mother came with them.”

“I know the family.”

“I thought you did.”

“Of course I do. Good men at Ashford, though they know how to charge a guest for everything short of the air he breathes.”

Brenna folded her hands upon the table.

“And what did Lady Ashford tell you?”

Alaric hesitated.

Orryn remembered the hunt beneath the willows, the falcon on his fist and Lady Ashford riding beside his lord in her plain dark habit.

He remembered her words about the Redwynes becoming the Reach.

For a moment he wondered whether Alaric would tell them.

“She spoke of Garth's growing dependence upon his wife's family,” Alaric said. “Of Lord Redwyne's ships, his place upon the king's council, and the money he brings to Highgarden. She believes the old landed houses need a counterweight.”

“Rowan,” Brenna said.

“Rowan among others.”

“And Moryn.”

“Yes.”

Brenna leaned back.

“My brother has been making friends since he could walk. He is very good at it.”

“Lady Ashford says he is respected among the lords who fear what Garth's government may become.”

“Some of them fear what Garth's government may discover.”

Alaric's mouth tightened.

“Not every lord who disputes a clerk's demands is hiding money.”

“I know that.”

“Or evading his obligations.”

“I know that too.”

“Then you know why men look to Moryn.”

“I know why they look to him. That is not the same as knowing what he will do for them.”

Armond had been watching the exchange with growing interest.

“Did she ask you to support Moryn?”

“She offered Matthos a place in his household.”

Matthos looked up at the sound of his name.

“She said Ser Moryn had asked for a Meadows boy,” he said.

“Matthos,” Alaric warned.

“I only meant that he asked.”

“She said he would consider you,” Alaric replied. “That is not the same thing.”

Matthos returned to his eggs, though he ate no more.

Brenna regarded Alaric.

“A place for your heir.”

“A squireship.”

“Both.”

“He is a capable knight.”

“I have never questioned Moryn's knighthood.”

“Then what do you question?”

“Whether you understand why Lady Ashford was so eager to offer him your son.”

Alaric's expression changed.

It was a look Orryn knew from Grassfield. He had seen it when a steward questioned a decision too late, or when a knight corrected his lord before servants.

“I understand her interests perfectly well.”

“Then tell me.”

“She is connected to Moryn through his marriage. Her family benefits when he has friends. I know that. House Meadows can benefit too.”

Brenna waited.

“Rowan and Peake have substantial influence among the landed houses,” Alaric continued. “Ashford has its own standing. The Merryweathers are close enough to Grassfield that I cannot afford to ignore them. If those houses become more closely associated with Moryn, I would sooner have some friendship with him myself than find every road to Highgarden passes through another lord's hall.”

Orryn thought of the road to Longtable, which Alyn had described so often that he could almost picture its moat.

Alaric was speaking more quickly now.

“I'm not pledging my banners against Garth. Nor am I asking Moryn to challenge his brother. I am finding a place for my son with a knight whose household may prove of value to us.”

Armond nodded.

“There is sense in that.”

Brenna turned toward her husband.

“There is sense in several things that are still unwise.”

“I have not said it was wise.”

“You were about to.”

Armond laughed.

“I was about to ask for more bread.”

A servant brought the loaf.

Brenna let him cut it before she spoke again.

“Alaric, I know Lady Ashford. She is not a foolish woman, and she is not a generous one when generosity gains her nothing. She came to your hall with a marriage party and left you considering how best to place your heir under my brother.”

“I was considering Matthos's future before she arrived.”

“I do not doubt it. But you are considering Moryn now.”

“Yes. Because I think it is advantageous.”

“Then you have made a choice.”

Alaric's expression hardened again.

“I have made no choice between Garth and Moryn.”

“No. But Moryn will have made a friend, and Lady Ashford will have brought him one. Garth will see whose household has gained your son. So will Rowan. So will my husband.”

Armond looked up.

“I am seeing it now.”

She ignored him.

“You may tell yourself this is only a squireship. I assure you nobody at Highgarden will be so modest in describing it.”

Alaric took a breath.

“What would you have me do? Refuse an honour because another lord might find it useful?”

“I would have you know what you are receiving before you give your heir in return.”

“He will receive instruction.”

“And you?”

“Goodwill.”

“From whom?”

“Moryn.”

“Has Moryn promised it?”

“He has offered to consider my son.”

“That was not what I asked.”

Alaric looked away.

Orryn felt suddenly uncomfortable on his stool. Lady Brenna's voice had not risen, and neither had Alaric's, but there was a quality to the exchange that reminded him of Lady Jeyne questioning a servant who had spent the household's money without permission.

He had not expected to hear his lord questioned in quite that fashion at his father-in-law's table.

Armond wiped his mouth.

“I'll tell you what I think.”

Brenna looked toward him.

“I think Alaric should go to Highgarden, present his boy and let Moryn decide whether he wants him. That's what he set out to do.”

“That is what he intends,” Brenna said.

“And I think he should remember that Moryn is not Lord of Highgarden.”

“I have not forgotten Lord Leo,” Alaric said.

“Good. I have served Leo since before his sons were old enough to wear swords, and I've never found it necessary to choose one against the others.”

“That has served you well,” Alaric said.

“It has.”

“But it will not serve everyone forever.”

Armond regarded him.

Alaric continued more quietly.

“You have a great marriage, an important castle and the friendship of the ruling lord. You can afford to wait. I cannot.”

Armond's expression became thoughtful.

“Perhaps not.”

“Garth will inherit. I do not dispute it. But when he does, the Redwynes will be closer to the lord's chair than they have ever been. Rowan understands that. It would be naive to think his only answer is to wait respectfully for Lord Leo to die.”

“You think me naive?”

“I think you can afford to be patient in a way that other lords cannot.”

It was not quite an answer.

Armond sat back.

“I have been called worse by men who had fewer reasons than you.”

Brenna picked up her pen again, though she did not write.

“Father would dislike hearing you speak of his death as though it were an appointment to be entered in a book,” she said.

“I wish him many years,” Alaric answered.

“So do I.”

Orryn could not tell whether Brenna was satisfied. Her expression had returned to the composed look she wore when receiving visitors.

“I expect Garth will inherit,” she said. “And I expect he will govern well in many things. He has more patience for accounts than Father ever had.”

“Then you favour him,” Alaric said.

“He is my eldest brother.”

“That was not what I asked.”

For the first time that morning, Brenna smiled without appearing to intend it.

“No,” she said. “It was not.”

Armond chuckled.

“Now you know what it is to argue with her.”

Brenna ignored him.

“Garth knows how much the Reach owes, and how much it spends. I wish more lords did. But I do not care to see every question settled according to what pleases the Arbor.”

“Then you agree with Rowan.”

“On that? Sometimes. Rowan has lands, men and rights older than any marriage my brother might make. He will not thank the Redwynes for treating him as an obstacle to be counted around. Nor should he.”

Alaric studied her.

“And Moryn?”

“Moryn knows how to make such men feel that Highgarden remembers what they are owed.”

“And does he mean to honour those debts?”

Brenna glanced at him.

“That is a question you might usefully ask when you meet him.”

Armond began laughing again.

Matthos looked from one adult to another.

“Will Ser Moryn still take me?”

Everyone turned toward him.

Alaric was the first to answer.

“If he thinks you suitable.”

“But Lady Ashford said—”

“Lady Ashford cannot make you his squire. Nor can I. Ser Moryn will decide.”

Matthos looked down.

Orryn had expected him to argue. Instead he pushed a piece of stewed pear around his plate and said nothing more.

*

The servants began clearing the table.

Ser Cleyton excused himself to inspect the horses. Armond sent for a groom and changed his mind twice about which mount should be brought for the ride to the gate.

Joffrey slipped away with a piece of bread.

Orryn rose to collect Alaric's gloves.

“Bring those here,” his lord said.

Orryn crossed the room.

Alaric took the gloves but kept him waiting.

“There is one more matter,” he said to Brenna.

She had begun gathering her letters.

“Yes?”

“Elyce.”

“Your younger daughter.”

“She's twelve.”

“I remember her age.”

Alaric glanced toward Matthos, who was finishing his breakfast with the stubborn slowness of someone determined not to attract any further notice.

“I had considered Ser Alyn Merryweather for her.”

Orryn felt his fingers tighten around the empty belt he was holding.

Alyn.

It was the first time he had heard his name spoken that morning.

He thought of the orchard wicket at Grassfield, standing unlatched beneath the branches. Of Leonette taking hold of Alyn's doublet. Of Lady Jeyne appearing among the rosemary.

Brenna seemed unsurprised.

“Lady Odessa's younger son?”

“Yes.”

“I heard there had been some discussion.”

“There was no agreement.”

“I know.”

“And there will be none. Alyn is no longer under consideration for Elyce.”

Brenna looked at Alaric for several moments.

“Has something happened?”

“He has left my service. I knighted him before we departed.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I mean to give.”

Armond glanced up from his plate.

“Not a bad lad, the Merryweather boy. Handsome enough. Good family.”

“He is not suitable for Elyce,” Alaric said.

There was something in his voice that prevented Armond from saying more.

Orryn kept his eyes upon the belt.

Lady Brenna folded the last letter.

“Very well. What would you have of me?”

“Assistance.”

“With Elyce?”

“Yes.”

Brenna looked at him.

“Jeyne knows you are asking me?”

“I have not yet spoken to her.”

“That was what I thought.”

“I intend to.”

“Then I should dearly like to hear how you explain it.”

Alaric looked impatient.

“Brenna, I came to ask whether you know of suitable young men whose families might consider a match. Nothing more.”

“Nothing more?”

“Nothing more.”

She set the letters beside her chair.

“Lady Jeyne would hate to call upon my help.”

Alaric did not answer.

“She would rather approach a stranger and be refused than owe a favour to me. You know that.”

“Jeyne wishes Elyce to marry well.”

“So do I.”

“Then why should there be difficulty?”

“Because she is Jeyne.”

Brenna spoke without raising her voice, and yet the words sounded less kindly than anything she had said to Alaric about Moryn.

Orryn had heard Lady Jeyne speak of her stepmother at Grassfield. There had been little affection in it. He had known that Brenna was younger than most women who married into a household with grown children. He had not understood why that should matter so greatly.

Now he thought he understood a little more.

Brenna belonged to Highgarden by birth and ruled much of Bitterbridge's household in practice. Jeyne had been born at Bitterbridge, but its accounts, servants and guests were now ordered by another woman.

Alaric put his gloves upon the table.

“I am asking for help with my daughter. You need not make it a quarrel between yourself and her mother.”

Brenna considered him.

“No,” she said. “I need not.”

For a moment Orryn thought she would refuse.

Then she added, “I will help.”

Alaric inclined his head.

“You have my thanks.”

“Don't thank me yet. I may find nobody suitable.”

“Any inquiry would be welcome.”

“I have acquaintances of my own at Highgarden, Alaric. Women I have known since we were girls, wives whose sons are coming of age, men who know which houses have daughters to provide for and which need to marry their younger sons. I can write.”

“I had thought perhaps Lady Arlanna—”

“My mother?”

“Yes.”

Brenna's expression changed.

“You will see my mother yourself when you reach Highgarden, if she grants you an audience. You do not require me to speak to her, and I am not going there.”

“Of course.”

“I shall make inquiries among people I know. There are advantages to having friends who are not one's brothers.”

Armond gave a satisfied grunt.

“She's right about that.”

Brenna continued.

“I will not promise Elyce to anyone, and I will not have letters sent in Jeyne's name without her knowledge. You may tell your wife that I am willing to make introductions and that she may decide whether to pursue them.”

“That is fair.”

“It is more than fair.”

Alaric said nothing.

Brenna looked at him with a faintly impatient expression.

“And tell her yourself. I do not want to learn from another letter that I have insulted her by doing what her husband asked.”

Armond shook his head.

“Jeyne has always been a proud girl.”

“She has reason to be,” Brenna replied.

Orryn looked up.

It was not the answer he had expected.

Brenna was watching her husband rather than Alaric.

“She is your daughter, and Lady of Grassfield, and she has children whose futures matter. She ought to be consulted before men arrange them.”

Alaric's face went still.

“I have not arranged anything.”

“Then you should have no difficulty telling her so.”

Armond pushed back his chair.

“Seven save us. I thought the quarrel was over.”

“It is,” Brenna said.

She rose.

“I shall have the letters prepared this afternoon. Alaric, if you return by way of Bitterbridge, you may hear what I have learned. Otherwise I will write to Grassfield.”

“Thank you.”

“And when you reach Highgarden, do not ask Moryn for three favours at once. He will promise you four and remember two.”

Alaric smiled despite himself.

“I shall remember.”

“See that you do.”

*

They found Matthos in the yard with Joffrey.

The two boys had discovered a Caswell stable lad willing to let them examine a horse that had recently arrived from the western estates. Matthos was inspecting its teeth in imitation of Lord Armond.

“Too old,” he declared.

The stable lad looked offended.

“He's six, my lord.”

“His teeth say eight.”

“His teeth say he's hungry,” Joffrey replied.

Matthos shut the horse's mouth.

“My father says a knight should know horses.”

“Mine says that too.”

“Then you ought to learn.”

Joffrey gave Orryn a long look.

Orryn resisted the temptation to laugh.

Ser Bertram was waiting beside the packhorse, whose bags had now been properly balanced. The knight had tied a second strap across the chest to prevent it swinging against the animal's ribs.

Alaric inspected the arrangement.

“Better.”

Ser Bertram bowed.

“I shall tell the bags you approve.”

“They may tell you when they fall off.”

“They shall not fall off.”

“Good.”

Matthos left the horse and came over.

“Father, how soon do we ride?”

“When everyone is ready.”

“I have been ready since dawn.”

Orryn glanced at Joffrey.

Joffrey was looking at the sky with great interest.

Alaric mounted.

“Then you may wait a little longer.”

Lord Armond appeared beneath the arch with Brenna beside him. A number of servants followed carrying the last of the travelling things.

Armond insisted upon inspecting the horses, though Ser Bertram assured him that they had already been examined.

“Never trust a horse that another man has looked at,” Armond said.

“Nor one that you have,” Brenna replied. “You buy them for their colour.”

“I have bought fine horses.”

“You have bought expensive ones.”

“There's often no difference.”

She left him to it.

Orryn had finished fastening his own bags when Joffrey came to stand beside the chestnut gelding.

“Will you write?” Joffrey asked.

“I don't know.”

“That is a poor answer.”

“Will you?”

“Perhaps.”

“That is worse.”

Joffrey smiled.

“You might tell me whether Highgarden is as grand as everyone says.”

“I can do that.”

“And whether the knights are as splendid.”

“Matthos will tell you.”

“I want to know what you think.”

Orryn looked toward Matthos, who was trying to persuade Ser Bertram to let him ride ahead of the party.

“I'll write if I can.”

“Good.”

Joffrey held out his hand.

Orryn took it. Joffrey's fingers were marked with small hard places from the lute strings.

“Safe roads, Orryn.”

“You too.”

“I am not going anywhere.”

“Then you should be quite safe.”

Joffrey looked pleased.

“That is the wisest thing you've said since you came.”

He stepped away as Orryn mounted.

At the gate, Lady Brenna was speaking quietly with Alaric. Orryn could not hear the words, and he made no attempt to.

Armond embraced his son-in-law with enough force to make Alaric's horse shift sideways.

“Give my regards to Leo,” he said. “And don't let Moryn fill the boy's head with too many songs of old battles.”

“I shall try.”

“If Garth asks after me, tell him I have received his clerk's letter and shall answer it when his father has spoken.”

Brenna turned her head.

“Armond.”

“What?”

“You will answer it before then.”

“Will I?”

“Yes.”

Armond sighed.

“I suppose I will.”

Alaric bowed to them both.

Matthos called his farewell with great enthusiasm. Joffrey waved from beside the mounting block.

The portcullis stood raised, and beyond it the road was already crowded with travellers.

Orryn followed his lord out of Bitterbridge.

*

The river lay broad and brown beneath the bridge.

From the saddle, Orryn could see the water curling around the stone piers and the long shadows of the arches beneath them. Two barges were passing downstream, one loaded with timber and the other with sacks beneath a patched canvas covering.

Men hauled at ropes along the bank. A woman stood on the nearer barge holding a small child against her hip.

Orryn watched until the bridge parapet hid them.

Beyond the crossing, the road rose between fields of cut wheat. There were orchards farther west, their trees close together behind low stone walls, and pastures where cattle stood beneath the little shade that remained.

The sun had already begun to burn through his tunic.

For a time nobody spoke.

Alaric rode at the head with Ser Bertram, and Matthos kept close beside them. Orryn followed with the packhorse between himself and the edge of the road.

At the first halt, Matthos came back to find him.

“Did you hear what Lady Brenna said?”

“About what?”

“About me.”

“She said a great many things.”

“She thinks Father ought not to send me to Ser Moryn.”

Orryn loosened the chestnut's girth a notch.

“I don't think she said that.”

“She said it was dangerous.”

“She said men would notice.”

“That's the same thing.”

Orryn looked at him.

“Is it?”

Matthos had no immediate answer.

He took a drink from his water skin and offered it to Orryn, who accepted gratefully.

“Father knows what he's doing,” Matthos said.

“Yes.”

“He would not send me if it were foolish.”

Orryn thought of Alaric sitting across from Brenna, his face hardening whenever she questioned him. He thought of the previous evening, when Lord Armond had spoken of threatening the young lord of Grassfield into serving King Daeron.

There had been a time, not so long ago, when Alaric had considered following a different dragon.

He wondered whether Alaric had believed himself foolish then.

“Ser Moryn may not take you,” Orryn said.

Matthos scowled.

“He will.”

“You haven't met him.”

“Lady Ashford has.”

“She isn't you.”

Matthos handed him back the water skin.

“You're always finding something wrong.”

Orryn wanted to say that Matthos had asked.

Instead he gave the skin another drink, fastened the stopper and handed it back.

Matthos looked toward the road, where Alaric was calling for the horses to be brought together.

“I'll be glad when we get there,” he said.

“So will I.”

“You'll only stay a little while.”

“Yes.”

Matthos looked almost pleased by that.

“Then you'll see what sort of place I've gone to.”

Orryn nodded.

He had spent the last year living in another family's household, away from Grandview and his parents. He knew the first strangeness of unfamiliar beds, unfamiliar servants and men who expected a boy to know rules nobody had thought to explain.

Matthos, who had never lived anywhere but Grassfield, seemed to imagine Highgarden would be a larger version of his father's hall.

Perhaps it would be, Orryn thought.

Only with more people to tell him where to stand.

*

The road was busy throughout the afternoon.

They overtook two merchants travelling with wool and were passed by a party of mounted men wearing no house colours Orryn recognised. At a village beside a stream they stopped to water the horses and purchase bread.

Orryn helped Ser Bertram check the packhorse's shoes.

The left hind shoe had begun to loosen.

“Another day's riding like this and he'll cast it,” Ser Bertram said.

“We have spares.”

“We have spares for three horses and a smith's work for six. See if the village has a farrier.”

Orryn went.

He found a smith beneath a lean-to at the far end of the village, repairing a cartwheel while his apprentice worked a pair of bellows.

The man agreed to look at the horse, but wanted payment for leaving his wheel unfinished.

Orryn went back to Ser Bertram.

“How much?”

“Three pennies.”

“For looking?”

“For leaving his work.”

“Two,” Ser Bertram said. “And let him finish the wheel first. We are stopping to water in any case.”

Orryn returned to the smith.

The man laughed at the offer.

“Two pennies and his lordship can stand here while I work.”

“He will not stand here.”

“Then his squire may.”

Orryn stayed.

The smith finished the wheel, cleaned his hands and examined the shoe. It needed two nails and a little hammering. Orryn held the horse's head while he worked.

When the smith finished, he charged another penny for the nails.

Orryn paid it from the money Ser Bertram had given him and was displeased to discover that the knight had expected exactly that.

“Three pennies,” Ser Bertram said when Orryn returned. “A fair price.”

“You said two.”

“For leaving the wheel.”

“You knew he'd charge for the nails?”

“I hoped he wouldn't.”

Orryn stared at him.

Ser Bertram mounted.

“Come along, Grandison. We shall be here until winter if you question every penny.”

Orryn remounted the chestnut and followed.

He had spent the morning listening to lords argue about ancient rights, great marriages and the balance of power in the Reach. All of it had seemed terribly important.

Yet the smith had wanted three pennies, and had obtained them without knowing a single thing about Garth Tyrell or Lord Rowan.

Orryn wondered whether Lady Brenna would have made him pay two.

He suspected she might have persuaded the smith to pay her.

*

They lodged that evening at a village inn large enough to accommodate Lord Alaric but not his whole party.

The innkeeper provided a chamber for the lord, a second for Matthos and Ser Bertram, and a loft above the stables for Orryn and the two grooms. There was fresh straw beneath the old, a smell of horses rising through the boards, and a window so small that Orryn could scarcely put his head through it.

He had slept in worse places.

At supper Alaric was in better spirits. He praised the horses, complained about the wine and asked Ser Bertram whether the road farther west was likely to be as crowded.

“Crowded enough,” the knight said. “Everyone has business at Highgarden when Lord Leo keeps court.”

“Everyone has business at Highgarden when he doesn't.”

“Then perhaps we should have stayed at Grassfield.”

Alaric laughed.

Matthos spoke at length about the equipment a squire might require in Moryn's household.

His father told him to wait until he had been accepted before purchasing any of it.

Orryn ate his supper quietly. It was a stew of mutton, onions and beans, with bread so coarse that he found little pieces of husk between his teeth. He ate two bowls.

Later, when Alaric sent him to fetch the saddle bags from the stable, he found Matthos standing in the yard.

The boy was looking west.

“Can you see it?” he asked.

“Highgarden?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Perhaps from the hill.”

“It is too far.”

Matthos considered that.

“Tomorrow, then.”

“Perhaps.”

He turned away.

Orryn collected the bags and took them upstairs.

Before he went to his own bed, he laid out Alaric's clean shirt and put the travelling papers beside the candle. His lord had asked him twice that afternoon whether they had been packed, and Orryn had assured him twice that they had.

The papers contained introductions, accounts, letters of business and a copy of the proposal concerning Matthos's service.

Orryn had helped fold them at Grassfield.

He knew which seal belonged to which letter, though he did not know everything the letters said.

He thought of Lady Ashford riding beside Alaric beneath the hot sky, offering a place in her kinsman's household. Of Brenna at Bitterbridge, asking what Alaric expected in return. Of Lord Armond trusting that Leo Tyrell would keep his sons and his bannermen from becoming too powerful.

The letters lay neatly in their leather case.

In the morning he would carry them into Highgarden.

He wondered whether Moryn Tyrell would read them himself, or whether some clerk would take them away and return with an answer.

He wondered whether the answer would be yes.

When he went to the loft, the grooms were already asleep. The stable beneath them was quiet except for the occasional stamping of a hoof.

Orryn lay on his back, listening to the unfamiliar sounds.

Matthos had spent the day looking toward Highgarden as though the place were already his.

Orryn had tried to do the same, but whenever he imagined its great halls and famous knights, another face intruded: Alyn's, beneath the unruly chestnut hair, grinning at something foolish.

He wondered where Alyn was sleeping that night.

Longtable, perhaps. Or some road between his father's house and Highgarden.

Nobody had said.

Orryn rolled onto his side.

Tomorrow they would ride farther west. There would be more roads, more horses, more inns and more things for which someone expected payment.

He hoped he would not have to bargain with another smith.

Then he drew the blanket over his shoulder and slept.
