Highgarden, 208 AC

The stocking had begun to smell of Orryn's bag.

He had carried it through two meals, an afternoon in the practice yard and a visit to Lord Leo's stables, where a colt had stood upon his foot. Now he had taken it out for the third time and was holding it beneath the supper table while Matthos pretended not to see him.

"Take it," Orryn whispered.

"What?"

"Your stocking."

Matthos looked down. "Where did you get that?"

"Behind the chest. Your things were gone."

"I thought they'd taken everything."

"They hadn't."

Matthos snatched it from him and stuffed it beneath his belt.

Across the table, an older boy with a narrow face watched them over the rim of his cup.

"Has he brought you a stocking, Meadows?"

"No."

"I should have thought it one. You've put something into your breeches."

"It's mine."

"An excellent reason to put it there."

The older boy was Will, the squire who had shown Matthos the tackrooms that morning. He had changed from his riding clothes into a green wool doublet, though the sleeves were rather shiny at the elbows. His dark hair lay flat against his head, as if he had wet it and combed it in some haste.

Matthos coloured.

Orryn said, "He left it in our chamber."

"Then your old chamber has lost something of value." Will raised his cup. "You're doing well, Meadows. One day at Highgarden and you've acquired a servant who follows you about with your clothing."

"He doesn't follow me about."

"Then why is he here?"

"Because his lord is here," Orryn said.

Will gave him a second look.

"That's so, is it? Well, Lord Meadows shall be pleased to have you back. I've got this one now, and he's already asked whether he may choose his own horse."

"I asked which horse I was to ride," Matthos said.

"That's what I told you. It was the way you asked that made it sound like a choice."

Orryn smiled into his cup. Matthos kicked him beneath the table, catching the foot that the colt had trodden on.

He nearly cried out.

"Seven hells, Matthos."

"What?"

"My foot."

Matthos looked guilty for an instant, then annoyed. "I didn't know."

"Neither did the horse."

Will laughed. "What's happened to your foot?"

"Lord Leo's colt stood on it."

"Did you ask him to move?"

"Not quickly enough."

"You'll do well here," Will said. "We lose a boy to a horse every other day."

They were seated at the lower end of Ser Moryn Tyrell's household table, in a lesser hall off Highgarden's principal court. Orryn had come because Alaric had been invited to dine with Moryn and several visiting lords, and his own place had been found among the younger attendants.

There were more servants in this lesser hall than attended the whole of Grassfield's great hall on an ordinary evening. They brought roast mutton with onions, pigeon pies and a dish of small river fish that had been fried until the bones could be eaten. Orryn had taken three of the fish before discovering how sharp they were.

At the upper end, Ser Moryn was speaking with Lord Rycherd Rowan of Goldengrove. Alaric sat beside them, eating steadily. He had washed and changed since the stables, but Orryn could still see a streak of pale dust near the cuff of his sleeve.

Lady Melessa Tyrell occupied the seat at Moryn's right hand. When a servant leaned between her and Alaric to refill a cup, she moved the wine jug aside and said something that made the servant retreat and approach from the other direction.

Orryn thought of Lady Arlanna, whom Alaric was supposed to visit on the morrow. The women of Highgarden had a way of correcting things without raising their voices.

"Will," Matthos said, "are we training again tomorrow?"

"Every morning."

"Even after a feast?"

"Especially after a feast."

"This isn't a feast."

Will looked at the remains of three pigeons, a platter of mutton and two bowls of stewed pears.

"Then I should hate to see what they feed you at Grassfield."

"We eat well enough."

"I'm sure you do. But I heard Ser Luthor say you were to be put with the younger riders until you've learned your hands."

"He said I was to ride with them tomorrow."

"Yes. That's rather what I meant."

Matthos set down his knife. Orryn knew the look gathering in his face.

"Ser Luthor told me I had a good seat."

"He told you that you were heavy with your hands. Both may be true."

Before Matthos could answer, a servant came to say that Ser Moryn wished to see Will.

The older boy rose at once.

"You'll find the south gallery after supper," he told them. "Follow the music. If you find the sept, you've gone too far."

"Are we invited?" Matthos asked.

"You're in the household now. Try not to look surprised by it."

He went up toward the high table.

Matthos watched him.

"I don't like him."

"You've only known him a day."

"That's been long enough."

Orryn took another fish.

"He's all right."

"He thinks he's better than everyone."

"He knows more about Highgarden than we do."

"That isn't the same thing."

Orryn could hardly disagree. He had heard Matthos make the same complaint about older squires at Grassfield, often shortly before trying to become one of them.

At the high table, Lord Rowan said something that caused Alaric to lay down his knife and laugh. It was an unfamiliar sound to hear from his master at supper, not because Alaric seldom laughed, but because he ordinarily had too many things to attend to while eating.

Tonight there was no steward from Grassfield waiting with an account, no servant asking whether a broken latch could be repaired, no miller with a complaint about water.

Alaric lifted his cup.

Orryn finished his fish.

*

The gallery was full of music before they reached it.

A fiddler had taken his place on a small platform beside the windows, with a pipe-player and a boy carrying a drum. The pipe was too shrill for the room. It carried above every conversation and made the laughter seem louder.

The south gallery had long windows overlooking a terrace where pale roses climbed the stonework. The shutters stood open to the night. A wind came through them, cool enough to stir the flames of the candles and bring the smell of damp earth from the gardens below.

Highgarden had fountains even in a dry summer. Orryn could hear water falling somewhere beyond the terrace, though it was too dark to see where.

Servants moved between the guests with wine, bread and bowls of sugared almonds. A table had been cleared for dice near one window, and two men were arguing over whether a third had thrown before his wager was properly laid down.

Ser Moryn stood among a gathering of lords and ladies. His younger brother Olyvar had joined them, wearing a green coat with silver fastening at the throat. Lord Rowan was there, and Lord Gormon Peake of Starpike, who had the broad shoulders and handsome face of a man accustomed to being watched.

Alaric was speaking to a woman Orryn did not know.

She wore a dark blue gown with narrow sleeves and a girdle of worked silver. Her hair was brown, bound at the back of her head with a ribbon, and when she laughed she bent toward Alaric as though he had told her something she wished to hear again.

Orryn had never seen her at Grassfield.

He began toward his master, but Matthos caught his sleeve.

"Come on. Will said to find him."

"Where?"

"Over there."

At the far end of the gallery a company of young men had taken possession of a cushioned bench and two chairs. One sat on the arm of a chair rather than its seat, and another stood beside the window with a silver cup in either hand.

Will was among them.

The boy upon the chair arm had pale brown hair and a handsome, rather gentle face. He wore a green silk doublet with a golden rose worked upon the breast, and a chain at his throat that looked heavier than anything Orryn owned.

Orryn recognised the rose. He had been told enough about Lord Leo's family to understand who the young man must be before Will spoke.

"Ser Harlen," Will said, "I've brought our new boy."

The young knight rose so quickly that the boy seated beside him had to catch the cup he had left on the chair.

"You're Matthos Meadows?"

"Yes, ser."

"Good. My uncle said you were coming. Welcome."

He held out his hand.

Matthos stared at it for a moment before taking it.

Ser Harlen Tyrell was eighteen, Garth Tyrell's eldest son and the heir after him to Highgarden. Orryn had heard Lord Leo speak of his grandson in the stables that afternoon. The old lord had called him soft, though he had said it with affection.

Harlen looked rather different from his grandfather. He was slighter, with dark hair inherited, perhaps, from his Redwyne mother. His hands were clean and his nails neatly kept. He had the bearing of a young knight, but when Matthos bowed too deeply he laughed in embarrassment and begged him not to make such a ceremony of it.

"And your friend?" he asked.

"Orryn Grandison," Matthos said. "He serves my father."

"Of Grandview?"

"Yes, ser."

"I've heard the name." Harlen smiled. "You must both have some wine."

He looked around for a servant.

The boy who had caught his cup said, "We have wine."

"Then give them some, Hugh."

"I've only just filled these."

"Well, one of them."

Hugh sighed and surrendered a cup to Matthos.

He was younger than Harlen, perhaps fifteen, with bright, restless eyes and a costly doublet of deep purple cloth. Clusters of grapes were embroidered at the collar in silver thread.

"That's Hugh Redwyne," Will told Orryn. "Ser Arron's son. Harlen's cousin."

"The relation was plain enough from the wine," said a boy sprawled upon the bench.

He was sixteen or thereabouts, sandy-haired and fine-boned, with an orange-and-black sleeve slashed to show white linen beneath. His boots were polished, but the silver buckle on one was missing a pin.

He smiled at Orryn with the easy assurance of someone who expected his remarks to be enjoyed.

"Gyles Peake," he said. "You may bow if Meadows has left you any room."

Matthos stiffened.

Orryn bowed once, not very deeply.

Gyles appeared satisfied.

"Another stormlander," he observed.

"Half a Reachman, by my grandmother," Orryn answered.

"That is a dangerous condition. You'll never know which side to blame when it rains."

"Nobody is likely to blame either side for rain this summer," said Harlen.

"Your grandfather would give half the Arbor for it," Hugh said.

"He cannot give half the Arbor," Harlen answered. "It belongs to your grandfather."

"He could ask him."

Harlen smiled. "He could."

A knight leaning against the window laughed into his cup.

"Better ask the Arbor for a fleet to carry the water here."

He was older than the others, perhaps twenty, with thick brown hair cut short above the ears and a long, strongly made body. A sword hung at his hip, and the green of his coat was darker than the Tyrell silk. A red huntsman had been stitched upon his breast, bending his bow.

"Ser Denys Tarly," Will said. "From Horn Hill."

"Grandson to Lord Roderick," Denys added. "Who would tell you that the Arbor's ships are less use than a good well, and charge you for the water besides."

"Your grandfather would charge a man for breathing if it kept him fit for war," Gyles said.

"He'd rather you learned to breathe before making speeches."

Gyles grinned.

Their words seemed friendly enough. Yet when the laughter died, neither looked away from the other.

Orryn knew the Tarlys held Horn Hill in the southern Reach and had long guarded the approaches to the Dornish Marches. Lord Roderick had been given command there after the Blackfyre Rebellion, when the Peakes were stripped of the office and two of their castles.

Gyles's father had fought for Daemon Blackfyre. He had kept Starpike, but not the rest.

Orryn did not know how much of that the two boys remembered for themselves. He had been very small during the war and remembered almost nothing of it.

"Sit down," Harlen said. "There's room."

"There isn't," Hugh replied.

"Then we'll make some."

Harlen moved to the edge of his chair and squeezed himself against the arm.

Gyles made no effort to shift along the bench. Will pulled a stool from beside the wall, and Matthos sat upon it.

Orryn remained standing until Harlen noticed.

"Here," the young knight said. "Take my place."

"I can stand, ser."

"There's no need."

"I don't mind."

"Nor do I."

Harlen rose again.

Hugh made an impatient sound.

"Oh, sit, Harlen. You'll have us all standing before the night is out."

Harlen sat down, looking relieved.

Will dragged over another stool.

"There," he said to Orryn. "A great dispute settled. We might yet govern the Reach."

Orryn sat, grateful to be off his injured foot.

The cup they handed him was filled almost to the brim.

He sniffed it.

Arbor wine.

He had tasted it before at Grassfield, when Alyn Merryweather smuggled a flask into the squires' chamber and declared that no good memory began with water. This was sweeter and stronger, with a taste that lingered at the back of his throat.

He drank a little.

Hugh watched.

"Is that all?"

"I've only just got it."

"You'll be here until morning at that rate."

"Leave him be," Harlen said.

Hugh laughed. "I'm not stopping him."

"Then don't hurry him."

For an instant Orryn thought Harlen might say more. Instead the young knight reached for the jug and asked whether anyone wanted another cup.

They all did.

*

The game began with Will's last three copper stars.

He set them upon the table and challenged Hugh to match them. Hugh had no copper, only silver, which he displayed with unnecessary care. Gyles offered to change a stag for him, but wanted two coppers for the trouble.

"You are a robber," Hugh declared.

"My father says the same of yours."

"My father's a seneschal."

"And mine has fewer castles. There must be some reason."

Denys laughed. "You'd sell one if you had it."

"I'd sell the stones and keep the name."

"Then you'd be a Peake indeed."

Gyles threw a sugared almond at him. It struck the window frame and fell into the darkness.

Will collected the money and shook the dice in a wooden cup.

Orryn watched the first throws without wagering. The rules were simple enough, though Gyles kept proposing additions whenever a throw went against him.

Matthos put down two copper stars.

"Where did you get those?" Orryn asked.

"My own purse."

"Your mother gave you that purse."

"That doesn't mean the money isn't mine."

Will tipped the dice.

Matthos won.

He grinned and reached for the coins, but Gyles put his hand over them.

"A new boy's luck. He must throw again."

"Why?"

"Because I say so."

"That's no rule."

"It has always been a rule at Highgarden."

"Not since I've been here," Harlen said.

"Then you weren't listening, cousin."

"I don't think I ever heard it."

"Precisely."

Will took Gyles's hand off the money.

"He won. Let him have it."

Matthos gathered his coppers with an expression Orryn knew well. He would be intolerable now.

"Perhaps I ought to teach you all to play."

"Perhaps you ought," Gyles said pleasantly. "We'll learn to lose our money to a boy whose father still pays for his boots."

Matthos looked up.

"My father is Lord Meadows."

"Is he? I'd taken him for the man who sent your stocking."

Will laughed, but Harlen looked confused.

"What stocking?"

"Nothing," Matthos said.

"His first gift from home," Will explained.

"Then I hope it keeps him warm."

Harlen said it with such evident sincerity that even Matthos laughed.

For a time the game went well enough. They wagered pennies and coppers, drank and argued over the dice. Denys told a story about a knight who had mistaken a baggage mule for his own horse during a night march. The horse had been found in the camp's kitchens, eating turnips, while the mule had carried the knight nearly half a mile before he noticed that something was wrong.

"Was the man drunk?" Matthos asked.

"No."

"Then how could he fail to see?"

"It was dark."

"You'd know the difference by the saddle."

"He hadn't saddled either of them."

"Then he was a fool."

"He was," Denys agreed. "He's a landed knight now."

Gyles wanted to know whether that made him fit to serve under Lord Roderick, and Denys answered that it made him fit to inherit a castle. The distinction delighted Will so much that he asked to hear the mule story again.

Orryn laughed until his foot hurt from knocking against the stool.

He had missed this. At Grassfield there had always been someone speaking over supper, some quarrel about a horse or a sword belt, Alyn making a game of everything and Perkin determined not to lose.

The boys at Highgarden were not his companions. He knew that. Yet for a while they seemed not so very different from the boys he had left behind.

Only the money was greater.

When Harlen lost three silver stags to Hugh, he laughed and paid without complaint.

Hugh won again.

"You've a fortunate hand tonight," Will said.

"It's always fortunate."

"Not with a practice sword," Denys observed.

Hugh made an obscene gesture at him.

Harlen lost another stag.

"Perhaps I should stop," he said.

"No," Hugh protested. "You're winning it back."

"Am I?"

"You will."

"I don't see how."

"Then try."

Harlen glanced at Will, who was collecting the dice.

"One more," Will said.

Harlen nodded.

It became two more, and then three.

Orryn was watching the money when he noticed that Harlen had ceased reaching for the dice of his own accord. Will simply passed him the cup, and Harlen threw when the others told him.

He still smiled whenever somebody spoke to him.

At last he lost a little heap of silver to Gyles.

"That is enough," he said.

"One more throw," Gyles answered.

"I've had enough."

"You've hardly lost anything."

Harlen looked at the coins.

"I know. But I think I'll leave it there."

Gyles lifted the cup and placed it before him.

"Then make it a last throw. For luck."

Harlen did not reach for it.

"Come, cousin. I have never known you refuse a last throw."

"You've known him refuse a horse," Denys said.

"Only when it was mine."

"Your horse kicked him."

"One kick. Harlen took it very ill."

Harlen laughed. "He broke two of my ribs."

"See how well you recovered?"

The others laughed. Harlen took the dice.

Orryn watched him shake them.

He remembered Lord Leo in the stables, speaking of a grandson who left a room rather than win an argument.

Harlen threw, lost and paid.

"Now we're finished," he said.

Gyles swept up the money.

"An excellent last throw."

Harlen smiled again, but Orryn thought he looked tired.

From the centre of the gallery came a burst of laughter louder than anything at their table.

Lord Gormon Peake had raised a cup above his head.

"To the men who remember what a lord's word is worth!"

Several voices answered him. Others did not.

Will looked toward the gathering.

"Your father is in good form tonight."

Gyles glanced over his shoulder.

"My father is always in good form when somebody else is paying for the wine."

"Then he and you have that in common," Hugh said.

Gyles took another almond.

"My father has paid more dearly for his convictions than yours ever has."

Hugh's expression changed.

Before he could answer, Denys held out his empty cup.

"Has anyone thought to bring another jug?"

Harlen was already looking for a servant.

The new jug came from the Arbor, as all the others had, and Hugh declared it inferior to the last.

"You said that about the last," Will told him.

"Because it was."

"Perhaps your father should send for wine from somewhere else."

"My father says the best wine is the wine another man pays for."

"He sounds a wise man," Gyles said.

"He is," Hugh replied, looking pleased.

Orryn had heard Ser Arron Redwyne's name several times since arriving at Highgarden. He was Lord Leo's seneschal, the man who saw to much of the business the old lord preferred to leave to others. Earlier that afternoon, Lord Leo had signed a paper Arron sent him without troubling to read it.

Orryn looked at Hugh, who was helping himself to the wine Harlen had paid for, and tried to imagine him growing into such a man.

It was not easy.

Denys set down his cup. "Are you all going to sit here until dawn?"

"What else would we do?" Gyles asked.

"There's dancing."

"You dance, then."

"I have."

"With whom?"

"A lady."

"Was she pleased?"

"She didn't complain."

"Then she's not yet heard you sing."

Denys leaned down and seized Gyles beneath the arms.

The younger boy gave a shout as Denys hauled him from the bench.

"Come. I'll teach you."

"Get your hands off me!"

"One turn."

"I said leave off."

Denys released him. Gyles stumbled against the table, upsetting a little dish of almonds.

Will began laughing.

"You should have seen your face."

"I told him to put me down."

"And he did."

Gyles straightened his doublet, his cheeks red. For a moment Orryn expected him to strike Denys.

Instead he said, very distinctly, "There are a great many things your family has been given, Tarly. A sense of humour was not one of them."

Denys was still smiling.

"Nor a third castle."

The words were scarcely spoken before Will stopped laughing.

Gyles stood quite still.

For a little while there was only the pipe-player and the sound of a servant sweeping broken pottery from the floor beyond them.

"Denys," Harlen said.

"What?"

"That was unkind."

Gyles turned his head toward Harlen.

"Let him speak, cousin. He likes to remind me of my father's poverty."

"I said nothing of your father."

"You needn't. You wear his command upon your sleeve."

Denys looked down at the red huntsman embroidered upon his coat.

"My grandfather's command," he corrected.

"The distinction must comfort him."

It was no secret that House Peake had once held command in the Dornish Marches. Lord Roderick Tarly had received that charge after the rebellion, when Gormon Peake lost more than the right to command men.

Orryn had heard grown men speak of the same matter in Highgarden, though never with quite such naked dislike.

Harlen put his cup down.

"Come, Gyles. You know Denys meant no harm."

"Do I?"

"He was only making sport."

"So was I."

"Then there's no quarrel."

Gyles regarded his cousin for a moment. He looked as if he might say something worse. Instead he bowed with an exaggeration that caused Hugh to grin.

"As you say, ser."

He sat down and reached for the remaining almonds.

Denys remained standing.

"I didn't mean to give offence," he said after a moment.

"You seldom do," Gyles replied.

There was a fresh silence.

Then Will gathered the fallen dice.

"Who was winning?"

"Me," Matthos said.

"Then you've been robbed. There was money here a moment ago."

That was enough to send Matthos searching beneath the table, while Hugh insisted that half the coins belonged to him. By the time the money had been recovered, Gyles was laughing again.

Orryn was not certain that the quarrel had ended. He had seen men at Grassfield exchange apologies over less and carry their anger into the next season.

But nobody spoke of Starpike again.

A little later Denys came around the table and placed a folded cushion upon Orryn's stool.

"For your foot."

Orryn looked up.

"You've been holding it off the ground for half the night," the knight said. "That won't help it."

"Thank you, ser."

"Thank the horse. If it had broken your foot, I'd have had to carry you upstairs."

He returned to the window before Orryn could think of an answer.

Will watched him go.

"Don't let him hear you say he's kind. He'll never forgive you."

*

By the time the musicians changed their tune, the older company had begun to dance.

Not everyone took part. Lord Rowan remained seated beside a little table crowded with cups, talking to Ser Moryn and a knight in a blue cloak. Lord Gormon had drawn several men toward the open terrace, where their voices rose and fell above the sound of water.

But Ser Olyvar was dancing with a lady in pale yellow, and several younger knights had joined them.

Alaric was among the dancers.

Orryn noticed him because he was laughing.

His master had taken the hand of the woman in dark blue. She moved lightly, turning beneath his arm, and when the dance carried her away she glanced back at him with a smile.

Alaric followed the steps well enough. He had never been a particularly eager dancer at Grassfield, though Lady Jeyne occasionally insisted upon it when guests were present.

Once, at the Merryweathers' visit, Orryn had seen Lady Jeyne tell her husband that he had been invited to dance. Alaric had replied that he had not, and the lady had said she had sent Marya to invite him.

He had danced then, though not for long.

Now he remained upon the floor when the tune ended.

The lady spoke to him. Alaric bent his head to hear her, then laughed and offered his hand for the next dance.

"Your lord is enjoying himself," Will observed.

Orryn looked around. He had not realised Will was watching.

"He likes good company."

"Then he's found some."

"Who is she?"

"Lady Meriel Vyrwel. From Darkdell. A widow."

"Is she?"

"Unless she's married again since yesterday."

Will took a handful of almonds.

"She comes to Highgarden sometimes. She's a friend to Lady Melessa, I think. Or to somebody Lady Melessa knows. I don't concern myself with women's friendships."

"Then why do you know her?"

"Because I know the people who come here."

He spoke as if that settled everything.

Orryn watched the dancers.

Lady Meriel was older than most of the ladies upon the floor. She had an easy manner and did not appear to mind when Alaric missed a step. Once she set a hand against his shoulder to turn him the right way, and he laughed so heartily that the musicians' boy looked over from his drum.

Orryn could not remember his master ever laughing quite like that.

It pleased him.

He had seen Alaric angry, tired, impatient and relieved. He had watched him pace a chamber until the rushes were disturbed, count coins three times because the first sum displeased him, and stand in the heat at a mill while two men told him why neither could do what he required.

At Highgarden, for a little while, none of those things seemed to trouble him.

"He's a good dancer," Harlen said.

Orryn had not noticed him approach.

"Lord Alaric?"

"Yes."

"He was taught properly."

"Lady Jeyne makes certain he remembers."

Harlen smiled. "My mother says the same of my father."

"Does he dance?"

"Not very much."

Orryn had seen Ser Garth at the council table that morning, surrounded by clerks and officers. He found it difficult to imagine the man taking anyone's hand upon a dancing floor.

Harlen leaned against the window beside him.

"I ought to go and speak to Lord Meadows."

"Why?"

"To welcome him. Properly, I mean."

"You've welcomed Matthos."

"That's not the same."

"No, ser."

Harlen watched the dancers.

"I shall do it afterward."

A servant passed, and Harlen took another cup of wine.

Orryn thought he might have forgotten the matter already.

They stood together awhile, watching.

At last Harlen asked, "Do you like Highgarden?"

"Very much."

"Have you seen the lower gardens?"

"Only from above."

"You ought to go. There's a walk beneath the walls where the roses grow over the stones. It's cooler there in the mornings."

"I'd like that."

"Then you must."

Harlen drank.

"Will knows the way. Tell him I said he should take you."

"Thank you, ser."

"Not at all."

His expression was so pleasant that Orryn felt an unexpected desire to please him in return.

"Lord Leo showed us his horses this afternoon," he said.

"Did he? Which ones?"

"The colt Ser Luthor wanted another boy to ride."

Harlen laughed.

"Did he stand on your foot?"

"Yes."

"He stood on mine last week."

"Then he has a habit of it."

"He has several."

Harlen told him about a little grey mare his grandfather had bought from a knight who claimed she had been trained in Dorne. She had arrived with a fine saddle and an appalling temper, and thrown three riders before Lord Leo managed to stay upon her.

Orryn enjoyed the story. Harlen was not as gifted a teller as his grandfather, but he remembered the horse's particulars with such care that Orryn could almost see her: grey ears flattened, narrow chest heaving, the whites of her eyes showing as a groom approached with the bridle.

"Did Lord Leo keep her?" he asked.

"Of course. He said any horse that had thrown three men was worth more than one that had never tried."

"And was he right?"

"She carried him for six years."

Harlen looked pleased.

Then Hugh appeared at his shoulder.

"Harlen. Come here."

"What is it?"

"Will says we may be excused tomorrow if you ask."

"Excused from what?"

"The early exercises."

Harlen glanced at Orryn, then back at his cousin.

"I don't think I can do that."

"Of course you can."

"Ser Luthor wouldn't like it."

"You're his lord's grandson."

"He knows whose grandson I am."

"Then ask your grandfather."

"He's likely abed by now."

"Tomorrow."

Harlen hesitated.

"What's wrong with the exercises?"

"Nothing. But if we're to drink half the night, we ought to be permitted to sleep beyond dawn."

"Then perhaps you should drink less."

Hugh looked astonished.

Orryn nearly laughed.

Harlen coloured.

"I only meant that Ser Luthor might say so."

"He would say so. That's why we need you."

"Well..."

"Just ask him."

Harlen looked toward the table where Will and Gyles were disputing over a wager.

"I suppose I could ask."

"Good."

"But I cannot promise he'll agree."

"He will if you ask properly."

Harlen said nothing.

Hugh took him by the sleeve and led him back toward the others.

Orryn remained by the window.

He wondered why Hugh had come to Harlen rather than asking Ser Luthor himself. Then he remembered Ser Luthor's hard face and the short stick he carried in the practice yard.

It was not difficult to understand.

*

Alaric found him while he was eating a piece of bread.

"Grandison."

Orryn swallowed hurriedly and rose.

"My lord."

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes, my lord."

"Good. You've been standing on that foot a great deal."

"It's better."

"Is it?"

"A little."

Alaric looked down at his boots.

"Then keep it still. There are chairs in this castle, whatever the servants may have told you."

He had a cup in his hand. His cheeks were flushed, and the front of his dark green doublet had come open at the throat. There was a faint smell of wine about him.

"Have you seen Matthos?"

"He's with Will and the other squires."

"Getting into trouble?"

"Not much."

"That sounds promising."

Alaric looked toward the younger company. Matthos was talking animatedly to Gyles Peake, holding two fingers close together as though demonstrating the width of something. Gyles listened with a doubtful expression.

"His mother's going to ask me whether he's settled," Alaric said.

"He seems to be."

"Then I'll have one good answer, at least."

Orryn hesitated.

"Will said there's training tomorrow."

"Of course there is."

"Even after tonight."

"Particularly after tonight."

It was almost precisely what Will had said.

Alaric took another drink.

"You're not to make yourself ill upon wine, Grandison. I should dislike explaining to Lady Jeyne that you survived a horse only to be defeated by a cup."

"No, my lord."

"Nor is Matthos."

"I haven't seen him drink very much."

"Then you've probably been watching the wrong end of the table."

Orryn began to smile.

"Come," Alaric said. "There are people you ought to know."

He led Orryn toward the centre of the gallery.

Lord Rowan was speaking to a knight who had a scar running from the edge of his ear into his beard. Ser Olyvar had left the dancers and was leaning upon the back of a chair. Lady Melessa stood with two women beside the windows.

The woman in blue joined them as they approached.

"Here is the young Grandison," Alaric said. "The one I told you about."

Lady Meriel Vyrwel looked at Orryn.

"He seems rather more respectable than you described, my lord."

"That will not survive acquaintance."

Orryn bowed.

"My lady."

"Lord Meadows says you manage his accounts."

"Not all of them, my lady."

"No," Alaric said. "I still require the use of my fingers."

"But he reads the smaller entries?"

"He reads what I tell him to read."

"And sometimes what he ought not?" she asked.

Alaric laughed.

"He has been known to discover a difficulty after I've declared it settled."

"Then he must be useful."

"Frequently. It's a most irritating quality."

She smiled at Orryn.

"Take care not to become too useful, Master Grandison. Men who can do things properly are seldom permitted to rest."

Orryn was not sure whether she meant Alaric or himself.

Behind them, Lord Rowan was saying something about grain wagons.

"...and if the carts are permitted to cross free at Bitterbridge under a grant from Lord Leo, I should like to know why the next clerk considers the grant expired."

Ser Olyvar replied, "You should ask the clerk."

"I did."

"And?"

"He asked me for a copy."

Alaric turned.

"Did you give him one?"

"I gave him the original."

"Then he's a braver clerk than most."

Rowan's mouth tightened.

"He sent it back with three places marked."

"Three?" Lady Meriel asked.

"One for the date, one for the seal and one for the wording."

"Then he had plenty to occupy him."

"That's what I told him."

Olyvar laughed. "You ought to have sent him to my lord brother. Garth would have found a fourth."

"Indeed," Rowan said. "But I should prefer to have my grain delivered before winter."

He turned to Alaric.

"You've had dealings with the officers yourself, I understand."

"Three mornings' worth in two days."

"And what did you learn?"

"That my road is poorer than I thought, and that several men wish it repaired."

"Who will pay?"

"That appears to be the one point upon which they have not yet agreed."

Lord Rowan laughed.

"A familiar difficulty."

"Lord Roxton wishes it made fit for soldiers," Alaric continued. "Ser Garth wishes to know what it costs. Ser Moryn wishes to know whether my tenants have been heard. All good questions."

"And Lord Leo?" Rowan asked.

Alaric looked toward the open terrace.

"Lord Leo wishes men to remember the kindnesses he has done them."

There was a little silence.

Lady Meriel said, "Then I hope somebody has written them down."

"He has," Alaric answered.

"Has he?"

"On more pieces of parchment than any one man can remember."

Lord Rowan raised his cup.

"To the parchments, then. May they prove as durable as the promises."

The others drank.

Orryn had a clearer memory than he wanted of Lord Leo placing his name upon a paper in the stables without reading a word. He thought of the tollman who had refused green wax at a bridge on the road from Bitterbridge.

Lady Meriel was looking at Alaric again.

"And what is your own opinion, my lord? Of the roads, I mean."

"That they are too narrow, too dry and too expensive."

"An opinion nobody can dispute."

"Then I must be getting better at politics."

She laughed.

Alaric looked pleased.

Ser Olyvar began telling Rowan of a captain who had presented himself for inspection with forty men and only twenty-six proper shields. Rowan objected that the captain ought to be praised for bringing forty men at all, when some lords could barely be persuaded to bring ten.

Their conversation grew louder.

Lady Meriel turned to Orryn.

"Does Lord Meadows always speak so little of his own merits?"

Orryn glanced at his master.

"No, my lady."

Alaric gave him a look of mock outrage.

"Traitor."

"I only meant..."

"I know precisely what you meant. Go on. You'll find more agreeable company elsewhere."

Orryn bowed and withdrew before his lord could invent another charge against him.

As he went, Lady Meriel said something that made Alaric laugh again.

*

The younger company had acquired another jug, two more boys and a song.

Will knew the words. So did Denys, though they disagreed over the third verse and sang different lines until the whole thing collapsed into laughter.

Harlen sang softly and not very well.

Hugh shouted the chorus.

Gyles had discovered that Matthos knew a different version from Grassfield and insisted that he sing it. Matthos, who ordinarily liked nothing better than an audience, suddenly claimed he had forgotten the words.

"Sing," Gyles commanded.

"No."

"You're ashamed."

"I'm not."

"Then prove it."

"I've told you, I don't remember."

"I think he sings like a goose," Hugh said.

Matthos's face darkened.

Orryn knew what was coming.

"His name isn't Goose," he said.

Hugh looked at him.

"What?"

"Mine is."

Will burst out laughing.

Matthos stared.

"You weren't supposed to tell them."

"Then you might have warned me."

Gyles leaned forward.

"Goose?"

"It's an old name," Orryn said.

"From Grandview?"

"From Grassfield."

"What did you do to deserve it?"

"I'd rather not say."

"Then it must be good."

"It isn't."

"That makes it better."

For the next quarter of an hour, Gyles called him Goose whenever he spoke. Hugh tried it too, but gave up when Orryn failed to answer.

Matthos alternated between looking furious and laughing.

Will found the whole business delightful.

At last Harlen said, "We ought to call him by his name."

"But Goose is his name," Gyles protested.

"He said it was an old one."

"It is old. I'm told it dates from his hatching."

Harlen laughed despite himself.

"Even so."

Gyles took a drink.

"As my lord cousin commands."

Orryn could not tell whether Harlen recognised the mockery.

He suspected he did, for the young knight stopped smiling and became interested in the musicians.

The fiddle had fallen silent for a moment. A woman was asking for another dance, and one of the players was tightening a string.

Harlen rose.

"I ought to see whether my mother needs anything."

"Your mother?" Hugh said.

"She's been speaking with Lady Melessa."

"She won't need you."

"I should ask."

"Stay," Gyles said. "We've hardly begun."

"I'll come back."

"Promise?"

Harlen hesitated.

"Yes."

He went.

Hugh watched him disappear into the company.

"He always does that."

"What?" Orryn asked.

"Leaves when we're having fun."

"He said he'd return."

"He says a great many things."

Will began gathering the scattered coins.

"If he tells Ser Luthor we ought to sleep late tomorrow, I shall think him a very fine knight indeed."

"He won't," Denys said.

"Why not?"

"Because Risley isn't a fool."

"He isn't my father," Will replied.

"He's your uncle."

"Which is why I know."

Gyles stretched out his legs.

"Harlen will ask. Risley will refuse. Harlen will apologise to us, and Risley will have us in the yard before dawn to teach us the cost of asking."

"Then you've chosen an excellent night to drink," Denys said.

"Have I ever chosen otherwise?"

Hugh laughed, but Matthos looked less pleased.

"Ser Moryn told me I was to report at first light."

"Then you'd better report," Will said.

"What about the others?"

"The others have been here long enough to know what happens when they don't."

Matthos considered this.

"Would Ser Harlen really be unable to have us excused?"

Will looked toward the place where Harlen had gone.

"He could ask his grandfather. He could ask his father. He could even ask Ser Moryn."

"And?"

"And one of them might ask Ser Luthor, who would tell them why he expects squires to be awake in the morning."

Gyles laughed.

"Then what use is he?"

Will did not answer at once.

Orryn watched him turn a silver stag between his fingers.

"He's a good friend," Will said at last.

It was said quite simply.

Gyles gave an exaggerated sigh.

"Very well. We shall all rise at dawn for the sake of friendship."

He reached for another drink.

*

It was late when Orryn decided to seek Alaric.

The musicians had become less exact, and the servants no longer hurried to replace every empty cup. Several of the older guests had retired. Lady Melessa had taken leave of the company some time before, after speaking quietly to her husband.

Lord Rowan was gone.

Lord Gormon Peake remained, seated beneath a window with two knights and a cup that seemed never to empty.

Will was talking to another squire about the following morning's duties. Matthos had fallen into a disagreement with Hugh over whether a horse from the Arbor could outrun one bred in the Marches. Neither had seen the other's horse.

Gyles had gone to find more wine.

Denys was dancing again.

Harlen had returned, as promised, and was listening to an older knight speak about a tournament. He looked sleepy, though he continued to nod whenever the knight addressed him.

Orryn had enjoyed himself.

He had drunk more wine than Lady Jeyne would have allowed at Grassfield, and his foot hurt whenever he stood. He was tired of the smoke and the pipe-player, and the sweetness of the wine had begun to leave a sour taste in his mouth.

He wanted his bed.

But he could not simply disappear without his master's leave.

He looked around for Alaric.

The lord of Grassfield was no longer among the dancers. Orryn searched the open gallery, the benches and the knot of men near the hearth.

He did not find him.

He went toward the terrace.

The night beyond the windows was cool and dark. A few candles burned beneath the arches, set inside glass lanterns to shelter them from the breeze. The garden lay below in folds of black and silver, with a thin ribbon of water catching the moonlight as it fell into a basin.

Orryn stood for a little while upon the threshold, grateful for the air.

Somewhere along the terrace a woman laughed.

He recognised the sound before he recognised the voice.

Lady Meriel.

He turned toward it.

The terrace bent around the side of the gallery, where a shallow arch sheltered a stone bench. Roses climbed the wall above it, and the leaves cast moving shadows in the candlelight.

Alaric stood beneath the arch with Lady Meriel Vyrwel.

They were close enough that Orryn stopped walking.

He could hear their voices, though not every word.

"...and I told you I was a married man," Alaric was saying.

"Several times."

"Then you cannot accuse me of concealing it."

"I had noticed your honesty."

There was amusement in her voice.

Alaric said something too quietly for Orryn to hear.

Lady Meriel laughed again.

Orryn ought to have turned back.

He knew that much.

But he had already stepped into the light from the gallery, and before he could move away Lady Meriel reached up and put her hand against Alaric's collar.

Alaric bent his head.

He kissed her.

It was not a dance, nor a jest, nor one of the courtly courtesies Orryn had watched the knights exchange with ladies during the evening.

Lady Meriel's hand remained at his collar. Alaric had one arm about her waist.

Orryn felt his face grow hot.

He stepped backward.

His injured foot struck the raised edge of a paving stone.

He stumbled, catching himself against the wall.

The sound was small, but both of them turned.

For one dreadful instant Orryn could think of nothing to say.

Lady Meriel lowered her hand.

Alaric looked at him.

"Grandison."

"My lord."

"What are you doing out here?"

"I was looking for you."

Alaric said nothing.

Orryn could hear the musicians beginning another tune inside.

"I wanted to ask whether I might retire," he managed.

Lady Meriel looked away toward the garden.

Alaric's face had become very still.

"Go to your chamber," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"I shall come presently."

Orryn bowed.

He did not know whether to bow to Lady Meriel as well. He began to do so, thought better of it, and turned away.

He went back through the gallery without looking at either of them.

*

The company had grown louder in his absence.

Hugh was attempting to persuade Matthos to wager his new saddle against a horse he had never seen. Will insisted that no wager was worth making unless both sides could name the thing being risked. Gyles had returned with another jug and was arguing that the price of a saddle had nothing to do with its value.

Orryn passed them.

"Goose!" Matthos called.

He stopped.

"Where are you going?"

"To bed."

"So soon?"

"It's late."

"Not that late."

"Late enough."

Gyles raised his cup.

"Good night, Goose. Mind the stairs. There are more of them here than at Grandview."

Orryn made a small bow and went on.

Harlen was standing near the doorway.

"Are you leaving?" he asked.

"Yes, ser."

"Then good night."

"Good night, Ser Harlen."

The young knight looked toward the company behind him.

"Did you enjoy yourself?"

"Very much."

"I'm glad."

He seemed to mean it.

Orryn hesitated.

"Thank you for the wine."

"It was nothing."

Harlen smiled.

"And tell Lord Meadows I hope to speak with him tomorrow. I meant to tonight."

"I will, ser."

"Unless he's gone to bed already."

"I don't think he has."

"Well. Tomorrow, then."

Orryn bowed again and went out.

The passage beyond the gallery was almost empty. A servant carried away a basket of candle ends, and somewhere above him a door closed. Highgarden seemed to have become an entirely different castle now that so many people had gone to sleep.

His footsteps sounded upon the stairs.

He thought of Lady Jeyne.

It was not something he had meant to do.

He remembered her at Grassfield, standing beside the great bed while Alaric complained that he had been given too much work and too little time to rest. She had told him that no one wanted anything of him that evening, which was the thing that distinguished him from most men in his own household.

He had lain back upon the bed with his boots still on, and she had laughed.

Orryn had seen them argue as well. Lady Jeyne could be very quiet when she was displeased, and Alaric could become impatient over trifles.

He had never wondered whether his master might kiss another woman.

Now he wished he had not seen it.

There had been other things at Grassfield he had wished not to see.

Alyn Merryweather and Leonette, at the end of the stair. Alyn had been the finest companion Orryn had known, careless and generous and sometimes cruel without meaning to be. Lady Jeyne had learned enough of what passed between Alyn and her daughter to send him away.

Orryn had not told her.

He wondered what she would say if she knew about tonight.

Then he became angry with himself for wondering.

Alaric was his lord, and Lady Jeyne his lady. He served them both in different ways, though the pledge he had sworn had been made to Alaric.

He had not been asked to keep a secret.

Perhaps there was no secret. Lady Meriel had kissed his master upon an open terrace, scarcely a dozen paces from a gallery full of people. Someone else might have seen them. Someone might have known already.

Orryn could not tell.

By the time he reached the guest chamber, he was too tired to think clearly about it.

The room was dark.

He found the candle beside the bed and lit it from the ember kept beneath the hearth cover. The little flame caught, showing Alaric's travelling chest, the folded clothes upon its lid, the washbasin and the second bed that Matthos had occupied until that morning.

The bed was empty now.

Orryn sat upon his own and pulled off his boots.

His foot had swollen a little beneath the stocking. He pressed it gently and decided that the colt had done him less harm than the stairs.

There was water in the jug. He poured some into the basin, washed his face and drank the rest from a cup.

He would have liked to lie down without undressing, but his lord might need him when he returned. He folded his tunic over the chest, laid out Alaric's nightshirt and set fresh water beside his bed.

Then he sat in the dark.

He heard the distant music for a long time.

At last footsteps came along the passage, and the door opened.

Alaric entered carrying a candle, its flame leaning sideways in the draught. He shut the door with his heel and stood for a moment looking at the travelling chest.

"That is not where I left it."

Orryn sat up.

"My lord?"

"The chest."

"It hasn't moved."

"Then I have."

Alaric laughed at his own answer. He had been drinking enough that the laugh came more easily than Orryn was accustomed to hearing, though he stood steadily enough and had found his chamber without assistance.

"You're awake," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"Why?"

"I was waiting for you."

"I told you to go to bed."

Orryn knew he had. He had undressed and lain beneath the sheet for a little while, but sleep had not come, and when he heard footsteps on the stairs he had sat up again.

"I thought you might need something."

"I need someone to tell me where my bed is."

"Behind you, my lord."

"An excellent squire."

Alaric set the candle down and began working at the fastening of his doublet. He loosened the first hook, missed the second, and swore at the third.

Orryn watched him.

He had helped Alaric dress on a hundred mornings. He knew which fastening caught, which sleeve was slightly tighter, which belt belonged with the green doublet and which had a buckle that rubbed against the cloth. There was nothing unfamiliar in the work.

Yet tonight he found himself hoping Alaric would ask him to do it.

His master pulled at the hook again.

"Seven hells."

"Shall I help, my lord?"

Alaric looked over.

"Can you see better than I can?"

"I think so."

"Then come here."

Orryn rose at once.

He was conscious of moving too quickly, though Alaric paid no attention. His master had already lowered his hands and turned a little toward the candle.

Orryn began with the troublesome fastening. It had caught a thread from the lining, and he had to work the cloth loose with the edge of his nail.

"There," he said.

"Good."

But there were other hooks below it, and Alaric made no move to take over.

Orryn continued.

His fingers knew what to do. The work was simple enough that he could hardly pretend to require all his attention, yet he kept his eyes upon it even after each fastening had come free.

When he finished, he did not immediately step away.

"Your doublet, my lord."

"Take it, then."

Alaric shrugged it off and handed it to him.

Orryn folded it carefully. More carefully than it required. He straightened one sleeve, found that the other had turned inside out, and corrected that too.

His master sat upon the bed.

"Boots next, I suppose."

Orryn looked up.

"If you wish."

"Unless you intend me to sleep in them."

He knelt.

It was ordinary work. A squire removed his knight's boots after a long day, particularly when the knight had been riding, drinking or wearing armour. Orryn had done it before, and nobody would have thought anything of finding him there.

He knew that.

He also knew he was taking longer than he needed to.

The first boot came away with an unpleasant sucking sound.

Alaric laughed.

"There's a fine noise."

Orryn smiled despite himself.

The second boot caught at the heel. He knew it would. Alaric braced a hand upon the bed and lifted his foot.

"Pull, Grandison."

Orryn pulled.

The boot came free so suddenly that he almost fell backward.

"Careful."

"Yes, my lord."

"You've had enough wine to ruin your balance."

"I haven't had so very much."

"That is what every man says before falling down a stair."

Alaric held out his hand for the boot. Orryn passed it to him, and his master set it beside the bed.

For a moment Orryn remained where he was.

He should have stood.

There was nothing more to be done with the boots, and no reason to remain kneeling. He understood that quite clearly. Yet the thought of stepping away displeased him in a way he could not have explained, even to himself.

"Up you get," Alaric said.

Orryn rose.

His master had begun removing the rest of his outer clothing. Orryn turned toward the chest to put the doublet away, then found himself glancing back.

He looked away.

A moment later he looked again.

It was not curiosity about how a man dressed for bed. He had shared chambers with boys and attended knights often enough. Nor was there anything remarkable happening before him.

That made it worse.

He knew he was lingering over a task that had ended. He knew that if Alaric noticed, he would have to invent some reason for remaining there.

He did not want to be noticed.

But neither did he want to leave.

The wine had made his thoughts less orderly. At Grassfield he might have turned away at once, occupied himself with water or candles, and told himself afterward that there had been nothing to consider.

Tonight he found himself making the decision to turn away, and then failing to carry it out.

"Grandison."

He started.

"My lord?"

"The nightshirt."

"Yes, my lord."

It lay upon the chest, where Orryn had placed it earlier.

He fetched it and held it out.

Alaric accepted it without a glance.

"Thank you."

There it was. The entire matter settled in two words.

Orryn should have been relieved.

Instead he felt an unreasonable disappointment.

Alaric put on the nightshirt and sat back upon the bed, rubbing one hand over his face.

"That was a fine evening."

"Yes, my lord."

"Did you enjoy yourself?"

"Very much."

"Good. You ought to. Highgarden has its uses beyond making men count what they owe."

He reached for the water Orryn had left beside the bed and drank.

"Did you meet that young Tyrell? Harlen?"

"Yes, my lord."

"What did you make of him?"

"He's kind."

"So his grandfather says."

"He gave us wine. And he was very courteous."

"Anything else?"

Orryn considered.

"He let the others take his money."

Alaric lowered his cup.

"Did he?"

"They were throwing dice. He said he wanted to stop, but they persuaded him to go on."

"Did they take much?"

"Several silver stags."

"Seven save us. At that age I'd have fought a man for half as much."

Orryn smiled.

"Did you win any?"

"A little."

"How much?"

"Four coppers, my lord."

"Four!" Alaric looked impressed. "Then we shall have to put you in charge of the treasury. Ser Garth could learn something from you."

Orryn laughed.

"I lost two afterward."

"Then you shall have to serve Ser Moryn instead."

His master settled back against the bedpost, looking very pleased with himself.

"Did Matthos win?"

"At first."

"And afterward?"

"He lost most of it."

"Good. He has learned something useful already."

"He didn't think so."

"Then he'll learn it again tomorrow."

Alaric finished his water and set the cup aside.

"And what of the ladies? Did you find anyone worth dancing with?"

Orryn felt his cheeks warm.

"No, my lord."

"Not one?"

"I wasn't asked."

Alaric laughed.

"You're not a maiden, Grandison. You're permitted to ask."

"I know."

"Do you? You spent half the evening sitting with those boys."

"So did Ser Harlen."

"Harlen has half the Reach trying to marry him. He can afford to remain seated."

Orryn could think of no answer.

Alaric stretched his arms and winced.

"Seven hells. I shall feel those dances in the morning."

"You danced a great deal, my lord."

"I did."

"More than I've ever seen you dance."

"Then you have been fortunate enough to avoid my better evenings."

He laughed at that, plainly delighted with the memory of the night.

"Lady Meriel is an excellent dancer," he added.

"Yes, my lord."

"Better than I am, certainly."

Orryn remembered the terrace, the dark blue gown, and Lady Meriel's hand upon his master's collar.

Alaric appeared to find the recollection entirely pleasant.

"Though I believe I managed well enough."

"You did, my lord."

"Did I?"

"She seemed pleased."

Alaric looked at him for a moment, then burst out laughing.

"Well observed, Grandison!"

Orryn smiled, embarrassed and pleased at once.

"There's more to being a good squire than remembering where I've put my boots. You must know when your knight has acquitted himself honourably."

"Then I suppose you did, my lord."

"You suppose?"

"I wasn't watching every dance."

"A great pity. I'd have had a witness."

Orryn laughed with him.

For a little while Alaric seemed less like the lord who counted every expense at Grassfield and more like one of the young knights in the gallery. He had enjoyed the wine, enjoyed the dancing, and evidently saw no need to be solemn about either.

"Did you ever have evenings like this when you were younger, my lord?" Orryn asked.

"Younger?"

Alaric looked genuinely offended.

"How old do you suppose I am?"

"Thirty, my lord."

"Thirty. Listen to him. He speaks as though he's found me in a tomb."

"I didn't mean..."

"At thirty a man is only beginning to know which wine to drink and which women to dance with."

Orryn laughed.

"At fourteen," Alaric continued, "a boy thinks he'll have learned everything worth knowing by twenty. At twenty he thinks thirty is old. At thirty he discovers he was an idiot at twenty."

"And at forty?"

"I shall tell you when I get there. Assuming I survive tomorrow's headache."

He held out the empty cup.

"More water, if you please."

Orryn fetched it.

Alaric drank, then handed the cup back.

"Come, Grandison. Don't look so solemn. You've had good food, good wine and a better evening than most boys your age could hope for."

"I did enjoy it, my lord."

"Then show it. You're permitted to be happy without first making an account of the expense."

Orryn smiled.

"There. Much better."

Alaric began settling himself beneath the coverlet.

"Now, if you're quite finished making an old man of me, put out that candle."

"Yes, my lord."

"And don't forget Lady Arlanna tomorrow."

"I won't."

"Nor my clean doublet."

"No, my lord."

"Nor to wake me."

"At what hour?"

Alaric considered.

"An hour later than usual."

Orryn looked at him.

"Ser Luthor says the squires are to train at dawn."

"Then you had better train at dawn."

"And wake you afterward?"

"Precisely."

"That seems unfair."

"Of course it is. I'm a lord."

Orryn laughed again.

Alaric grinned at him and pulled the coverlet up.

"Good night, Grandison."

"Good night, my lord."

Orryn extinguished the candle.

He lay down with the sound of his master's laughter still in his ears.

He had spent so much of the evening watching Alaric attend to other people that he had scarcely expected to have him to himself afterward. Yet they had spoken more freely than they often did at Grassfield, and for a little while Alaric had seemed content simply to enjoy his company.

Orryn wished they might have talked longer.

Across the chamber the bed ropes creaked, and Alaric gave a long, satisfied sigh.

Within a few minutes his breathing had grown slow and even.

Orryn remained awake.

He remembered his master's laughter, the ease with which he had accepted Orryn's help, and the careless pleasure with which he had spoken of Lady Meriel.

There was something he envied in that ease, though he could not have said precisely what.

He turned upon his side.

Tomorrow Alaric would be his lord again, with questions to ask, people to meet and work to be done. Orryn would fetch his clothes and attend him as usual.

For tonight, he was glad they had laughed together.
