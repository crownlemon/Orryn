Bitterbridge, 208 AC

The Mander had shrunk since Orryn last crossed it.

He saw it from the road a little before the town, a long brown shine between the willows, and it was some while before he understood what was wrong with it. Then they came down to the bridge, and he saw the sandbars. There had been none when they rode down to Highgarden. Now two yellow tongues of sand lay in the middle of the river with gulls standing on them, and the piers showed a pale band of dry stone a good yard above the water, like the ring a wet cup leaves on a board. The boats that had crowded the wharf were drawn up on the mud with their keels out. Under the striped awning the same clerk sat with his chin on his fist. He had nothing to count.

“It’s dropped,” Orryn said.

“It has,” said Lord Alaric. “Lord Armond will tell you it was lower in his father’s time. He will be wrong, and he will say it very loudly.”

They had come two days from Highgarden, and the dust of it was in Orryn’s teeth. He was fourteen, and Lord Alaric’s squire, and by the second noon he had learned all over again how much of squiring is dust. Ser Bertram Corliss, a household knight of Grassfield, rode behind with the two men and the groom, and had not spoken since the noon halt except to curse the sumpter. Orryn had been on Rook long enough to know when the dun meant to stop and make water, which was now. He let it, and was passed by Alaric, who did not look round.

The castle stood above the road behind its pale walls, with the white banners hanging slack and the yellow centaur on each one drawing his bow at nothing. Lord Armond Caswell was in the courtyard before the gate had finished opening. He was Lady Jeyne’s father, and so goodfather to Alaric, whose lady she was: a big man with white moustaches and a red nose, who took Alaric by both shoulders as he had the first time, so hard that Orryn thought a man might be greeted to death at Bitterbridge.

“Back already! I told my lady wife you would not stay at Highgarden. Nobody stays at Highgarden who has a mill at home.”

“I have two,” said Alaric.

“Then you’re a fool twice over. Where’s Jeyne’s boy?”

“With Ser Moryn. As you know.”

“Aye. So he is.” Armond let go of him, and for a moment looked as if he had come into a room and forgotten what for. “Well. He’ll come to no harm in a Tyrell stable. Where’s my wife? Brenna!”

Lady Brenna had been standing in the arch of the passage all the while. She was slight and brisk, in a gown the colour of dry sage, with the Tyrell rose in gold thread at her sleeve and the same fixed smile that Orryn remembered, as though it had been pinned there at her dressing and she meant to wear it until she was unpinned. She was Armond’s second wife, Lord Leo’s youngest daughter, and Lady Jeyne’s stepmother, though she was scarcely older than her stepdaughter, and Orryn had still not found the place in his head where all of that sat comfortably.

“You are early, Lord Alaric,” she said. “I had counted you for tomorrow.”

“The roads were dry.”

“The roads have been dry for a moon. I had thought you might be delayed by something else.” She gave him her hand, and then, after a moment, gave it to Orryn. “Master Grandison. You have grown thinner. Armond, let them wash before you ask them everything.”

“I’ll ask them nothing till supper,” said Armond. “Supper, and then everything.”

Joffrey was in the stable yard, sitting on an upturned bucket with his lute case across his knees, in the shade of the same wall where he had eaten his bread. Orryn did not see him until he spoke.

“You came back.”

“We were always coming back.”

“Everyone says that. Almost nobody does.” Joffrey did not get up. He was thin and pale and sandy, as he had been, with the same long nose burned red, and a smear of rosin on his sleeve. He was fifteen, Lady Brenna’s son and Armond’s heir, and so Lady Jeyne’s half-brother, though she was thirteen years his elder and Orryn had never once heard her speak of him as anything but a boy who needed feeding. “How was Highgarden?”

“Big.”

“Is that all?”

“I’ll tell you after supper.”

“Tell me during supper. They’ll be telling it wrong at the high table, and I shall want something to laugh at.”

They supped in the small hall, under the painted centaur with its forelegs in the air, with the hunting spears on the walls and the horns that were too big for the room, and the shutters open to let in what air there was. There was no river fish tonight. A servant told Orryn, when he asked, that the men who netted the Mander for the castle had come back that morning with three eels and a boot. There was mutton instead, and onions, and bread that was not quite white, and a wheel of cheese that went once round the board and was not offered a second time.

Lord Armond had been as good as his word. He asked everything.

Who had been at Highgarden, and who was dead, and who was wed or about to be, and whether it was true that Lord Gormon Peake had stood up at a feast and drunk to men who remembered what a lord’s word was worth. Alaric answered him in order and a little shortened, the way a man leads a horse he is fond of, and Armond laughed and slapped the board and now and again turned to Orryn for a name. Orryn, who had been warned by Ser Bertram’s elbow, gave it.

To Armond, Alaric gave the warm and brief version. The old lord was well and had shown him his horses. The young knights were as thick as flies and about as useful. Ser Olyvar, who was Lady Brenna’s brother, had offered for Matthos in the yard and taken his refusal as a joke against himself.

“That’s Olyvar,” said Armond, delighted. “He’d be pleasant to a man who was hanging him.”

“He is pleasant,” said Lady Brenna, cutting her mutton into very small pieces. “It is not the same as kind. He has never been refused anything that mattered, so he takes a refusal for a courtesy and rides away to be pleasant elsewhere. Did you see his riders?”

“At Little Ford.”

“And?”

“Forty horses ate a village’s winter in two nights,” said Alaric. “He paid for it with a purse he did not weigh.”

“He would.” She did not smile. “He is my brother, Lord Alaric, and I will not hear him called a fool by anyone else. I shall say it once, here, to those who will not repeat it.”

“I will not repeat it.”

Armond had stopped listening. “And Lady Arlanna? She received you kindly?”

“Kindly. She asked after Jeyne and the children. She says she will look for a husband for Elyce.”

“A Highgarden match!” Armond put down his cup so hard it jumped. “By the Seven, Alaric. Jeyne’s girl, and a Tyrell cousin at the end of her—”

“She gave no name. She said she had not given one before its time in forty years.”

“My mother does not give names,” said Lady Brenna. “Do not let him count it done, Armond. She means that she will look. It is more than most men get, and it is not a promise.”

“It is a good deal.”

“It is a good deal. It is not a promise.”

Armond went a little red and then laughed. Lady Brenna went on with her knife. At the lower board, beside Joffrey, Orryn watched her put it down at last and fold her hands, and saw the fixed smile shift at one corner, as though somebody had touched a nail. He knew that Lady Arlanna had said something to Alaric about her daughter that was not about Elyce at all. Lady Melessa had told him so, afterward, with her shrewd friendly face. Alaric had not repeated it, and nobody had asked him to, and Orryn kept it where it was.

“And the grain?” said Lady Brenna. “I have heard the carts.”

“You have heard them from Rowan’s people.”

“I have heard them from everyone. Tell me what you saw.”

So Alaric told her, and told it straight, as he did when it was for her and not for her husband. Ten carts to a string, two spears to a cart, and the grain let out at a fixed share where every lord on the road could watch it go.

“In the open,” she said. “And not in anyone’s pocket. It is more than my father does.” She looked at Armond, and away, and spoke to the board. “My brother Garth is the only one of the three who has asked what the Reach can feed. I do not love him for it. He is a cold man and he writes things down, and Moryn would give me a better supper. But when the wells go dry a lord who counts is a mercy, and a lord who forgives is not.”

“Garth would put a seal on the sunrise,” said Armond comfortably.

“Then the sun would know when to rise.” She said it quite mildly. It was her own answer from the last time, and nobody laughed.

Alaric gave them the rest quickly. That Ser Moryn Tyrell, Lord Leo’s second son, had taken Matthos into his household and fed his friends, whose barns were full and whose doors were shut. That Lord Osmund Leygood, the Justiciar, sat in Highgarden’s lesser hall and ruled between neighbours, fairly, and did it well. That Ser Lyman Beesbury, who kept Lord Leo’s treasure, had a Hightower mother and a father who owed his oath to Oldtown, and found the two seldom asked of him on the same day.

“And Lord Leo forgives a man one year and the next man the year after,” said Alaric, “and calls it peace.”

Armond grunted. Lady Brenna said, “And what does my brother’s road do to a house that lives on its bridge?”

“He means the grain to go over every bridge between here and the Arbor without being stopped for silver. He asks. He has not yet had to tell.”

“Asks.”

“For now.”

“A man who asks for leave to cross my bridge,” said Armond, “is a man who has already decided it is his.”

“Armond.”

“What?”

“Let him tell it.”

“I’ll let him tell it when I’ve had my cheese. This is my table, madam, and you’ve been at him since the mutton.”

He said it jovially, in the voice he used for asking for salt. Lady Brenna took up her cup, and drank, and set it down. “Then I shall ask it again when you have eaten,” she said. Armond laughed, and cut himself a piece of the cheese, and Orryn found that he had been counting the length of the silence, and was ashamed of it, and could not stop.

She asked it of Orryn instead.

“Master Grandison. A tollman on a road of my husband’s refused Lord Leo’s green wax. My father heard of it in his stable, from a boy.” She said it very pleasantly. “I should like to know what you told him.”

Armond’s knife stopped.

Alaric did not look at Orryn. There was only a quality of stillness in the back of his neck, and Orryn thought of the stones in the road to Highgarden, and how a horse would stand when it knew there was something in the dark that it could not see.

“I told him what I saw, my lady,” he said. “We came down the Bitterbridge road, and at a bridge a man took silver from a cart that carried a paper with green wax. The paper said the cart might pass. The man said it was not his lord’s wax. The carter paid, and the cart was half a day late. Lord Leo asked how things went on the road, and I told him.”

“Did you say it was my husband’s man?”

“I said it was a man at a bridge on the Bitterbridge road.”

“And what did my father say?”

“Nothing, my lady. I thought he had not heard me. That afternoon he signed a paper for Ser Arron without reading it. I don’t know whether it was mine.”

“It was somebody’s,” said Lady Brenna.

“My man takes what a paper says and no more,” said Armond. His voice had lost some of its jolliness. “If it says nothing he takes silver. That was how it was in my father’s day, and I’ll not have Highgarden teach my tollman what wax to love.”

“It was how it was,” said Lady Brenna. “I do not think it will be how it is.” She turned to the boy, not unkindly. “You did right to tell the truth, Master Grandison. I should like you to know that it was not necessarily wise.”

“He told it because he was asked, my lady,” said Alaric. “By his lord’s lord. I should dislike a squire who did otherwise.”

“So should I.” She nodded, and took up her cup, and Orryn’s ears went on burning for some time.

“She’s been wanting to say that since you rode in,” Joffrey murmured, behind a dish of onions. “She says it to everyone. She said it to the cook.”

“Said what?”

“That the truth isn’t necessarily wise.” He looked into his cup. “It’s the nearest she comes to a blessing.”

It was almost dark when the captain of the gate came in.

He was a heavy man with a bad knee and a bald head the colour of a boiled ham, called Rudd, and he stood just inside the door as if he expected to be hit for what he had to say.

“Riders on the Highgarden road, my lord. At the bridgehead, thirty horse or near it, and a sergeant’s mule with a cask. They carry the rose and a green banner with a red huntsman on it. The knight asks guest-right, and the bridge.”

“Whose huntsman?”

“Tarly, my lord.”

Ser Cleyton Caswell, Armond’s cousin and Joffrey’s master, a fair vain man who had hung his new blue cloak across the back of his chair where all could see it, rose at once with a bright face. “A Tarly! I knew Lord Roderick’s heir at a tourney once. He was entirely charming about my seat.” Nobody answered him. Armond was already looking at the door.

“Let the knight in,” he said. “The horses can wait at the gate until I’ve had a look at him.”

He had been a long day and a night on the road, and looked it. His coat was green, darker than Tyrell silk, with a red huntsman bending his bow on the breast, and the dust lay on it so thick that the huntsman seemed to be drawing from the bottom of a well. He was tall and long-bodied, with thick brown hair cut short above the ears, and he came down the length of the small hall with the easy stride of a man who had not once in his life wondered if he would be let in. A stocky grey sergeant followed a pace behind him, missing two fingers from his left hand, with the look of someone who had walked a great many young knights into a great many halls and expected to walk this one out again.

Orryn knew him before he reached the hearth. It was the knight who had put a cushion under his foot, at the south gallery, and told him to thank the horse. Ser Denys Tarly was twenty, Lord Roderick Tarly’s grandson, from Horn Hill, whose lords had held the Dornish Marches for the Reach since the Peakes lost them. But he was the son of a younger son. Someone had told Orryn so, at Highgarden, as a thing that explained a great deal. The heir at Horn Hill was Denys’s uncle, Ser Samwyle, and Denys had a sword, a horse and a grandfather, and was expected to make something of them. Orryn had liked him at Highgarden, so long as the jokes were falling on someone else, and had watched Gyles Peake learn what it was to be the one they fell on.

He bowed to Armond and to Lady Brenna as a man lays a sword down on a table. “My lord. My lady. Ser Olyvar sends his love to his sister, and a cask of Arbor red that is half a mile behind me on my sergeant’s mule, and begs she will forgive him for sending a boy where he ought to have come himself. Hollis, here, will see to the cask.”

“He sends wine,” said Lady Brenna, “when he is about to ask for something.”

“I was told you would say so, my lady.”

“By whom?”

“By him.” The corner of his mouth moved. He turned his head, and Orryn saw him find Alaric, and then the boy standing behind the lower board. “Lord Meadows. I had not thought to find you so soon. And the lad the colt stood on. How is the foot?”

“Mended, ser.”

“Then the horse was kind.”

“Sit, ser,” said Armond. “Eat. You’ll want it.”

“My thanks, my lord, but I’d sooner say my errand standing. I am Lord Roderick’s grandson and Ser Olyvar’s man, and I am bound for the Mander lords with a plain request. Lord Tarly asks every lord who can spare ten spears for the Marches before the year turns. Ser Olyvar sent me because I know the road and my grandfather’s face. Ten spears from Bitterbridge, my lord. Hay and water for thirty horses tonight, and your bridge at first light.”

Armond laughed. Then he stopped, and the laughter went out of him like water from a bucket, and what was left was red and heavy and not unfriendly.

“No.”

“My lord?”

“I’ll give you a bed and a cup, lad, and your sergeant a pallet by the stable, and I’ll hear your grandfather’s message with a good heart. I’ll not give you the rest.” He leaned forward over the board. “I’ve four thousand souls in this town and a granary half what it was a moon ago, and a river I could cross tomorrow in my slippers. I’ll not feed thirty horses. I’ll not send ten of my men to stand in the dust of Dorne while my own fords lie bare. They’re what I’m for.”

“Defender of the Fords,” said Denys Tarly. He said it lightly, like a man reading a name off a stone. “I had heard that was a title that did some defending, my lord. The Marches are the Reach’s fords too.”

“Don’t you teach me my title, boy.”

“I’d not dream of it. I only say that Lord Tarly is not asking for your good will. He is asking because the defence of the Reach is every lord’s task and every lord’s honour, and there are lords on the Mander who think it is Lord Tarly’s alone.” He looked round the hall, not hurrying, at the spears and the horns and the painted centaur. “The streams in the Marches are as dry as yours. Dorne has the same sun. When a Dornishman’s goats die, he looks north, and my grandfather does not like how quiet it has been.”

It was not what Orryn had expected from him. He had expected the jokes. It was said so flatly, and so much as if the speaker had already lost the argument and meant to put the thing down anyway, that for a moment nobody in the hall had anything to say.

“Grassfield sent no spears either, Ser Denys,” said Alaric, “if that is what you are measuring.”

“So I heard. Ser Olyvar offered you thirty, and you told him the Reach owes Highgarden spears when Lord Leo calls.” Denys looked at him. “Lord Leo has not called a man to arms since Redgrass, my lord. He sends a pardon, and a cask of wine.”

“Then I shall go when he does.”

“And meanwhile the Marches go on being quiet.”

“I stood in Little Ford’s barn two days ago,” said Alaric. He said it slowly. “Forty of your horses ate what that village would have eaten until the rains, in two nights. Ser Olyvar paid for it with a purse. It was not that he did not pay, ser. It was that there was nothing for the coin to buy.”

“So the Marches are to be defended with wells.”

“The Marches are to be defended with men who are still alive,” said Lady Brenna. “My husband’s town will eat its own hay and what it can hold of Garth’s grain before it feeds a single horse of my brother’s. That is not a discourtesy. That is the dry.”

“It is, my lady.” Denys turned his head, and now there was something in his face that was not flat. “And I know whose men you are. I thought it was Lord Leo’s table I was coming to. I see it is Ser Garth’s.”

Armond said, “What?”

“I have heard it in every hall between here and Highgarden, my lord. A hard dry year wants order, and order wants Garth. Ser Garth wants every cart written down and every well and every spear, and a justiciar at Highgarden to say who is right when the writing fails. A tidy road. I should like to ask Lord Rowan how he likes it.”

“What has Rowan to do with it?”

“Ask Lord Meadows. He was on the bench.” Denys did not look at Alaric. He looked at Armond, with the same easy face. “Lord Osmund Leygood sat in Highgarden’s lesser hall two days since and spoke the law over a knight and a widow, both Rowan’s own vassals, as though they were poachers taken in the king’s wood. Rowan’s court should have had them. Nobody asked Goldengrove. Ser Garth’s justiciar ruled on Rowan’s wells in Rowan’s absence, and it was the fairest judgment in the Reach.” He smiled. “Fair as a coin. They talk of nothing else in Olyvar’s yard.”

Nobody moved. At the far end of the hall Ser Cleyton had discovered that he was wanted elsewhere, and had taken his cloak with him.

“I’d sooner follow Ser Moryn, my lord,” said Denys Tarly gently. “As Lord Alaric has done. Moryn at least leaves a lord his own court.”

The silence went on a long time. Orryn heard a servant set down a dish in the passage, and the dish rang once on the stone. He heard the heat in the room, which had a sound like a held breath. His stomach had dropped very quietly, as it does in a dream where one is falling a very little way.

“He did what?” said Armond.

He had not looked at Denys. He was looking down the board, at Alaric, and his face had gone from red to something more dangerous and more tired.

“It is so,” said Alaric. “I was there. I sat on the bench.”

“You told me it was fair.”

“It was fair.”

“You sat there, and he spoke the law over Rowan’s men, and you came to my table and said fair.”

“The ruling was fair. I did not say that it was right.”

“A man’s own men are his to judge.” Armond’s hand lay flat on the board, as Lord Osmund’s had lain on his, but it was a much bigger hand, with the old scar white across the back of the wrist. “His wells. His widows. His knights who’ve lost their heads. That’s what it is to be a lord. Else what are we? Fellows with banners who pay for the shearing. I’ve a clerk on my bridge who marks Rowan’s papers three times, because Highgarden has a new way of writing grants. I thought, well, Garth is no fool. A hard year. Order. And all the while he’s had a man in my wife’s father’s hall taking a Rowan well out of Rowan’s hands.”

“My father’s hall,” said Lady Brenna.

She had gone white about the mouth. It was the only thing about her that had changed; she had not moved, and the smile stayed where it was, so that her face seemed to be two faces, one on top of the other.

“In my father’s name. In my father’s hall. With Rowan not asked.”

“Ser Tom named Lord Rowan,” said Alaric quietly. “The bailiff named Ser Moryn. Neither of them named your father.”

“No. They would not.”

“Brenna—”

“I said at this table that a lord who counts is a mercy.” She spoke to nobody. “I said it of Garth, and I meant it. I did not mean that he might count another man’s people and call it mercy. I think I was wrong, or half wrong. A man may count what is his. He may not count what belongs to another and ask to be thanked.”

“Then it’s done,” said Armond. “No more of Garth’s wax at my bridge. No more of his clerks. If his officers want my toll, they’ll find it where my father left it.”

“Is that an answer to my ten, my lord?” said Denys.

“It is not.”

Orryn found that he was thinking of an old man in a stable, and a paper signed unread. He had said a few honest words once, to a lord who did not seem to be listening, and they had gone out into the Reach and come back as a clerk’s three marks and a tollman’s silver and this. He could not say which part was his.

“Ser Denys,” said Alaric.

“My lord.”

“You have said two things of me, and I shall answer both. The first is that I sat on that bench and said nothing. That is true. I thought the judgment fair, and I thought the court was wrong, and I said so that day, to my squire, in four words. Mark who they named.” He did not look at Orryn, but Orryn said, “Yes, my lord,” before he knew he was going to.

“And the second?”

“The second is that I follow Ser Moryn. My son is in Ser Moryn’s household because there was a place there and a master who would teach him to sit a horse. I refused Ser Olyvar for him when he asked. I said at Highgarden that I would sooner have a fair judge than a kind lord. I did not say I would sooner have a judge than a lord. I will go to Lord Osmund’s hall when I ask for it. I will not go because someone has asked on my behalf.”

“Then whose man are you?”

“My own. It is a small side.”

“It has no spears.”

“It has Grassfield.” Alaric’s voice did not change. “I shall send my spears when Lord Leo calls, and not before, and I shall thank Lord Tarly for asking. I am sorry that is not what you rode a day and a night to hear.”

Denys Tarly laughed. It was short and rough, and the first honest sound he had made since he came in, and it seemed to take him a little by surprise.

“No,” he said. “It’s not.” He rubbed the back of his neck, and for a moment looked his twenty years and no more. “It’s better than what I’d have said, my lord. And I have not been charitable to the ones I came to ask.”

“Ser Denys,” said Lady Brenna.

She had turned in her chair. The smile had moved a very little, and her voice was perfectly ordinary. “You are my brother’s man and you have ridden half the night. You will sup with us, and your sergeant. Your men shall have the meadow below the east wall and bread from my kitchen, and the horses water at the castle well, and—”

“You’ll give them nothing, Brenna.”

Armond did not shout. That was what Orryn would remember, afterward. He said it as he had said when I’ve had my cheese, in the voice a man uses to a dog that has jumped on the table, and he did not look at her while he said it.

“Armond, he is my brother’s—”

“I know whose he is. I know whose wife you are, too, and it’s neither your brother’s nor your father’s nor Garth’s. This is my hall.” He picked up his cup. “You were singing Garth’s praises at my table since supper. Now you’d feed his brother’s horses out of my kitchen because your heart’s soft for a wine cask. You’ll hold your tongue, madam. You have said enough for one night, and half of it was your brother’s.”

For a moment Orryn thought she would speak. She sat quite straight, with her hands folded, and the pinned smile did not move at all.

“As my lord husband pleases,” said Lady Brenna.

Nobody looked at her. Ser Bertram studied his bread. At the lower board Joffrey had put down his cup some time ago, and he had not touched it since.

“Ser Denys,” said Armond, to the room. “You’ll have a bed under my roof, because I’ll not have it said that Bitterbridge turned a Tarly into the road. Your men may sleep on the river meadow outside the wall, on what they brought. They’ll have no hay and no bread from me. At first light I’ll open my gate for one rider. Not thirty. And not across my bridge.”

“Then I thank you for the bed, my lord,” said Denys, “and I’ll leave you your hay.” He bowed shortly, and turned, and the grey sergeant turned with him, and they went out through the passage. Orryn heard the sergeant say something low, and Denys’s voice answer, rough and short. He could not hear the words.

The hall began to breathe again. A servant came forward to clear the cheese. Armond poured himself a cup of wine, spilling a little, and did not seem to notice.

“Lord Alaric,” said Lady Brenna.

She had risen. She did not look at her husband; she looked at the shuttered windows and the dark river somewhere beyond them. “Will you walk with me on the wall? I should like some air.”

Alaric looked at Armond, who was pouring a second cup and did not look at either of them.

“Gladly, my lady.”

They went out together, not close, not touching, a slight woman in a dry-sage gown and a tall man with dust still in the creases of his coat, and the door closed. Orryn had stood up without noticing. His legs ached, and he was hungry, and the mutton on the board had gone cold and he could not have eaten it.

“Come up,” said Joffrey, beside him.

He had not stood up. He was sitting with both hands flat on the table, looking at them, and when he raised his head his face was perfectly pale and perfectly steady, and his voice was the dry light voice he used for every other thing.

“I’ll play you the end of the tune. You’ve waited long enough.”

The gallery was as he remembered it, a long cool passage with a bench in every window, and the smell of river coming up through the open shutters. It was a different smell now. There was more mud in it and less water, and under that, faintly, something that had begun to turn.

Joffrey sat on the bench with the lute across his knee and did not tune it. He looked at the strings. The shutter beside him framed a long dark piece of the Mander, with the two sandbars paler than the water, and below the wall, on the river meadow, the first of the riders’ fires.

“Well,” he said. “That was my father.”

Orryn said nothing. He had meant to say something kind and had found, when it came to it, that every kind thing he could think of was the sort that made a bad hour last.

“Don’t,” said Joffrey. “Mother says you’re never to say anything kind afterward. It only makes it a story. Tell me about Highgarden.”

“I did. I said it was big.”

“Tell me the rest. Who you met, and what you thought of them, and don’t be nice about it. I’ve had nice all night.” He turned a peg a quarter-turn, and turned it back. “Did you meet Harlen?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“He’s kind.”

“That’s not what I asked. Is he still a shy pushover?”

Orryn thought about the gallery at Highgarden, the cups, the dice, Harlen standing up and sitting down and standing up again because he could not bear a boy to be left on his feet. He thought of Lord Leo in the stables, speaking of a grandson who left a room rather than win an argument.

“He’s weak,” he said. “Lord Leo said soft. He said it as though he was fond of it. But he said it.”

“He’d apologise to a sword for being in the way of it. Grandfather says soft of anyone he can’t flatter into obeying.” Joffrey almost smiled. “Mother says a nice lord is a lamb with a crown. The Reach finds out in a year and eats him in two. They’d rather have a wolf who’s bad at it than a lamb who’s good.” He played a single dry note. “What did he do?”

“He lost three stags to Hugh Redwyne at dice, because he didn’t want to say no. Then he lost more to Gyles, and said it was enough, and Gyles put the cup in front of him again and he threw.”

“Of course he did.”

“He was kind to me.”

“So’s a spaniel.” But Joffrey said it without much heat, and put the lute down on the bench. “He gets a chain, and a horse that doesn’t throw him, and a Rowan or a Blackbar to marry, whichever they decide, and he sits there with clean nails and lets Hugh Redwyne rob him. If it were me I’d have sold the chain for a better horse.” He considered. “I’d have sold the chain for a lute.”

“Will said he was a good friend.”

“A good friend is somebody you can borrow from.” He picked at a seam of his sleeve. “Hugh Redwyne’s a wine-sack with his father’s chin and nothing behind it. I’ve met him twice. And Gyles Peake’s my cousin, did you know? Grandmother’s brother’s boy. He’s been kept at Highgarden since he was ten, and he looks at everyone as though they had his castle in their pocket. That’s about all a Peake has, now. A tongue.” He shrugged. “I’m told I’ve the Peake nose.”

“You’ve a long nose.”

“I said I’d been told.” But he looked pleased.

“They want to marry him to Roslin Rowan,” said Orryn, “or Meredyth Blackbar. Your mother said so at supper, when we came before.”

“I know. They’ve been at it since before you were born. Which?” Joffrey did not wait for an answer. “Lord Rowan comes through here twice a year with a hundred men and a daughter who has never said thank you in my hearing. Roslin. She looked at our hall once as though she were pricing it. The Blackbar girl I’ve never met. Mother says Redwyne pretty and Hightower proud, which is two ways of saying she knows what she’s worth.”

“Your mother knows a great deal.”

“My mother knows everything and says it to the wrong people.” He glanced toward the window, and away. “It won’t matter, you know. If Rowan and Redwyne claw at each other over Harlen till somebody’s eye comes out. Highgarden has cousins the way a hare has fleas. Tyrell, Peake, Oakheart, Redwyne. Somebody will have a girl to spare. They’ll find him a third and a fourth before they ever let one of the girls choose.”

“Is that what you’d do?”

“I’d marry him to whoever paid for the wedding.” He said it lightly, and Orryn did not think he was altogether joking.

There was a step on the stair.

It was not a servant’s step; it was heavier and in no hurry. Orryn’s back had gone straight before he knew it. Joffrey’s face changed in a way he could not read, and then the door at the end of the passage was pushed open with a boot, and Ser Denys Tarly stood in it with a jug in one hand and two cups hooked on the fingers of the other.

“Is that the noise that’s been keeping my sergeant from his rest?”

“It’s the noise that’s been keeping me from mine,” said Joffrey.

“Then we are well matched.” He looked at the lute. “I thought a boy had been strangled. It was only you not playing.” He came down the gallery, set the jug on the sill, and sat beside it with one boot on the bench and his back against the stone. “Olyvar’s red. Your lady mother’s been told to hold her tongue, and I had not heard that it extended to wine.”

It was a hard thing to say, and Joffrey took it quite calmly. “She’ll hold it about the wine.”

“I thought she might.”

He poured three cups, without being asked, and handed them round. Orryn took his. It was sweet and strong, and tasted like the colour red. Beside him, Joffrey shifted, and the bench was narrow, and a shoulder rested against his own. He did not move away. He was very careful not to think about it.

“I was going to speak to you in the hall,” Denys said to him. “After. And then your father said what he said, and I thought, I’ll be the last man in this castle whose talk he wants.” He drank. “You’re the one with the nose.”

“I’ve been told.”

“We were speaking of Harlen,” said Orryn. “Before you came.”

“Were you? Everyone does.” Denys seemed relieved by it. “Harlen Tyrell. If you put a crown on that boy he’d ask the crown’s pardon. I saw him say sorry to a door once, and it was shut at the time. He’s no bad lad. I’d lend him money and never ask for it back. I’d not trust him to hold a piss-pot, let alone a gate.”

“You said so in front of him,” said Orryn. “In the gallery. With the dice.”

“I said he’d been kicked by a horse. It’s different.” But the corner of his mouth moved. “Hugh Redwyne is a wine-sack who’d piss in your boot and ask what you’d done to it. Will Risley is the best of that company, and he knows it, which is nearly as bad. Gyles Peake looks at me as if I took his father’s castles with my own hands, and I was eight when it happened. I said what I said at the dice because it was funny, and I’d say it again, and it is why he will cut my throat someday if he can find me in a dark corridor with a knife.”

“You could stop,” said Joffrey.

“I could. I don’t believe I will.”

“Matthos?” said Orryn. He had not meant to ask.

“Your lord’s boy? Loud.” Denys drank. “He’ll be a good one if somebody hits him a few times.”

“He’s my nephew,” said Joffrey.

“Is he?” Denys looked at him, and Orryn saw him work it out. “Aye, so he would be. Jeyne’s boy. He’s your sister’s son.” He laughed. “That’s a thing. A nephew a year younger than his uncle.”

“By a year. He calls me uncle when he wants something.”

“Then he’s cleverer than he looks.”

They laughed, the three of them, and it was like a stone being lifted off the chest. Orryn found he was laughing with his whole face and could not stop. When he looked round, Joffrey was watching him, rather than Denys, with an odd steady look, and he took the cup out of Orryn’s hand without asking and drank from it, and gave it back.

“Which of the girls?” said Denys. “You were on it when I came in. Harlen’s brides.”

“Rowan or Blackbar.”

“Roslin Rowan’d eat him. Meredyth Blackbar would let him think she hadn’t.” Denys put the back of his head against the wall. “If he lets either one go, I’ll put in for her.”

“You?” said Joffrey.

“Why not? A Tarly younger son’s son has nothing but a sword. A Rowan field or a Blackbar fleet would suit it.” He grinned. “Lord Rowan would say that I am a nobody with thirty borrowed men. And I would show him what a man looks like who has been shot at on the Marches. Harlen’s never drawn on anything that bled.”

“Have you?”

“Twice.”

“And?”

“One was a goat thief.”

“And the other?”

“Was not a goat.” He looked at his cup. It was a short answer, and Orryn did not ask for another.

“I’ll tell you what it is,” said Denys, after a time. “My grandfather gave me thirty men because he’s no use for me at Horn Hill. Olyvar gave me thirty men because he’s no use for me at Highgarden. My father has a chair at my uncle’s table and a sword he doesn’t wear, and I have a horse. If I ride home with no spears, I ride home as nothing.” He did not say it in any particular voice. “I rode a day and a night to be told no by a man with a painted horse on his wall.”

“He’s my father,” said Joffrey.

“He was right. That’s the devil of it.” Denys shrugged. “I’d have done the same. I’d not have done it to my wife in front of a stranger.”

Joffrey said nothing. He rubbed his thumb along the neck of the lute.

“My grandmother was a Peake, you know,” he said suddenly.

“I know. I dined with her once. She counted my spoons.”

“She counts everything. She counted the Tarlys’ castles.”

Denys laughed so hard he had to wipe his eye with the heel of his hand. It was the same laugh as in the hall, short and rough and surprised, and Orryn laughed too, helplessly, without knowing what at. When it stopped he saw that Joffrey was no longer looking at Denys at all. He was looking at Orryn, with his lips pressed together, and then he looked away and said in a flat voice, “Tell him what you told me. About the yard.”

“What did I tell you?”

“That you don’t like it much.”

“I said I didn’t mind it.”

“You said not much. I was there.”

“A squire who doesn’t care for the yard,” said Denys. “You’ll go far. I don’t, half the mornings. I’m good at it, which is the worse trouble.”

They finished the jug. In the window beside them the Mander went on being dark and low, and the fires burned on the meadow, one beside another, and Orryn found that he had counted them without meaning to.

“Eleven fires,” he said. “And thirty-one horses on the line.”

“Thirty and the sergeant’s mule,” said Denys.

“Is that the wall?” said Joffrey.

It was. Beyond the bend of the gallery’s window, where the curtain wall ran down to the river, two figures were walking slowly along the parapet in the light of a single torch. One was slight and one was tall. They were far enough that nothing came across but the shape of it: the woman speaking, a good deal, with her hands, and the man with his head bent, listening. They did not touch. When they reached the tower at the end they turned and came back, and the woman went on speaking.

“She talks to him like a septon,” said Joffrey.

“She talks to him because he didn’t say your uncle was a fool,” said Denys.

“He did say he was pleasant.”

“That’s worse. He meant it.”

Nobody laughed. They watched the two figures to the corner of the wall, where the torch dipped out of sight.

“Hollis will have my head if I’m not on the meadow by the third watch.” Denys got to his feet and gathered the cups. He stood for a moment looking down at the two of them on the bench, the thin boy with the lute and the other with his hands between his knees. “Your lady mother’s a harder woman than she looks, Caswell. I’d watch how you take after her.” He looked at Orryn. “Sleep, Grandison. Your lord’s a better man than I made him out to be in there.”

He went out, and his steps went away down the stair, heavy and unhurried, and the door at the bottom closed.

For some time neither of them spoke. Joffrey set the lute on his knee and turned the pegs, one by one, quite carefully. Then he began to play. It was the long tune, the one he had not finished, the one that began as a wedding song and went somewhere else. Orryn sat with his back against the stone and his boots on the bench and let it go on. He had been going to say that he was tired, and that his lord would want him, and that he ought to go.

It was not so long a tune after all. At the end it came to a note that Joffrey held a little too long, as though he did not wish to be the one to stop.

“You stayed,” he said.

“I said I would.”

“People say that.” He looked at the strings. “Go on. Go to your lord. I’ll be all right.”

Orryn went. At the head of the stair he looked back, and Joffrey had not moved, and the lute lay quiet on his knee, and beyond him the river lay black and low between its two pale bars of sand.

Lord Armond Caswell woke the whole east range at first light by walking down it with his boots on.

“Up,” he said, at Alaric’s door, and then, rather louder, at the door of the little room where Orryn lay with his mouth tasting of Olyvar’s red. “All of you. I’m going to look at my river.”

It was not an invitation, and nobody treated it as one. Alaric came out in his shirt with his hair standing up on one side and did not remark on it. Ser Bertram, asked, found a reason to stay and see to the horses. Joffrey was fetched from wherever he slept by a groom and arrived in the courtyard grey-faced and unwashed, with a cloak thrown over his nightshirt, and his father looked at him once and said, “A lord’s heir ought to know his own fords,” and that was all that was said on the matter.

They rode out of the postern by the east wall, four horses in the thin cool light, and down a track of baked clay to the river. Orryn rode Rook and kept a length behind. Nobody had told him to come. Nobody had told him not to.

The ford lay half a mile below the town, where the Mander broadened and ran shallow over a bar of gravel, in three braided threads that had once been a single sheet of water. Orryn had seen it on his first day, from the bridge, as a little darker line across the brown. Now the threads were so thin that he could see every stone on the bottom, and the horses went down into the first of them with the water not quite to their fetlocks. The second was a little deeper. The third was only a wet place.

The river meadow was empty.

He had been looking for it since they cleared the wall, and he had not wanted to say so. The grass was trampled flat in a great irregular oblong, with the black rings of eleven fires and a litter of dung, and a long straight line of beaten earth where the picket rope had been. There was nothing else. There was not a man, not a horse, not a mule. Down at the water’s edge the mud was cut up by the hooves of thirty-one animals who had gone in one after another, and the tracks went out into the first thread and the second and the third, and up the far bank in a broad dark scar, and over the lip.

At the top of the far bank, small and clear against the pale sky, the last of them were riding away. Orryn could see the green banner. He could see a stocky shape on a mule, and a tall one in a dark coat, who had turned in his saddle at the very edge of the bank and was looking back across the water.

Armond did not move. He sat his big horse at the water’s edge with his hands on the pommel, breathing through his nose, and his face did not change.

“They’ve gone across,” he said. “At night. I’d have sworn they’d wait for the gate.”

“They did not need the gate,” said Alaric.

“No.” Armond looked at the water for a long moment. “No, they didn’t.”

The tall rider lifted a hand. It might have been to Armond, and it might have been to the town, or to the ford. Armond did not return it. Joffrey, on his other side, kept both his hands on his reins and looked straight ahead. Orryn found that his own right hand had come up, and he held it there, for no reason, and then put it down. When he glanced aside Joffrey was looking at him, and then at the far bank, and then at nothing.

“Horses cross,” said Armond.

He said it to the river. “A man on a horse can go over where he pleases, and I can’t stop him. I never could. A cart of grain’s another matter. Garth’s carts are ten to a string and heavy as sin, and they’ll not go through that.” He pointed with his chin at the third thread. “Not yet. They’ll want my bridge, and they’ll ask for it, and I’ll have the asking of the price.” He turned his head to Alaric. “It’ll be a ditch by the new moon. And then what am I? A man with a bridge over a field.”

“A man with a bridge,” said Alaric.

“Don’t be wise at me, lad.” But he did not say it unkindly. He put his hand into his belt and drew out a small purse, tied with a thong, and weighed it in his palm. “Rudd gave me this. At the gate. Said the Tarly boy left it with the warden before the third watch, and said it was for the grass.”

“How much?”

“Eight stags.” He laughed, once, harshly. “Eight silver stags for a meadow of dust. In a dry year. Is that courtesy or insult?”

“Yes,” said Alaric.

Armond looked at him, and then, unwillingly, the corner of his big red mouth began to move. He put the purse away.

“Don’t tell Jeyne how I spoke at table.”

“I shall tell her you were right about the hay.”

“Tell her the rest, too, if she asks. She will.” Armond turned his horse. “Come. I’ve a hall to sit in and a wife to see, and I’d as soon be done with one of them before I’m at the other.”

Nobody said anything on the way back. The sun came up over the willows while they climbed from the river. Orryn rode with clay dust on his boots, thinking of nothing he could name, and tried to count the stones in the track, and lost the number at forty.

*

They left after breakfast, which was eaten without conversation, and there was dust on everything and a heat already in the courtyard that promised worse. The groom had the sumpter loaded. Ser Bertram stood at the stirrup of his horse with the two men, looking as if he would give a good deal to be on the road. Joffrey did not come down.

Lady Brenna was waiting by the gate.

She wore a clean gown, grey-green, with the rose at the sleeve, and her sandy hair was drawn back from her temples very neatly. The smile was in its place. She stood with her hands folded in front of her, and she looked at Armond as he rode in, once, and he did not look at her, and she looked at Alaric.

“My lord,” she said. “You have been very patient.”

“My lady.” He had dismounted. He stood holding the bay’s rein, and did not say anything else.

Orryn, behind him, stood at Rook’s head and looked at the dog asleep under its bench. It was the same old hound as before. It had not moved in the night. He could feel Lady Brenna’s attention go past him, and come back, and he kept his eyes on the dog.

“Tell Jeyne—” she said.

She stopped.

Alaric waited. The sun was in the courtyard now, hard and yellow on the wall, and a groom somewhere was whistling, tunelessly, and a cart went out of the gate with its wheels grinding. Lady Brenna looked past him at the arch, beyond which the road ran down toward the bridge. Her lips were a little parted. Whatever she had meant to say seemed a long way back in her, and she did not reach for it.

“I will tell her.”

“Yes.” She inclined her head. “Thank you, Lord Alaric. Master Grandison. Safe road.”

She went in. She did not look back, and Armond did not look after her.

They rode out under the gate with the centaur drawing its bow above them. High up, in the window of the gallery, Orryn saw a thin pale figure leaning out with something across his arm, and it lifted a hand. He lifted his own. Then the arch was behind them, and the road ran down through the market, past the awnings and the lean clerk with nothing to count, and onto the bridge.

The stones rang under the horses’ hooves. Orryn looked over the parapet. The Mander lay below him in the white light, thin and brown, with the two sandbars standing out of it, and the old waterline on the piers a hand’s breadth higher than it had been yesterday. He could see the bottom. He could see the ribs of a sunken boat, and a drowned cart-wheel, and the long pale shapes of things that had been under the water since before he was born.

He had never seen so much of a river at once.

He kept looking until the bridge was behind him.
