Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

Wick, the kitchen lad, was on the wall again, and this time he had been told to say it quietly.

He did not manage it. “Five riders,” he informed the yard, in a whisper that carried to the stables, “and a banner, orange, with a sun on it—a white sun—” and Ser Wyman Latham, the master of horse, crossing below with a halter over his shoulder, looked up and said nothing so patiently that the boy stopped.

Orryn had been sent to the gate because Lord Alaric wanted a squire in the yard, and Baldric could not be found, and Perkin could not be trusted to stand still. He stood in the white light with his hands clasped before him, as Ser Lucan had taught him to stand for guests, and watched the dust come up the south road. It hung a long time over the road before the riders were in it. Everything hung a long time these days: the dust, the smoke from the cookhouse, the heat itself, which no longer moved from one hour to the next but simply lay on the keep like a hand.

The man in front rode a tall grey with one white sock and sat it as if the horse had been his idea. Orryn took the bridle as he came through the gate, and found himself looking up at a face he had last seen across a yard with a blunted sword in its hand.

“Master Grandison,” said Ser Androw Ashford. “You still have the dun?”

“Yes, ser.”

“Then he is a better horse than he looks, or you are a better squire than I was told. I shall not ask which.” He swung down. He was thirty, the eldest son and heir of Ashford on the Cockleswent, which was a lordly seat of the Reach a week’s ride to the south and west, and for two moons now he had been the husband of Lady Wylla Merryweather, who had been Alyn’s sister before she was anything else to him. His mother was a Caron of Nightsong, a Stormlander born, who had ridden beside Lord Alaric at the hunt and told him a few useful things about the Redwynes; his father’s sister was Lady Melessa, wife of Ser Moryn Tyrell, Lord Leo’s second son. He wore a brown riding coat with a week of dust in its seams and a cloak the colour of his house’s field, orange gone dull at the folds, with the white sun above its chevron stitched small at the shoulder. He was long in the arm and easy in the way he stood, his weight a little back on his heels, as though the ground had asked him to wait. Orryn had watched him turn Ser Lucan’s defence inside out in this same yard without once appearing to hurry, and had been trying to learn the trick of it ever since, without any success. “Is your lord within?”

“He is coming, ser.”

He was. Lord Alaric came down the steps two at a time with his sleeves rolled, and behind him, one hand on the rail and the other under the swell of her belly, came Lady Jeyne, who did not hurry. Orryn knew by now that she did not hurry for anyone. It was a thing she did with the whole of herself, the way other women did needlework.

“Androw,” said Alaric, and took his hand, and then his arm, and then seemed to notice the dust on it. “Seven hells, you look like a miller. Ser Everard will be glad of you. He has had nobody to hit but boys for a moon.”

“I have come to ask you something, and I would have come without being sent.” Ser Androw smiled and turned and bowed over Lady Jeyne’s hand. “My lady. Lady Ashford bids me say that she thinks very kindly of Grassfield, and most of all of the mint in the water.”

“It is a dry year,” said Lady Jeyne. “We put mint in everything.”

“So she says.”

“She is kind to remember it.” Her smile was perfectly pleasant. She had asked for Wylla Merryweather for her own son, Orryn remembered, standing very still with the grey’s bridle in his fist; she had been put off for a moon and then refused, and nobody had said so aloud, and now she was smiling at the man who had got the girl. “Is she well? Your lady?”

“Very well, my lady. She bade me tell Lady Leonette that she has the pattern for the gown and has not forgotten it.”

“Leonette is a wife now, and at Tumbleton.”

“Then I shall tell my wife to send it there.”

“Yes,” said Lady Jeyne. “That would be a courtesy.” She inclined her head to him exactly as far as was due, and went up the steps one at a time with her hand on the rail. Orryn watched her go, and could not have said whether he had seen a woman forget a thing or a woman write it in her book.

*

They sat in the solar with the shutters half drawn and a wet sheet hung over the sill, which had been dry again before Orryn was done carrying the wine. It was good wine, watered, in the good cups. He stood against the wall with the jug and tried to be furniture.

For the first while there was nothing in it. Ser Androw said the Cockleswent was so low at Ashford that a child could cross at the bridge without wetting his ankles, and that his father found this a personal insult, since the bridge was the best thing Ashford owned. Alaric said the lower well had been giving a bucket and a half before the mud settled, and Ser Androw said theirs gave one, and they agreed that it was a fine thing to be lords in a dry year, and that they would not be so much longer. Ser Androw asked after the hunting. Alaric said the hawks were eating better than the men, and they sat quiet a moment.

Ser Androw drank half his cup and set it down. “I shall ask it plainly, my lord, because I think you will prefer it. What did Grassfield put in writing, when Ser Garth’s man came?”

“Nothing I would not say to your face.” Alaric had taken the window seat with one boot drawn up under him, which he did when he did not mean to be hurried. “Ser Robyn Rhysling wrote my answer in his own book, in front of me, and read it back, and I let it stand. He had a clerk with him. The clerk stood at my steward’s elbow and counted every sack in my granary, and wrote his figure in his own hand, and took it away with him. I told him that Grassy Vale’s grain stays in Grassy Vale. I told him that when Grassy Vale is fed, and there is more, I will sell it at a fair price to any man with honest silver, and he might tell Ser Garth to send a cart.” He turned the cup in his fingers. “That is all. I sent no letter. I did not want one to read aloud at somebody’s table.”

“You let him count.”

“I let him count. I will not be called a liar over a few hundred sacks. If the number is what it is, and he has seen it, there is nothing left for him to ask me but the grain.”

“And he asked for the grain.”

“He asked for a tenth. I said no.”

“Ah.” Ser Androw laughed once, quietly, and it was not at Alaric. “It is very near what my father said, except for the clerk. Ser Humfrey Beesbury, brother to the Lord Beesbury, brought us Ser Garth’s ask, as Ser Robyn brought it to you, and my father heard it standing, and said that a lord’s word was a lord’s count. He wrote a figure himself. One number, for all of Ashford, and sealed it, and Beesbury carried it off in his saddlebag. No clerk went into our granaries, and no tenth goes to any depot. He told Beesbury that if Highgarden wanted Ashford’s barley it might send carts to the Cockleswent bridge and buy it like anyone else.”

“At what price?”

“A fair one,” said Ser Androw. “Ashford’s.”

Alaric’s mouth moved. Orryn remembered Lord Armond at table, saying that at Ashford they would charge a guest for everything but the air.

“Then we are agreed that the grain does not leave.”

“On that we are agreed.” Ser Androw turned his own cup. “On the clerk we are not. My father says that a man who opens one door will be asked to open the next, and the next, until there is nothing in his house a seneschal has not read. You say that a man who has nothing to hide may as well be counted. I have not decided which of you is the wiser, and I shall not decide until I see what is asked for next.”

“It will be asked for,” said Alaric. “And I am told I am to go to Goldengrove, if I mean to ask Lord Rowan for anything, and ask it myself, as a lord asks a lord.”

“Then go before the road is worse,” said Ser Androw. “He is a man who remembers how a thing is asked. My aunt’s husband says so, and my aunt says it more often.”

That was the first of it, and Orryn thought it was done. Then Ser Androw looked up from the cup, and at Orryn by the wall, with a kind of friendly weighing, as a man looks at a boy to see whether he will repeat things. Orryn kept his eyes on the plaster.

“Let him stay,” said Alaric. “He pours very quietly. He has not yet learned to do anything else.”

“Then I will tell you what I heard at my father’s table,” said Ser Androw, “and you may tell me what you make of it. It does not go in a letter. Ser Humfrey stayed two nights at Ashford. A big man, loud, fond of a story and a leg of mutton, and more honest than he looks; I would not play at tables with him, but I would trust him with a horse. On the second night a rider found him at supper. A Hightower man, in a grey coat with the white tower on the breast, off a lathered horse, who had been four days on the road behind him to say it. Ser Humfrey had him say it again at the board, for my father’s sake, because it pleased him. I was sitting across from him. I heard it with my own ears.”

“Tell it.”

“Lord Jon Hightower has sent grain to Horn Hill. As a gift to House Tarly. Not a loan to the Reach and not a tenth of anything; a gift, in carts, off Oldtown’s own granaries, with the white tower painted on the tilt of every one.” He drew breath. “Ser Matthew Mullendore escorts it. Of Uplands—a Hightower knight, in the old way; his house has held its lands of Oldtown for a very long time. He had been gone from the city three days when the rider left. Ser Humfrey says he is likely at Horn Hill by now, or in a ditch.”

“Mullendore,” said Alaric.

“You know him?”

“I have heard of him. He has a name for a good table.”

“He has.” The corner of Ser Androw’s mouth moved. “He is a big man who would sooner be at supper than on a road, and no better a soldier than he must be. I think that was the point. Lord Jon wants no brave deed on the way, my lord. He wants the carts to arrive. Nobody sets on a train with the Hightower banner at its head, whatever sort of man rides beneath it. You could not say as much of a Tarly banner on a Tarly cart.”

Orryn found that he had set the jug down on the sill, and picked it up again.

He did not know Ser Matthew Mullendore. He knew Ser Humfrey Beesbury only as a name out of the grizzled old knight’s mouth in the small hall, carried on the same errand to a different hold, and now the same name had turned up at a table a week away, with a rider at his elbow. It made the whole country smaller and more like a house. Lord Jon Hightower he knew as everyone in the Reach did: the lord of Oldtown, which was the greatest city of the Reach and the oldest, with the beacon tower that every ship on the Whispering Sound steered by, and which held of Highgarden and did not always remember it. Horn Hill was the Tarlys’ seat, in the south, nearer the Marches than any other great house of the Reach, and Denys Tarly’s grandfather ruled it. A rider had run four days to bring the tale, and a lord had made him tell it twice, and Ser Androw had sat across from him. Orryn could not see why anybody should have made it up. He did not know whether the carts had arrived. Neither, it seemed, did anyone.

And it was very good. He thought it plainly and at once, with the thirty-one measures of oats and the six saddled horses in his head. There was a lord with grain and a lord in want of it, a long way off, and the first had sent it to the second, and nobody had been asked to count a thing. It was the first piece of news in a long while that did not leave the taste of dust in his mouth.

“Ser Humfrey said one other thing,” Ser Androw went on, “and said it as though it were nothing. That Ser Garth’s letter about the burnings has gone to Lord Theomar Redwyne, and Lord Theomar means to lay it before the king’s council, and it is there now. Whether it has been read, and what the council makes of it, he did not know. He did not seem to think anyone did.”

“A council,” said Alaric slowly, like a man tasting. “Reading a letter, while Tarly’s people eat Hightower’s bread.”

“Both at once, I think. Both are good things.” Ser Androw shifted his weight. “Now I will tell you why I rode a week to say it, since you will have guessed. I rode to see your face. And then on to Longtable, to tell Wylla’s father I have come to no harm.”

“You have my face.”

“I have it.” He leaned forward, forearms on his knees. “Consider what Lord Jon did, my lord. He knew who was in want. He knew where to send it. He did not ask Tarly for a count, or a tenth, or a promise, or leave of Highgarden. He sent it under his own banner, so that every town between Oldtown and Horn Hill might see whose carts they were. Oldtown has never loved being told what to do by Highgarden, and this tells Highgarden what Oldtown does without being told. Now consider who is pleased. Not Ser Garth, who wrote to every lord in the Reach for a tenth and was told that he might buy it. But every lord who answered as you did. Ser Moryn’s friends, who are Rowan’s friends, who are mine, if I am honest. They can feed Horn Hill and keep their own stores shut. Nobody’s name in a seneschal’s book.” He sat back. “My father laughed at it, and then he did not. A lord who can do that is a lord we may all want to be on good terms with, and he knows it, and I think that is what the carts are for.”

“And Ser Garth is the one who asked,” said Alaric.

“Yes.”

“And Lord Jon is the one who gave.”

“I did not say it was kind, my lord. I said it was clever.”

Orryn had not thought of that. He saw, quite suddenly, the thing he had not seen. He had thought of the gift as something that had arrived. He had not thought of the man who sent it, or of what a man might hope to have for it, and he felt his ears go hot, because it was a plain thing, and Baldric would have thought of it before the sentence was finished.

Alaric did not look at him. He looked at the dull shine of the wine in his cup for some time.

“I am glad of it,” he said at last. “I want that said. Horn Hill has more mouths than bread, and so has half the Marches, and if Jon Hightower will feed them I would not undo it for any grudge of mine. I do not think he does it for love of Tarly. I think it may serve my turn too, if it means nobody comes to my granary with a clerk and a tenth. A man may take a useful thing from a man he has no reason to love.” He paused. “But I will ask what I have been asking myself since you began. Lord Jon had grain. Lord Jon has a friend in Horn Hill. What does the next lord in want do, who has no friend in Oldtown and no granary but his own?”

Ser Androw said nothing.

“I do not say it to quarrel.”

“No. I know.” The long hands had gone still on his knees. “I have not found an answer, my lord. I thought you might have one. Perhaps nobody has. Perhaps it is only that this year it is Tarly, and the next it is Rowan, and the year after somebody who has not had the sense to be friends with anyone.”

“Then he goes begging to Highgarden or to Oldtown,” said Alaric, “and pays for his bread whichever door he knocks at.” He drank at last. “I will give you supper, Androw, and a bed with a roof. And in the morning I will give you my greeting to carry to Longtable, which Lord Merryweather will have heard twice already from other mouths and will want to hear a third time in a plainer voice.”

“Lord Merryweather will want to hear whatever has been said in a plain voice,” said Ser Androw, “because it will be shorter.”

*

The Ashford men had taken their horses to the long trough, and the lower yard was full of the sound of it: a dozen throats working, the slow knock of hooves on flagstones, and the voices of grooms who had decided a guest’s horse would be watered before a Grassfield one. Orryn was sent to see that the grey had been rubbed down. It had. He found Baldric leaning against the trough with a rag in his hand, looking at nothing.

“You were in there,” said Baldric.

“I was pouring.”

“Then you heard it.” Baldric Haye, Ser Bertram’s squire, was sixteen, square and pale, and in the low light his face had the set, sleepless look Orryn had seen on the six riders under the beech. “It’s round the yard already. One of the Ashford grooms told Wick, and Wick told the cook, and the cook told me. Hightower’s feeding Tarly.”

“Grain,” said Orryn. “Carts of it. Lord Jon sent it to Horn Hill, and Ser Matthew Mullendore rides with it. Ser Androw had it from Ser Humfrey Beesbury, who had it from a Hightower rider, and nobody knows if it’s arrived.”

“I didn’t ask that.” Baldric wrung the rag between his fists. “Is it an army?”

“What?”

“Olyvar’s lot. They ride south with their banners and Denys Tarly out in front, and here’s Hightower sending carts to his grandfather. Is that them getting an army? Has somebody finally decided to send a proper one, and not boys?”

Orryn thought about it as honestly as he could, which took some time. “I don’t know,” he said. “Ser Androw never said so. He said grain. Nobody’s said spears.”

“Grain.” Baldric laughed, one short breath through his nose. “Grain doesn’t hold a pass.”

“No. But the men who hold it have to eat.”

“They do.” He had not meant to say it that way, Orryn thought; it came out quietly, and surprised them both. He looked at the rag. “I was thinking of Denys, you know. When the names went round, I mean. He can’t sit a horse, and I hated him a bit for going, and then I thought: he’ll be hungry. Somebody’ll have given him a bit of salt pork on the road and a ditch to drink from.” He shook his head. “Seven hells. It’s a thing to think about a man you can’t stand.”

“You don’t hate him.”

“Don’t tell me what I do.” But there was no heat in it.

The Ashford grooms had begun leading the horses off, and the noise of the trough thinned and went. Orryn found that he wanted very much to say something, and had not settled what, and that Baldric was waiting.

“I’m glad,” he said. “That somebody fed them. The ones who rode. I thought about it all the way across the yard. Ser Androw says it has a price, and Lord Alaric thinks so too, and I see it now that they have said it, but it’s grain, and they’re hungry, and it was sent. I am glad of that.”

“That’s all right to say.” Baldric looked at him. “That’s the easy one.”

“Is it?”

“It’s not the one you can’t say.”

Orryn opened his mouth, and the other thing was there, very plain, standing behind a chair in a hot hall with the sun coming through the shutters: that a lord had told six men to unsaddle and sleep in beds, and that he had been glad of it, and was glad of it still, and that Denys Tarly had gone and he had not. He knew the words. He had known them for days. They were not hard words. He could not get them past his teeth.

“I’m glad about the grain,” he said again.

Baldric waited a moment longer. Then he put the rag over his shoulder and did not ask twice.

“Come on,” he said. “Wyman wants the oats counted again, and it’s your turn. I told him you’d do it.”

It was dim in the long stable, and the stick was on its nail where Ser Wyman kept it. The number was lower than the last time. It was lower every time. Orryn counted it twice, with his lips moving, and the second time it came out the same.
