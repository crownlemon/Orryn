He began carefully.

"I will go on ahead," he told Wat, and Wat said "Aye, Master Orryn," as though it were a thing that had been agreed in some other year, and Pate said nothing and looked at the mule. Orryn turned the dun down the long slope with his back straight, his heels down, and his hands low and quiet on the reins, exactly as the master-at-arms had taught him in the yard at Grandview. A walk. A good, slow, dignified walk, such as a squire might use who has been sent for by a lord and means to arrive composed.

He rehearsed as he rode. My lord. I am Orryn, Steffon's son. I have come as you wrote. No, that was too stiff. My lord, I thank you for the place. That sounded like a beggar. Lord Alaric. He tried it with the weight on the first word, and then on the second, and found there was no way to say it that did not sound like a boy practising how to say it.

He straightened his collar. He tugged his sleeve down over the stained cuff. He tried to remember what he knew of the man at the end of this road, and found it came to very little: a lean figure in a plain dark coat at some wedding long ago, who had looked at the plate before he looked at the guests, and who had not smiled when the others did. Cold and exact. He wished suddenly that Maris had said something kinder. He wished, which was worse, that he had a hat.

The dun walked on, and the valley opened below him, and the long light lay gold across the stubble. A hedge ran straight down the hill on his left. On his right there was a pasture, and a wide pale track along its edge where cattle had worn the grass to dust, and the track ran on toward the keep as straight and smooth as a ribbon.

He looked at the track. The dun looked at the track.

"Only a trot," Orryn said.

The dun trotted. It was a good trot, long and level, and Orryn rose and fell with it, easy as breathing. A thrush burst out of the hedge. The wind came up the valley and filled his sleeves.

"A little more," he said, to nobody.

The dun, who had perhaps been waiting some time for this, put down his head and went.

It was like falling and not being afraid. The pasture flew past, the dust of the track rising behind them in a long gold plume, the hedge a green blur at his knee. Orryn crouched low over the horse's neck with the mane whipping his face and the wind taking the breath out of his mouth, and felt the great muscles gathering and flinging beneath him, and heard himself laugh, a high, ragged, ridiculous laugh that went out across the fields. He had not ridden like this since the spring. He had not ridden like this since before the letter came, and the packing, and the sums of what he would cost. A flock of sheep scattered at his coming; a dog ran after him a little way, barking, and gave it up. He stood in the stirrups. He shouted, a wordless shout, only to hear it. He would, he thought, have ridden straight through the wall of Grassfield Keep if the wall had been in the way.

There was a ditch. He saw it late, a dark line across the grass, and the dun saw it at the same moment and gathered himself without being asked. They went over it in a single long surge, and landed with a jolt that clacked his teeth together and nearly cost him a stirrup. Orryn clutched at the mane. He yelled again. The dun slowed, blowing, plainly pleased with himself.

"Oh, you are a good horse," Orryn gasped. "You are a very good horse."

The dun tossed his head.

It was only when he reined in, on the lip of the last rise, that he looked at himself.

His hair was full of grass seed and dust. One sleeve was torn at the elbow. His cloak had come half off his shoulder and hung by a single pin, and there was a great smear of green across one knee where the mane had been, and his face, when he put his hand to it, was hot and wet and grinning. He thought of the man at the end of the road. He thought of cold and exact. He took the grin off his face with both hands, as one removes a mask, and found it came back at once.

"Well," he said to the horse. "We shall have to make the best of it."

He spat on his palm and flattened his hair, which did nothing. He pinned the cloak. He found, in his pocket, a squashed pear that he had no memory of taking and ate it in three bites, core and all, because it seemed a poor idea to arrive hungry. Then he gathered the reins, put his heels down, and rode on at the sort of walk he had begun with, though not with quite the same face.

Grassfield rose to meet him, bigger than it had looked from the ridge. The curtain wall was grey and weathered and patched in two different stones, one darker than the other, as if the keep had once been hurt and had been very carefully mended. The gate stood open. Above it a man in a leather jerkin leaned on the wall-walk, watching him come.

"Who comes?" the man called down, not unkindly. He had the voice of someone who had asked the question ten thousand times and still hoped for a better answer.

"Orryn Grandison," Orryn said. His voice cracked on the last syllable. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Of Grandview. Squire to Lord Alaric, if he will have me. I was told to come by dusk."

The man looked at him: at the grass seed, the torn sleeve, the green on his knee. At the horse. Something moved in his weathered face, quickly, and was put away again.

"Aye," he said. "His lordship said. Go on in, young ser. The yard's on your left." And then, as Orryn passed beneath the arch, so quietly he might have meant it only for himself: "He's in a mood, mind. It's the grain."

The yard was cobbled, wide and busy, ringed by stables and a smithy and the long low front of the hall. Boys ran with buckets. A grey-haired woman was scolding a goose. Orryn drew rein by the well, and a stable-lad looked at the dun, and then at Orryn, with the careful blankness of a boy who has been taught to say nothing about a gentleman's horse, and took it by the bridle.

That was when he saw the man at the top of the steps.

He was lean, and he did not seem to be standing still even though he was. He had light brown hair cut short for work and hazel eyes that went along the yard as a finger goes along a line of figures, a row at a time. His coat was plain dark wool, without lace, with ink on the right cuff. Beside him a plump, pleasant-faced man in a good blue doublet, the steward, Orryn guessed, was talking and gesturing with a tally-stick, and the lean man was not looking at him at all. He was looking at a cart of grain sacks by the granary, and at a man beside it holding a paper, and he said something short and cold that made the man with the paper go white.

Orryn did not need to be told who it was.

He slid from the saddle. His knees nearly gave; he stood very straight to hide it, with the cloak pinned crookedly and his hands empty at his sides. The yard noise had not stopped, but it seemed to have moved a little away from him. Lord Alaric Meadows came down the steps one at a time, with the steward still talking at his elbow, and when he was perhaps four paces off, he stopped.

He looked at Orryn's face. He looked at the grass seed, the torn sleeve, the green smear on the knee, the pinned cloak. He looked, slowly, at the dun gelding being walked away across the cobbles, and at the saddle, which was a plain saddle, and at the blanket under it, which was not Grassfield's and not Grandview's.

"You are early," he said. His voice was level and dry and quite without heat. "The cart is not with you. And that," he said, without moving his eyes from the horse, "is not a horse I know. Whose is it?"
