Highgarden, 208 AC

Someone had watered the lower yard before dawn, and by the time Orryn came down the sand had gone dark and begun to smell like a cellar floor. It was the nearest thing to rain that Highgarden had seen since the dry began, and it would not last past the first sun on the wall. The squires stood about in the grey light like men at a graveside. A few had the look of mourners who meant to be sick behind the stables as soon as the thing was decently over.

Orryn had come because Lord Alaric had told him to, and he had put on the boots he had worn to the gallery because he could not find the others in the dark. His foot ached. He decided that this was only fair, since so did most of the rest of him.

Ser Luthor Risley walked the line with his short stick tucked beneath one arm and said nothing at all, which was worse than shouting. He was Will’s uncle; Orryn could see it now, in the long jaw, and in the way both of them looked at a boy as though they were measuring him for something and had not yet decided what. He stopped before Hugh Redwyne, whose face was the colour of tallow and who held his practice sword by the very end, as though it had been handed to him by an enemy. Ser Luthor looked at him for the space of three breaths. Then he moved on, and Hugh let out a sound like a bellows with a hole in it.

Gyles Peake whistled. He did it softly and without any tune, with great determination, and every time he did, Hugh closed his eyes. Matthos stood beside Will in the green of Ser Moryn’s household, which someone must have found for him after supper, for the sleeves were too long and he had turned them back twice. He was white about the mouth, and he was not talking. In Matthos that was a more serious symptom than any quantity of wine could account for.

Ser Harlen Tyrell stood at the rail with his arms folded, wearing the look of a man who had carried a message and been struck for it.

“I asked him,” he said, to nobody in particular, when Orryn drew near. “As properly as I knew how.”

“What did he say?”

“That a squire who can drink until the second watch can rise at the first. And that if I wished anyone excused I might apply to my lord grandfather, in writing.” He gave a small, rueful laugh. “I thought about it. For some time. I even had the first line.”

Orryn was set to work with Will, who hit him in the ribs twice in a manner that Will plainly thought restrained. After that he gave up thinking about anything.

They were let to drink at the second bucket. Hugh dipped the ladle, tasted, and said that the Arbor would not have given it to a horse.

“Is that how you speak of your friends?” Gyles asked, pleasantly. “I thought water was the one thing the Arbor never sent us.”

“It sends everything else. My father says that if the ships stopped for one moon the Reach would starve before the Marches had finished complaining about it.”

“And if Starpike stopped watching the Marches, Dorne would be drinking your wine by the next harvest, and paying less for it than we do.”

“Your father couldn’t stop Dorne drinking—”

“My father has one castle and a long memory,” said Gyles. “Yours has a hundred ships and a short one. Be thankful we are on the same side.”

“No army ever marched on wine,” Will said, to the bucket.

“Nor on talk,” said Ser Luthor, who had come up behind them without a sound, “and you have had enough of both. Shields.”

Orryn went back to Will and was hit in the ribs again. Over Will’s shoulder he could see Matthos, who had been given a shield too big for him and was doing his best to hide behind it. At the bucket he had said only one thing, low, to nobody, and Orryn had heard it. Ser Moryn says the Reach is held together by who remembers whom.

Orryn had thought at once of Lady Brenna at her table in Bitterbridge, writing in the margin of a letter. He will promise you four, and remember two. He wondered how many Matthos supposed it took.

*

By the time the sun cleared the stable roof Ser Luthor had let them go, and Orryn climbed to the guest court the long way, favouring the foot. He had been told to wake his lord an hour after the usual time, and he had been counting that hour against the squires’ yard, and it seemed to him that it must be well past.

Lord Alaric lay on his back with one arm across his eyes and the coverlet kicked to the floor, breathing like a man at the bottom of a well. The shutters were still closed. Orryn opened one a hand’s width, and the light fell across the bed.

“Is it dawn?”

“It’s long after, my lord.”

“Then the sun is a liar.” Alaric did not move the arm. “Is there water?”

There was, and Orryn brought it, and watched his lord drink the whole cup with the grim attention of a man swallowing a debt. Then he brought a second, and bread, and the blue doublet. Alaric looked at the bread as though it had said something about his mother.

“Lady Arlanna will have us in her solar in the time it takes to say a long grace,” Orryn said. “A page came while I was in the yard.”

“Of course she will.” Alaric sat up and held his head very carefully, the way one carries a full bucket. “Grandison. She will not ask you anything. Lady Arlanna does not ask squires things. She waits until they have told her, and they always do. If she looks at you, look at the floor. It is not discourtesy. It is how squires live to be knights.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“And if anyone should ask how I slept—”

“Well, my lord.”

“It is true,” Alaric said, “and it is all they want.”

He said nothing more about the night before, nor did he look at Orryn when he said this much. He stood and swayed once and let Orryn do up the hooks of the doublet, the awkward ones first, and Orryn did them as he had done a hundred such, and looked at nothing but the cloth. In the plain light of morning it was a very ordinary task. He could not now think what he had been afraid of.

*

Lady Arlanna kept her rooms above the herb gardens, in a range that Orryn would not have found without the page. They climbed a stair that turned twice upon itself and came out onto a gallery with an unglazed window, through which the smell of thyme and rue came up warm from the beds below. A woman in a grey wimple looked them over, said that her lady would see Lord Meadows, and opened the door without knocking.

The solar ran the whole width of the range, with windows along the western side thrown wide, so that the room smelled of dry earth. There was no fire. There was a long table with a lap desk upon it and a spill of letters, a high-backed chair with a green cushion, a lower chair beside it that nobody seemed to sit in, and at the far window a frame of embroidery at which three women worked with their heads bent, like a row of herons.

Orryn knew two of them. Lady Melessa Tyrell, Ser Moryn’s wife, looked up from her needle as they came in, and then at him, and kept looking, kindly and without any hurry, as a woman looks at a bill she means to pay in due course. The second, in grey, was Lady Meriel Vyrwel of Darkdell, who had danced with Lord Alaric for the better part of the night. She bent a little lower than the others over her work, and Orryn looked elsewhere. Lord Alaric did not look toward the window at all, with a thoroughness that was itself a kind of looking. The third woman was old and had a face like a walnut and might have been in the room since before it was built.

Lady Arlanna Tyrell sat in the high chair with a letter in her hand, and she did not lift her eyes from it until she had finished reading.

Orryn had time to look. He had seen her across a hall, at the length of a long table, when her attention was upon her servants; now he stood a dozen paces from the Lady of Highgarden and could see what the candle smoke had hidden. She was tall even seated, and thin, in the way of women who have been told for sixty years to sit up, and her shoulders had begun to bend regardless, so that she held herself like a candle in a crooked sconce. There were pearls at her throat and the green velvet upon her head, and the grey hair beneath it was dressed so tightly that it seemed to be holding the skin of her face in place. Her hands were the stillest he had ever seen on an old woman. They lay upon the page as though it might otherwise have got away. She had Ser Moryn’s eyes, or he had hers: brown and deep-set and so entirely without haste that Orryn felt each of his own faults come forward in turn to be looked over. She had been Arlanna Peake once. She had married Lord Leo Tyrell and borne him five children, and it seemed to Orryn that for forty years she must have ruled this house as a keel rules a ship, from underneath, without once being seen to.

“You are late, Lord Alaric.”

“A little, my lady. I beg your pardon.”

“My lord husband tells me you danced.” She laid the letter face down. “I am glad someone in this house did.”

“I regret it more than I did at midnight.”

“That is the correct proportion.” Her mouth did something that was not a smile, and might have been its ghost. “Lady Melessa. The barley water. Lord Alaric will want it more than he will want hippocras, and he is too well bred to say so.”

Lady Melessa was already rising. Alaric bent over the old woman’s hand and kissed it, and straightened more slowly than he had bent.

“And that is the Grandison boy.” She did not turn her head toward Orryn. “Let him sit. A squire who stands at a window is looking at the window.”

The old woman with the walnut face put a stool by the door without being told. Orryn sat, and was looked at once from head to foot, and was put away.

“You have given your son to Moryn.”

“Ser Moryn has taken him, my lady.”

“Moryn takes boys easily. He is kind to them, and it costs him nothing, and they remember it for the rest of their lives. It is the best investment in the Reach and it has never once been written in a book.” She straightened a corner of the letter. “How does the boy ride?”

“Well enough to learn. Not well enough to be told so.”

“Then Luthor will tell him. I am informed he bows too deeply.”

“He does.”

“He will be cured of it. Moryn cures everyone of something. It is how he gets them.” She seemed to settle upon the matter as a hen settles upon a nest. “Your lady is well?”

“Very well, my lady. She sends her—”

“Respects. Yes. She sent them to the women who write for me, a moon ago, in a very pretty hand. She writes to my women when she wishes me to hear something, and to nobody when she does not.” The brown eyes did not change. “You have been at Bitterbridge.”

“One night, my lady.”

“And my daughter was hard on your wife.”

“Lady Brenna was courteous to me.”

“To you. I did not say to you.”

There was a pause. In it Orryn heard the needles at the window, which had not stopped, and a bee somewhere among the thyme. Lady Arlanna looked at nothing in particular.

“I have told Brenna that it does not become her. It did not take. I sent that girl to Bitterbridge at fifteen, to a house that already had a daughter of twelve in it who believed the place was hers, and I am told they have not forgiven one another since, and neither of them did anything.” She said it with no more feeling than a woman reading a bill of lading. “Your wife is a proud woman, Lord Alaric. I have not known many who were not. I should not like it said at Highgarden that one of my daughters counted the cups in another’s household. And my daughter, you will have noticed, has not written to me at all. She wrote to three of my ladies, which is the same.”

Alaric said nothing. Orryn thought that for once it was not because he had nothing to say.

Lady Melessa came back with a cup of something pale and cool, and put it into Lord Alaric’s hand with a look that said she knew exactly what he would do with it, and went away again. Alaric drank. Some of the colour came back into his face.

“Lady Ashford writes that you hunted together.”

“She was kind enough to ride beside me, my lady.”

“She is never kind. She is exact, which is why I have liked her for thirty years.” The old hands moved a little upon the desk. “I taught her which fork to use when she came into the Reach. She was sixteen, from Nightsong, and thought a second fork was an accusation. She is a Caron, you know, and I am a Peake, and neither of us is permitted to forget whose sister we are. It makes for a close acquaintance.”

Orryn looked at the floor, because he had been told to, and because he had just understood what Lady Ashford meant at the edge of the hayfield when she said she had watched Highgarden for thirty years from a window in Ashford. He had taken it for a figure of speech. It was a window with a letter in it.

“She writes that you like clerks little, and Redwyne gold less.”

He knew the words. He had heard them with a falcon’s claws through a borrowed glove, in the last of the light, and they came back to him whole, with a strange cold feeling, as of a coin he had seen spent in one place turning up in another. Lord Alaric had said them to Lady Ashford with a squire riding behind. Lady Ashford had said them to this room.

“I said so, my lady.”

“I know. She does not invent. She has never needed to.” The eyes moved, at last, and rested upon Alaric the way a hand rests upon a table to learn whether it is level. “She did not write which of my sons you like better.”

“I have not been at Highgarden long enough to like any of them better.”

“Two days is long enough to like a Tyrell. Most men do it in one.”

“I find Ser Moryn courteous,” said Alaric, slowly. “Ser Garth clear. Ser Olyvar honest. I do not think those are the three words they would have chosen.”

“Courteous, clear, honest.” She tasted them. “You are very careful, Lord Alaric. A man who is that careful at this hour, with the head you have this morning, is either a great deal frightened or a great deal clever.”

“Frightened, my lady.”

“Then I am reassured. The clever ones are in my husband’s council.” She picked up a small silver knife from the desk and turned it over, though she did not seem to want it. “Olyvar offered for your boy in the yard, I am told, and you kept him for Moryn. I thought it well done. It told Olyvar one thing and Moryn another.”

“It told Ser Moryn that I had asked him first.”

“That is what I said.”

Lord Alaric turned the cup between his hands, and drank, and did not say whether it was what she had said.

“Then Moryn has a friend,” said Lady Arlanna.

“Lord Leo has a loyal bannerman, my lady, and Ser Moryn has a good squire.”

“I shall have that embroidered.” But she did not say it unkindly; and when Orryn risked a look he saw something at the edge of her mouth that might have been approval, or only an old woman’s pleasure in being bested in the form. “Everyone in this house is Lord Leo’s man. It is the first thing they tell you, before they tell you whose they are.”

“You have slept under two roofs this moon,” she said. “Longtable and Bitterbridge. I should like to know where they will stand.”

“Lord Merryweather is a friend, my lady. He will stand wherever the last man to speak to him is standing, and laugh while he does it. Lady Odessa weighs everything. She has weighed Ashford and Ser Moryn above Grassfield this year, and said so very kindly.” He paused. “In her place I should have done the same.”

“And Armond?”

“Lord Armond is Lord Leo’s man, as he has been since Redgrass.”

“Which is to say that he is the man who was last praised for his scar. Leo praised it first; Garth’s clerk has not yet, and so Armond is not Garth’s.” She said it without malice, as one reads out the weather. “He has told you how he came down upon a boy like a hammer, in the year Daemon rose. He has told it at my table eleven times. It is always a hammer.” The brown eyes lifted. “And what did you tell him, in that year?”

Orryn had not thought the room could grow quieter. It did. At the far window a needle stopped, and moved on.

“That I would consider both claims,” said Alaric.

“And did you?”

“For the better part of a day.”

“My brother Gormon considered one,” said Lady Arlanna, “for a good deal less. I do not say it was the wiser. I say that I have known many men who were told what to choose and are grateful for it now, and I have seldom known one who was glad of the telling.” She folded her hands. “My daughter?”

“Lady Brenna has asked for the old rolls, and has not refused the clerks.”

“She keeps both doors open and her husband in the room between. She learned that here.” The old hands moved. “Odessa writes that her younger son came home sooner than she had planned and will not say why.”

Orryn’s heart did a thing it had not done since the orchard. He kept his eyes on the floor, on a small flaw in the boards beside the stool, and fixed his whole mind upon it.

“He has his spurs, my lady. I gave them to him myself.”

“That is not why.”

“No, my lady.”

“Then I shall not ask what is.” She let it go like a cord. “A boy who goes that colour has been told to hold something. I commend whoever taught him.”

Alaric said nothing, and neither did the old woman by the door; and after a moment Lady Arlanna spoke of something else.

*

“You were with my husband in the stables.”

“He showed me a colt.”

“He shows everyone the colt. What did he say?”

Alaric drank the last of the barley water and put the cup carefully upon the table beside him, so that its base fitted into a ring left by some other cup. “That each of his sons gathers men,” he said. “Ser Garth the Redwynes and the clerks, Ser Moryn the old houses, Ser Olyvar the soldiers and the young knights. And that the oaths are his.”

“He said that to you.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Then he wished it carried.” She was quiet for a little while. “I will tell you what he did not say, because he does not know it is what he does. In forty years my husband has never let any man stand where he could not see him. When the king’s peace came to the Reach he forgave it one lord at a time, in a different year for each, so that every man might understand that his pardon was his own, and not a part of any bundle. A toll here. A mill there. A boy sent home from this house and not from that one. He is a very great giver, my lord husband. Every lord in the Reach holds a letter that says what Leo owes him, and no two letters are the same.”

She did not raise her voice. The whole room was listening.

“Now it is the sons. Garth counts, Moryn remembers, Olyvar rides. He has given each of them a door of the house, and not one of them the key. He will go to his grave with the keys at his belt, and he will call it love, and the worst of it is that he is not wrong.”

“Ser Garth is reading the letters,” Alaric said. “I told Lord Leo so. That his peace is written in tolls and exemptions, and that the clerks have begun to read it.”

“To his face.”

“In his stables.”

“Then you are a braver man than you are a frightened one.” She seemed to weigh that and set it aside. “Garth is right to read them. It is the only thing he has done that I cannot forgive.”

“I do not think the letters can be burned, my lady. Burn them, and every man in the Reach remembers a different one. Read them, and some men discover that they hold less than they were promised. I have not found the road between.”

“There is not one. There is only the man who goes first.” For the first time she looked, not at Alaric, but out at the window and the dry garden beyond it. “My brother lost two castles to a clerk who wrote rebel where other men had written late. I have no tenderness for parchment, Lord Alaric. Lord Tarly holds the Marches now on a better paper than Gormon had, and Gormon knows it to the last seal. When my son says he wishes everything written down, what I hear is a man deciding what Peake was.”

“And Ser Moryn would leave it unwritten.”

“Moryn would leave it unwritten, and remember it, and be owed for remembering. A man you owe is a friend.” She turned her face back. “A man you have only counted is a creditor.”

“That is paper of another kind, my lady.”

“Yes.” She regarded him as if he had been lent to her. “You are quicker than you pretend. It is the pretending that I distrust.”

Orryn followed perhaps two parts in three. He thought of his lord in the stables at dusk, speaking to Lord Leo of a peace bought with paper, and he saw that Lady Arlanna had said the same thing in quite another voice and with a different grief, and that the two of them stood on either side of it without ever quite meeting. A road looked different from the top of a hill. It might look different again from a window in Ashford.

*

“In a dry year it is the carrying that makes a lord,” she said. “Whoever owns the ships and the bridges owns the grain, and whoever owns the grain—” and she let it hang there like a pearl upon a thread, and Alaric did not reach for it. “Theomar Redwyne sits at the king’s council as Master of Ships. His son sits at my husband’s elbow as Seneschal. His daughter sits at my son’s. It is a family that does not make much noise, which I have found is the way of the very strong. I do not wish to see Highgarden counted by the Arbor. Do not mistake me. Alys is a good wife to Garth, and a good daughter to my lord husband. She asks for things twice. It is a clever habit. Leo was never asked twice by anyone before her, and he has not learned how to refuse it.”

“He speaks very warmly of her.”

“He does.”

Orryn had heard Lord Leo say nearly the same, in the stables, with a colt’s breath on his neck. It had sounded a good deal warmer there. Lady Arlanna’s answer was so even and so cool that it closed the matter like a lid, and she did not add to it until Orryn had begun to think she would not.

“A man is fond of what costs him nothing to be fond of.” She turned a page of the letter beneath her palm. “And behind the Redwynes, Oldtown. There is not a great house between the Mander and the sea that has not a Hightower grandmother somewhere in its nursery. Lady Clarice at Goldengrove. Her sister, Lady Alysanne, at Parchments, and they have a Targaryen princess for their mother. They are everywhere, and they do not look as if they were anywhere at all.”

“I understand there are two brides for Ser Harlen,” said Alaric carefully, “and that it is not mine to have an opinion.”

“That is the most expensive modesty I have heard this year.” But she let it stand. “The Blackbar girl is Redwyne in all but name, as her house is. The Rowan girl has a Hightower mother and a Hightower aunt, and Goldengrove has fields. My lord husband likes Rowan. Alys will have it Blackbar. Harlen would like us all to stop talking before his supper. You have seen him?”

“Three times, my lady. He pays his friends’ debts, and he gives wine to strangers.”

“He is kind.” She said it as she might have named a fever. “It is the thing I am most afraid for.”

No one spoke for a time. Orryn heard the thyme in the garden, ticking in the heat.

“There are men in this house who might do more than they are let,” Lady Arlanna said, in a different voice, brisk again. “My brother’s heir, Edmund, is two and twenty and ashamed of some of his father’s friends, which I count in his favour at Highgarden. He is ambitious. It is no sin in a man who must climb out of a ditch his father dug. My lord has a seneschal who is clever. I have sometimes thought Highgarden might be better served by a man who could be told he was wrong.”

“Ser Arron listened to me, my lady.”

“He listens beautifully. It is what the Redwynes are for.”

Orryn had not met Ser Edmund, though Gyles had spoken of an elder brother who was a knight, in the careful voice of a boy who admired something he did not like. He found that he did not much care for the thought of a Peake who was ashamed of his father’s friends. Gyles, he suspected, would care for it less.

“I have said Hightower,” Lady Arlanna said, “and you will have thought it a thing that happens in Oldtown.” And she turned her head, and Orryn’s stomach went with it. “It is not, Master Grandison. It has gone as far as Parchments, and it has gone as far as Grandview. You have a cousin wed to a Penrose.”

He had not expected to be spoken to. He stood up, which was wrong, and sat down again, which was worse.

“Lady Elenei, my lady. She married Ser Addam before I left Grandview.”

“Ser Addam’s mother is Alysanne Hightower. So there will be a Targaryen grandmother in your cousin’s nursery, whenever she fills it.” She considered him. “Do you know why Parchments wanted your cousin?”

“No, my lady.”

“Because your uncle is a vain man, and a vain man will follow anyone who tells him he leads. The Penrose wished for a follower who would not question, and they found one with a daughter. It was not clever of them. It was merely accurate.”

He felt the heat climb his neck. He felt Lord Alaric, at the edge of his sight, become very still.

“He is my uncle, my lady.”

“I know whose uncle he is. I asked you nothing. Is he a vain man?”

Orryn knew what his father said of Lord Alester. His father said it at Grandview, over the accounts, with a particular flatness, and Orryn had heard it so many times he could have said it in his sleep. It was not a thing to say in the solar of the Lady of Highgarden, with three women at a window and Lord Alaric hungover beside him. It was not a lie, either, and he could not make it into one.

“He is very much loved in his hall, my lady.”

“That is not what I asked,” said Lady Arlanna, “and it is the best answer I have had this morning.”

“I have a favour to ask, my lady,” said Alaric. His voice was quite level. “Not for myself.”

Orryn thought he had said it a little too soon, and that Lady Arlanna thought so too. She turned back to him as a woman lets a cat go out of a room.

“Elyce.”

“My younger daughter. She is twelve.”

“My lord husband mentioned it. He mentions everything to me, eventually, once he has done what he meant to do.” She was silent a moment, and her eyes went to the window and back. “I shall look. There are houses that keep their old friends, and some of them have sons whom their mothers are tired of. I will not give you a name. I have never given a name before it was time, and I am too old to be taught otherwise.”

“I ask nothing more, my lady.”

“You will ask for something, always. You merely ask for it later.”

Houses that keep their old friends. Orryn turned the phrase over, and could not make it lie flat. He thought of Lord Rowan at the window, and Lord Peake, and Ashford. He thought they were all houses that had been remembered by Ser Moryn, and he wondered whether that was what she meant, and whether Lord Alaric thought so too. When he looked, his lord’s face gave back nothing at all.

“And Lady Ceryse?” Alaric said. “I trust Old Oak keeps her well.”

“Old Oak does not keep her. She keeps it.” It was quick and sharp, a correction and no more. “She holds it for her son. She is well. Her other children are in my care, which is why she is.”

Orryn knew the Oakheart name as he knew the names of most great houses, from the rolls in the maester’s chamber at Grassfield; he had not known she had children, or that any of them were here. He looked at the lady in the green velvet and thought of her in a nursery, correcting a small boy’s grip upon a spoon. He did not think she was cruel. He thought she was exact, and that the two might not be so far apart as he had supposed.

“You are good to ask,” said Lady Arlanna, and did not say more.

*

When she dismissed them it was as she had greeted them, as if the audience had come to its natural end, like a candle.

“I shall write to Ashford that you are sensible,” she said to Alaric. “It will please her. She has been anxious to be right about you.”

“I am grateful, my lady.”

“Drink more water, Lord Alaric, and less of my son’s wine.”

He kissed her hand again. Orryn rose from the stool as soundlessly as he could, and found that the old woman by the door had already opened it for him. At the far window Lady Meriel’s needle was moving, and it moved once, he saw, to the very same place it had been before. She did not look up. He had the feeling that she had been taught how not to, and was very good at it.

Lady Melessa caught them at the head of the stair.

“My godmother likes you,” she said to Alaric.

“How can you tell, my lady?”

“She corrected you twice.” She looked at Orryn then, and he saw that her face, which was friendly, was also quite shrewd. “And she told you about Brenna. She tells nobody about Brenna.” She stood a moment as if considering something. “My husband will ask what she said. I shall tell him she asked about Merryweather.”

“Will he believe you?”

“He will think it is about Wylla’s betrothal,” said Lady Melessa, “and he will be content.”

She went back inside.

They went down in silence. Alaric took the stair slowly, one hand on the wall, and at the turning where the gallery opened on the herb garden he stopped, and put the other hand over his eyes, and stood there in the light.

“A vain fool,” he said, to the thyme. “Your uncle.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“You answered her very well.”

“I told her he was loved.”

“You told her the truth and let her do the rest. It is the only way I have ever come out of that room whole.” He lowered the hand. “I would not repeat any of it to your father.”

“My father has said worse.”

“I know,” said Alaric. “That is why.”

They went on across the gallery. Beyond the arch the lower yard showed between two walls, brown and trampled, and a line of boys with buckets going from the well to the horse trough. One was smaller than the rest and wore a green sleeve turned back three times.

“She thinks I am Moryn’s,” Alaric said.

Orryn waited. He did not ask whether she was wrong. He was learning what a squire could be told by not asking.

Alaric did not say. After a time he said that he would like a great deal of broth, and that the guest court seemed to have been built a mile from any kitchen, and Orryn said he knew a way.

Matthos had stopped at the trough. He had seen them. He stood with the bucket in both hands and the water slopping, and for a moment he did not seem to know what a boy in Moryn’s green ought to do when his father and his father’s squire went by on the gallery. Then he raised the bucket a little, a stiff, solemn salute, and spilled half of it down his leg.

Orryn lifted his hand.

It occurred to him that it would be a long while before either of them was anyone’s old friend. He kept his hand up until Matthos had set the bucket down and gone back for another.
