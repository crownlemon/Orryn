Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

Ser Androw Ashford, heir to Ashford and for two moons now the husband of Alyn Merryweather’s sister, rode out at first light, before the heat had properly woken, and Orryn held his stirrup.

Orryn was fourteen, a Grandison of Grandview in the Stormlands and Lord Alaric Meadows’s squire, and he had held a great many stirrups at Grassfield since the dry began. He had learned to hold them without looking up.

Ser Androw had sat late in the solar with Lord Alaric, and he had the careful look this morning of a man who knows exactly how much wine he drank. He took the reins, settled into the saddle, and glanced once up at the shuttered windows of the solar before he looked down.

“You will be going north,” he said.

It was not a question, and Orryn did not know how to answer it. Nobody had told him anything. “I don’t know, ser.”

“No. Well.” Ser Androw gathered the reins into one long hand. “If you do, and you see my goodbrother—and I think you will see him; he is generally knocking somebody off a horse—tell him that his lady mother has written to him four times since the new moon, and his sister twice, and he has answered neither. Tell him Wylla says that a knight may write a letter without loss of honour. She says she has seen it done.”

“Yes, ser.”

Ser Androw smiled, as if he knew something about squires and letters that Orryn would rather he did not, and rode out under the arch with his four men behind him and the orange banner drooping on its staff. They turned west for Longtable, and the dust stood up behind them and hung there.

There was a letter in his boot, folded so small that the creases had worn white. It had been there half a moon and more, and every morning he put his foot in on top of it and thought of the three letters he owed and wrote none of them. He would carry the reproach anyway.

Alyn had been senior squire at Grassfield until Lord Alaric knighted him in the sept and sent him away the next morning, for a reason Orryn had been told never to speak of. At Leonette’s wedding he had ridden in among Lord Rowan’s knights in a green cloak dusty at the hem, called Grandview! across the yard as though nothing had ever happened in the herb garden, and handed Orryn his reins as he always used to. In the sept Orryn had looked round once and seen him at the back, watching the altar while Leonette walked up to it on her father’s arm; and Orryn had watched Alyn, and then made himself stop.

“Grandison.”

Lord Alaric was on the steps. He was thirty, lean and restless, with light brown hair that never lay the way it was combed and a doublet of plain brown wool, because he said a man in a dry year had no business dressing like a harvest. He held a heel of bread he was not eating.

“My lord.”

“When the bell goes, the small hall. Find Ser Lucan on your way. And Grandison—” He considered the bread. “Tell Ser Wyman I shall want the dun looked at. Feet first.”

*

The small hall was cooler than the yard, which was not saying much, and somebody had wetted the rushes.

Lady Jeyne sat at the high table in the carved chair that Verrall had taken to carrying in after her. She was Lord Armond Caswell’s daughter and Lady of Grassfield these fifteen years, eight-and-twenty, with dark chestnut hair and a face that told you very little unless she wished it to. She was with child, and it showed now in the way she sat, and in the stool someone had put under her feet. Orryn had never before seen her put her feet up in company.

The rest stood. Ser Bertram Corliss leaned against the wall by the screen, lean and grizzled, with his scarred cheek and his battered gorget. Ser Lucan Marwick, Osric’s master, stood with his hands behind his back: a household knight of eight-and-thirty from a small landed house of the Reach, tall and square, with cropped dark hair and a face like a shut door, who generally arrived at a thing a little before he was sent for. Lyman Verrall, the steward, had his tablet and his smile. Maester Perrin leaned in his corner, long and grey, with his chain of many metals on his narrow chest.

Lord Alaric did not sit. He stood at the end of the table with the heel of bread still in his hand, as though he had forgotten it was there.

“I am going to Goldengrove,” he said. “Tomorrow, at first light.” He let it lie for the space of a breath. “Lord Rowan has let it be known, through Lord Armond, that if I want a thing of him I may come and ask it to his face. I find that I do want a thing of him, and that I would sooner ask it myself than have it asked for me by somebody else. He has the Oakheart boy in his keeping. Highgarden has offered my daughter to that boy without asking him, and without asking my wife. I do not mean to make it three.”

Nobody said anything. Orryn, by the door, became very interested in the latch.

The Oakheart boy was Lord Harys of Old Oak, thirteen, whose father had died in the rebellion while his house stood divided, and who had been given to Lord Rowan afterward to raise, a surety for his house’s good conduct. Lady Arlanna Tyrell, the Lady of Highgarden, was his grandmother. She had chosen him for Elyce and sent word of it by her daughter Lady Brenna, who was Lady Jeyne’s stepmother, and nobody had asked Lady Jeyne first.

“Three days there,” said Verrall, “and three back, my lord, and ten mouths on the road, and the horses—”

“Nine. Lem rides ahead today, so that Lord Rowan is not surprised by me.” Alaric glanced at Ser Lucan, and something in it was almost a smile. “I know what Ser Lucan will say. I am saying it first.”

“Then I need not say it, my lord.” Ser Lucan did not move his face. “I will say the rest. Go as a lord goes: a knight beside you, your banner, a rider before you, and something for his table that is worth having and not worth remarking. A lord who comes without warning comes as a friend or as a beggar, and Lord Rowan is not your friend.”

“No. He is not.”

“A falcon,” Verrall offered. “Ser Wyman’s young peregrine—”

“I am not buying the boy, Verrall. There is perry in the undercroft from the year before last; take a cask. It is the only thing Grassfield makes that Goldengrove does not.”

Maester Perrin cleared his throat, as he did before anything he expected to be refused. “A letter would carry the same request, my lord, and spare the road. A letter can be kept. What is said in a hall is only what the hall remembers.”

“That is why I am going,” said Alaric. “He did not say write to me, Maester. He said come.”

“And what will you ask him for, my lord?” It was Ser Lucan again. “Not the boy, you say.”

“Not the boy. To see him. To hear from the man who keeps him on what terms he is kept, and when they end. And to tell Rycherd Rowan, in his own hall, that Meadows does not marry its daughters over anyone’s head. Not his. Not mine.”

“In law,” said Ser Lucan, “the boy is Lord Leo’s to give, my lord. Lord Leo placed him at Goldengrove.”

“In law. And in custom?”

Ser Lucan was silent a moment. “In custom no guardian is passed over,” he said at last. “I would say both, if I were asked.”

“Then come with me, and say both, if you are asked.”

“As you command, my lord.”

Ser Bertram had not moved from the wall. When he spoke, it was in the flat voice he used for counting, as though the words were coins he was not sure of. “It will be said that Grassfield rode to Rowan’s hall on Ser Moryn’s heels.”

“It will.”

“It will be said at Highgarden.”

“Then let Highgarden ask me why, and I shall tell it.” Alaric turned to face him. “Not because Highgarden had no right. Ser Lucan has given you the law. Because it had no manners. I will say it the same in Garth’s hall or Moryn’s or the king’s.”

“Manners,” said Ser Bertram, as he had once said proper. He looked at the floor. “You will want the six.”

“No. The six stay stood down. You keep the keep. Lady Jeyne has the keys, and you have the gate.”

Something crossed the scarred face and was gone. “As you say, my lord.”

Lady Jeyne had said nothing the whole while. Now she raised her eyes.

“And what is the thing, my lord? The thing you want of him. You have told us three.”

Lord Alaric looked at her down the length of the table. “To know.”

“Then you had better take a squire who listens,” said Lady Jeyne, and for the first time she looked past him to the door, and at Orryn. “Master Grandison. When you have done with the dun, you may pack for your lord. The second-best doublet. Lord Rowan has seen the best.”

*

Lord Alaric’s bedchamber held the afternoon heat like a bread oven. Orryn knelt by the saddlebags and packed as he had been taught: shirts rolled, not folded; the doublet flat between two of them; the good boots wrapped in sacking; razor, comb, tallow, and a needle and thread, because there was always a button. Ser Wyman had looked at Rook’s feet and said the heel had held and might hold another moon, and that he was to walk the dun downhill and not argue about it.

He had got as far as the boots when the door opened and Lord Alaric came in, and behind him Lady Jeyne.

“Grandison—” his lord began.

“Let him stay,” said Lady Jeyne. “He has heard worse in this house, and kept it.”

She sat on the clothes chest under the window, and Lord Alaric pushed the footstool over to her without being asked, and she put her feet on it without thanking him. Orryn went on wrapping the boots, very slowly.

“You will see the boy,” she said.

“That is why I am going.”

“No. You are going because Rycherd Rowan said you might come to him, and you could not bear to be the kind of man who would not. You will see the boy because he is there.” She said it without heat. “Look at his hands when you see him. Whether he bites his nails. Whether he speaks to the grooms or only through them, and looks at you when you speak to him or at your boots. A boy kept in another man’s house since he was a babe has learned one thing very well. I want to know what it is.”

“I will look.”

“And you will promise nothing.”

“I have said—”

“I know what you said. You said it in my solar, with my father in the door. Now say it to me.”

Lord Alaric was quiet for a moment. Then he said, “Nothing will be promised,” in a different voice, and Lady Jeyne nodded as if a merchant had at last named his true price.

“Rowan has the same complaint as I have,” she said. “I should not have said so before my father. By now it is a thing I said at Highgarden.” Her hand moved on the curve of her belly and stilled. “Do not let him make it his. If he says yes to you, he is saying no to Highgarden, and he will want you at his shoulder while he says it. That is not the same as wanting Elyce.”

“I know it.”

“You know it here. You will know it less at his board, with his wine in you and his lady being kind.”

“Then I shall drink water.”

“You will drink what you are given,” said Lady Jeyne. “You always do.”

Orryn thought of a terrace at Highgarden, and a sheltered arch, and a lady in dark blue. He looked at the sacking in his hands and wrapped the second boot as though his life depended on its corners.

“And Ser Alyn Merryweather sits at Rowan’s board,” Lady Jeyne went on, in exactly the same voice.

“So I am told.”

“You knighted him.”

“I did.”

“You will be courteous to him, my lord. You will not be kind.” She turned her head, and Orryn felt the brown eyes rest on the back of his neck before he looked up and met them. “Nor will Master Grandison.”

“No, my lady,” he said. His face was burning. He did not know which thing she meant: the herb garden, which she had told him to keep, or something else, which nobody had told him to keep and which she could not possibly know. He was very much afraid it was the second.

She held his eyes a moment longer and then let him go, as one lets go of a sleeve.

“I told you that I would not be brought to bed in a house that had chosen a side by accident,” she said to her husband.

“And I told you that I had chosen it.”

“You chose a brook that runs at night and a keep with no hay in it.” For the first time something moved at the corner of her mouth that was almost a smile, and not a kind one. “Now you are riding three days to sit at the table of the man the whole Reach calls Ser Moryn’s best friend. If you mean it, mean it. If you do not, say so at his board, aloud, where someone will hear you.”

“I am going to the boy, Jeyne.”

“Every man who ever rode to Goldengrove was going to something else.” She shifted on the chest, and winced, and said in the same breath, “Master Grandison. Go and look at your horse.”

He went, and shut the door, and stood in the passage with the boots still in his arms. The oak let almost nothing through. Once he heard his lord laugh, short, the way he had laughed at the mill-pool; after that, nothing for a long while. Septa Lenora came up the stair with her sewing, looked at him and at the door, and went down again without a word.

When Lord Alaric came out, he did not look like a man who had lost an argument, nor like one who had won it. He looked at the boots in Orryn’s arms.

“Those go in the bag, Grandison. Not on your chest.”

*

They rode at first light, nine of them and three mules.

Lem had gone the afternoon before on the fastest horse in the stable, with a letter in Maester Perrin’s best hand saying only that Lord Meadows would wait upon Lord Rowan at Goldengrove, if Lord Rowan would receive him. Sergeant Cobb, broad and slow and grey, with his squint and the spear he used as a staff, rode at the head with the banner, then Alf, and two of the six who had stood saddled under the beech for nine days. One of them grinned at Orryn like a boy let off his lessons. Jory Marsh, Hob’s youngest, led the mules, and the second mule carried the perry in wet sacking that would be dry inside the hour.

Ser Wyman checked Rook’s feet again by lantern-light, though he had checked them at noon. “Downhill,” he said.

“Yes, ser.”

Perkin Fenwick had come down in his shirt to see them off, with his hair standing up on one side and the gap in his teeth on show. He was fifteen and Ser Tobin’s squire, and he had never in his life watched anyone ride anywhere without wishing to be sent instead.

“Bring me back a golden apple,” he said. “They say there’s one on every pillow at Goldengrove. And give Ser Alyn my love. Tell him the yard’s gone very dull.”

“I’ll tell him,” said Orryn.

“You won’t. You’ll go red and forget.” Perkin grinned. “It’s all right. I’d forget too.”

Baldric Haye stood by the trough with his arms folded, square and pale and sixteen. Ser Bertram was not going to Goldengrove, and so his squire was staying to count oats.

“Three days on a horse,” Baldric said, when Orryn came to fill his water skin. He did not look at him. “Three days on a horse with him and nobody else to talk to but a man with a squint and a boy who prays.”

“Ser Lucan’s coming.”

“Ser Lucan doesn’t talk. He inspects.” Baldric unfolded his arms. “You know what I’m going to say.”

Orryn did, and said nothing. The old award for the Alder Mill, Baldric’s father’s mill, which would say who bore the cost of the Graves men grinding there free, was in the three-lock chest in the undercroft, where it had been since the dry began, and Lord Alaric had said they would read it with Verrall when the guests were gone, and the guests had come and gone and come again.

“Well,” said Baldric. “It’s three days.” And he went to see to Ser Bertram’s horse, which did not need seeing to.

Orryn had his foot in the stirrup when Lady Elyce came out of the passage under the gallery at a run, in a shawl over her nightgown and somebody’s boots too big for her, with a maid behind her at a much less hopeful pace.

“Grandison.”

“My lady.”

She was twelve, small for it, with wide hazel eyes and freckles the sun had joined together across her nose. She put her hand flat on Rook’s neck and looked up at Orryn with great severity.

“You’re to find out if he can ride,” she said. “Properly.”

“Properly, my lady?”

“Not like a lord. Lords sit there and let the horse do it, and look at the people.” She frowned at Rook’s mane. “Like someone who likes horses.”

“How will I know?”

“You’ll know. You know with Matthos.” Then, in a lower voice, quickly: “And you’re not to tell him I asked. Or Mother. Or anyone.”

“No, my lady.”

She stood back. Up on the gallery the shutters of the solar were open, and Lady Jeyne stood in the gap with her hand on the frame, too far off for Orryn to see her face. Lord Alaric looked up at the window from the saddle for the space of a breath. Then he raised his hand, and the shutters did not move, and he lowered it and turned his horse to the gate.

Ser Bertram was at the gate, not under the beech. He stood aside to let them through, with his arms folded and the keys of the gatehouse on his belt, and he did not look round when they had passed.

*

The north road ran out of Grassy Vale between fields cut early and short, the stubble full of rooks. At the first village there was a line at the well, women with buckets and children with jugs, and they stood back to let the lord go by and watched him with flat, patient faces. Lord Alaric reined in and asked the reeve how deep the rope went now and whether the beasts had been sold, and when they rode on he was quiet for a long time.

By noon the heat sat on the road like a man on a bench. Osric Dunmere rode beside Orryn most of that day, since the squires were expected to keep together where they could be found. He was seventeen, careful and serious, Septa Lenora’s nephew, with a long earnest face and the silver seven-pointed star of the Faith at his throat.

Toward evening, where the road bent under a stand of thorn, there was a wayside shrine: a lump of grey stone with seven faces cut into it so long ago that the rain had made them all one face. Osric asked leave of Ser Lucan with his eyes and knelt in the dust, and Orryn held both horses and looked at nothing.

When Osric came back he was very red, as though he had been caught at something.

“Ser Harys,” he said, without being asked. “Graceford. My brother’s wife’s brother. I have met him twice. He gave me a hand up once at Holyhall, when I fell off the mounting block in front of everyone, and he did not laugh.” He swung into the saddle. “The Warrior for his arm. The Mother for the rest.”

They rode on a little. The thorn fell behind them. Osric was not finished; Orryn could feel it, the way you can feel weather.

“Were you glad?” Osric said at last. “When Lord Alaric stood the six down?”

Orryn looked at his reins.

“I was,” said Osric, before he could answer. He said it low and fast, to his horse’s ears. “I thanked the Mother for it, that night. I do not think it is a proper thing to thank her for. I asked my aunt, after, whether it was a sin to be glad not to be sent, and she said it was not a sin, it was only true, and that I was not to say it to Ser Lucan.” He swallowed. “So I am saying it to you.”

Orryn thought of the trough, and Baldric wringing the rag in his fists, and the thing he had not been able to get past his teeth. It was easier, somehow, with Osric’s face turned away from him and the dust going gold in the low sun. It was still not easy.

“I don’t think the Mother minds,” he said.

Osric was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, once, as though that were an answer, and perhaps it was.

*

They slept the first night in a farmer’s barn with the mules, and the second in the open, under a sky that had forgotten how to rain.

On the second day they left Grassy Vale behind, and Ser Lucan fell back beside the squires and began to instruct them, as another man might have begun to whistle.

“At Goldengrove,” he said, “you will not speak unless spoken to. You will be asked your names and your houses, and you will give them and not add to them. You will dismount when your lord dismounts. You will not tell anyone what you think of the wine.”

“No, ser.”

“Lord Rowan,” said Ser Lucan, “is Lord of Goldengrove and hereditary Marshal of the Northmarch, an office his house holds by inheritance and not by Lord Leo’s gift, and he will not forget it, so neither will you. His lady wife is a Hightower of Oldtown whose mother was a Targaryen princess, and you will not mention either fact, because both will be mentioned to you. His heir is promised to Lord Ronnel Penrose’s daughter, and Lord Ronnel holds the king’s purse.” He paused. “Lord Rowan is said to be Ser Moryn’s friend. That is true. He is also his own. Remember the second thing longer than the first.”

That night they camped in a fold of the hills where there had been water once, and Orryn pulled his lord’s boots off by the fire and rubbed tallow into the cracks, while Lord Alaric sat against his saddle with his bare feet in the warm dust and looked at the coals.

“Ser Cleyton says the boy asked him whose man he was,” Alaric said, after a while. “And what his house paid him.”

“Yes, my lord.”

“What would you make of a boy who asked that?”

Orryn kept rubbing. He thought of Hob’s Cross, and Ser Robyn Rhysling’s one pale eye resting on him across the cart: A Stormlander brings a Reach lord’s grain to the Highgarden road. He thought of how often this last year he had been asked, one way or another, whose he was.

“That someone asks him,” he said. “Every day, I’d think. In a house that isn’t his.”

Lord Alaric was silent. A coal settled. Somewhere out in the dark one of the mules shifted its feet and blew.

“Yes,” he said.

Orryn set down the first boot and took up the second. He did not know if he had said something stupid or something true, and he was not sure his lord knew either.

“I was asked it once,” said Alaric. “Whose I was. The year Daemon rose. I was nineteen, and Lord Armond came down from Bitterbridge with Brenna and stood in my hall and asked it, and I said that I would consider both claims.” He laughed under his breath. “I considered them for the better part of a day. People still remind me of the day.”

A day. Orryn’s hand had stopped on the boot. His grandfather had waited nine at Grandview, and ridden on the tenth, and come to Redgrass when the field was already won, and men had been calling Grandview prudent ever since, in the voice one uses for a dog that has fouled the hall. He had heard the word all his life. It had never once occurred to him that a man might be reminded of a single day.

“My lord,” he said, and then did not know what he had meant to ask.

“Go to sleep, Grandison,” said Lord Alaric, quite gently. “Goldengrove tomorrow. You will want to have your wits.”

*

On the third day they came down out of the hills into Rowan’s country, and Orryn knew it before Sergeant Cobb pointed out the boundary stone, because the trees changed.

They were orchards, apple all of them, in long rows on the slopes, and every tree had a ring of dark earth about its foot where somebody had been watering it. Not the old trees: the young ones, the new grafts, the ones that would die. Carts with barrels lashed upright went up and down the rows, and men walked from tree to tree with buckets as though they were feeding horses. Orryn thought of the east orchard at Grassfield, and the canker, and how many buckets it would take to save one row there, and where the water would come from.

Then the road turned over a shoulder of hill and he saw where it came from.

The Mander lay in the bottom of the valley, low, lower than it had any business being, with banks of pale cracked mud on either side and dead reeds standing up out of nothing. But it was running. Orryn could see it move, brown and slow and broad, with the light on it, and the men behind him went quiet, the way a hall goes quiet when the meat comes in.

Goldengrove, on the Mander, with a river at his door. Septon Emmon had said it at Highgarden, by his iron pot with a ladle in his hand, to his brother the Justiciar: a lord with a river at his door would not dig for a village on a hill. Orryn had not thought of it since. He thought of it now, watching the barrels slop over on the carts, and could not have said what he felt except that he felt it in his chest.

The road ran down to the water and along it, under willows that the river had kept alive, and where it turned away from the bank and began to climb, half a dozen riders were waiting in the shade.

One of them came forward, and Orryn had a very bad moment trying not to stare.

He was a short man, so short that his good bay made him look like a boy on a borrowed horse, and bald under a broad straw hat, which he swept off as he came so that his scalp shone pink and freckled in the sun. On the side of his neck was a swelling as big as a hen’s egg, which his collar had been cut wide to make room for. He wore a fine green doublet with a golden tree stitched on the breast and a great deal of sweat under the arms of it.

“Lord Meadows!” He reined in so hard that the bay threw up its head. “Ser Wendell Webber, my lord, at your service and my goodbrother’s. His lady sister is my lady wife, and my cousin is Lord Wyman of Coldmoat. You will know Coldmoat. Everyone knows Coldmoat.” He did not wait to learn whether Lord Alaric knew Coldmoat. “Rycherd had your man’s letter last night. He sends me to bring you in.”

“Lord Rowan is very kind,” said Alaric. “And so are you, ser, to come out in this heat.”

“Oh, I asked to come.” Ser Wendell beamed. “A man likes to see who is coming to supper. Rycherd’s suppers are not what they were, with the Penroses eating them.” He turned his horse to fall in beside Lord Alaric’s, and in the turning broke wind, loudly and at length, and did not seem to notice that he had done it. Alf made a noise behind Orryn like a man swallowing a fly. Sergeant Cobb’s face did not move at all. Ser Lucan looked at the river as though it had said something improper.

“The Penroses?” said Lord Alaric.

“The bride.” Ser Wendell lowered his voice to a little under a herald’s. “Lord Ronnel’s girl, that’s promised to Triston. Came up from King’s Landing with her brother and forty horse and a wagon of gowns, to be shown the house she’s to have, and she has been shown it. Every room. Twice. They feasted her the night before last, and I never saw so many swans, and not one of them carved for my end of the table.” He shook his head. “The brother is at the wine before noon. He owes my eldest boy nine stags at tiles. A princess’s son! He says his mother will pay it.”

Orryn’s hand had tightened on the reins without his leave, and Rook put back an ear to ask what he meant by it. Penroses. He looked down at the dun’s coarse black mane and thought, quite clearly and for no good reason, of a slip of paper with Ser Jon Penrose’s name at the bottom of it, which his lord had kept since the day Orryn rode in. Lent. No date of return.

“And your Merryweather,” Ser Wendell went on, delighted to have so many people to tell things to, “the one you knighted—he has put my second boy on his back every morning this moon. I have told Rycherd it is not courteous in a guest. Rycherd says he is not a guest, he is a knight of the household. My boy is his nephew!”

“I am sorry for your boy,” said Alaric gravely.

“So am I. He is a good lad. He has very good seat when he is in it.” Ser Wendell squinted sideways at him, and some slow thought appeared to cross his face and find nowhere to sit down. “You have come about the Oakheart pup, I expect. Rycherd said you would. ‘Meadows will come,’ he said, when Lord Armond had gone. ‘He is the sort that comes.’”

For a moment Lord Alaric said nothing at all. Orryn, riding at his left, saw only the side of his face, and it did not change.

“Did he,” said Alaric.

“He did. And you have a son, they tell me, squiring for Ser Moryn? A fine thing. Is he promised?” Ser Wendell did not wait. “I have daughters, my lord, a good many of them, and all of them sound in wind and limb.” His eye fell on the second mule, and the bundle on its back. “Is that wine?”

“Perry,” said Alaric.

“Ah.” Ser Wendell considered the cask with a kind of resigned tenderness. “Perry. Well. How many casks have you brought?”

“One.”

“Ah,” said Ser Wendell again, and was quiet, for nearly a quarter of a mile.

Beyond the willows the road climbed between walls of dry stone, and the sun came down behind them so that their shadows went up the hill ahead, long and thin as lances. At the last turn the riders in front drew aside, and Orryn looked up and saw Goldengrove.

It stood on its hill above the river, as Ser Cleyton had said, and he had not believed Ser Cleyton. The walls were of a pale stone that took the evening and gave it back, and for as long as the sun stood just so they were gold, gold the length of them, gold in the towers and gold in the crenels, so that the whole castle seemed to have been poured rather than built. Over the gate, on a banner that hung straight down for want of wind, the golden tree of Rowan spread its branches on a field of white.

Somewhere behind those walls was a boy of thirteen who asked people whose man they were, and a princess’s son who owed nine stags at tiles, and a girl who had been shown every room twice. Somewhere, too, was a knight with chestnut hair grown longer than it used to be, who had put a Rowan cousin on his back every morning for a moon, and who had asked after him.

Orryn did not let himself think further than that. He sat Rook and looked at the walls, and Ser Wendell talked about swans, and Lord Alaric said nothing.

It was a long way up the hill. The gold had gone out of the stone before they reached the gate.
