Highgarden, 208 AC

The village was called Little Ford, and by the second morning it had no hay.

Orryn saw it from the saddle, through the open doors of a barn where forty horses had been fed for two nights. The floor behind the doors was swept bare, with a few yellow stems in the corners, like the bottom of an empty cup. Outside stood a carter with an empty cart and the reins slack in his hand, looking at nothing.

“Don’t stare, Grandison,” Lord Alaric said, without turning his head. “Someone will think you mean it.”

They had ridden out at first light, because a man did not refuse an invitation from Lord Leo’s youngest son without a reason, and Lord Alaric had not yet found one that he liked. He sat a hired Highgarden bay with the ease of a man who had never in his life been thrown. The gelding under Orryn belonged to the same stables and disliked him. The stable-boy had said that it disliked everyone, which Orryn had taken for a kindness and now doubted.

Beyond the village the meadow ran away dry and tawny to a line of thin trees, and on it the drill was forming. Lord Luthor Roxton of the Ring sat a tall roan at the head of the field. He was Lord Marshal of the Reach, which Orryn understood to mean that when Lord Leo wished a thing done with horses, it was Lord Roxton who was told. He was a big grey man of nearly sixty with a face like a cracked saddle and a voice that Orryn could have heard from the wall at Grassfield. He was using it now.

“Not like a wedding! Like a man who means to arrive!”

The young knights of Highgarden rode in a long double rank, and behind them, at a respectful distance and in no rank at all, came the squires. Orryn had been told to take his place with them. He found Will Risley already there, and Hugh Redwyne, who looked better than he had in the yard and was plainly determined not to speak of it, and Gyles Peake, whistling. Ser Harlen Tyrell rode up and down the back of the line, quietly pushing a stirrup into place or a strap into a buckle for whoever needed it, and not once looking as though he knew he was doing it.

They were given blunted lances and a post with a shield nailed to it. Orryn rode at the post five times. He hit the shield once, dropped the lance twice, and on the fifth pass found that he had closed his eyes. Gyles, who had watched all of it, drew alongside him at the end.

“You look at the point,” he said. “Don’t. Look at where you want it to go, and let the point find its own way. It generally knows.”

“Does yours?”

“Mine finds its way into the nearest Redwyne,” said Gyles, and Hugh, passing, said something short that Orryn did not catch and that made Will laugh.

By the time Lord Roxton had finished with them the sun was well up and the dust hung over the meadow in a long flat cloud. Orryn’s arm ached to the shoulder. He walked the gelding back toward the village, and was nearer than he meant to be when Ser Olyvar Tyrell rode up to the barn.

Orryn had been told Ser Olyvar was thirty-three, and he looked it from nowhere but the eyes. He was the youngest of Lord Leo’s sons, broad through the shoulder and quick to laugh, and the only one of the three that the squires spoke of as though he were one of them. He was the man who had offered for Matthos in the yard and taken Lord Alaric’s refusal as a good joke at his own cost. He swung down and tossed a purse to the carter without looking at what it weighed.

“For the hay, friend. Ser Owen at Highgarden will make up the rest.”

The carter looked at the purse, and then at the barn. A grey man in a leather apron had come out of the nearest house and stood beside him, and it was he who spoke. He was the reeve, Orryn gathered, and he spoke the way a man does who has already lost the argument and is only stating it for the sake of the record.

“There’s no hay to be bought, ser. Not for gold. We cut the last of it before the dry began, and there’ll be no second cut.”

“Then we’ll find some,” said Ser Olyvar, kindly.

“Where?” said the reeve.

Nobody answered. Ser Olyvar was already looking for his horse. He had the look of a man who did not hear an argument as an argument but only as a thing that someone else was about to settle, and Orryn, holding the reins of two horses, found that he could not dislike him for it. He had spent a week of his first season at Grassfield learning what a horse ate in a day and what a field gave in a summer, and he stood there working out what the reeve had already worked out. Forty horses had eaten Little Ford’s winter in two nights.

Ser Olyvar came back across the yard with Lord Alaric, one hand on his shoulder.

“Thirty spears, Lord Alaric. Horse, if you can mount them, foot if you cannot. Lord Tarly wants riders along the Marches before the year turns, and he wants them from every lord who can spare ten. I would rather have yours with me than with a stranger.”

“Grassfield owes Highgarden its spears,” Alaric said. “It will send them when Lord Leo calls. It will not send them before.”

It was said pleasantly and without any weight at all, and Ser Olyvar laughed outright.

“You would have made a Tyrell. My father said the same of you, and I did not believe him.” He clapped Alaric’s shoulder once more and went to his horse, calling something to Lord Roxton as he went.

Alaric took his reins from Orryn and mounted, and for a little while he did not ride. He sat with his hands on the saddle and looked back across the village, at the empty barn and the carter, the reeve in his apron, the sun-bleached roofs. Then he turned the bay toward Highgarden, and Orryn followed, and neither of them said anything at all.

*

Lord Osmund Leygood kept his court in the lesser hall at Highgarden, which was not lesser in any way that Orryn could see. It was a long stone room with benches along both walls and a plain table at the far end, and high windows through which the light came down in slanted bars, full of dust. There was no throne, nor dais. A man sat behind the table on an ordinary bench, and everyone else stood or sat below him.

Orryn had expected someone larger. Lord Osmund was a narrow, upright man of fifty-six with a close white beard and a long upper lip, and he sat with both hands flat upon the board as though he were holding it down. He was the Lord Justiciar, which meant that when two men in the Reach could not agree, and neither of their lords could make them, it was he who was sent for. A servant had told Orryn the night before that he was the fairest man in Highgarden. A knight at the same table had called him the most tiresome. Orryn, watching him listen, thought that both might be true and that neither would trouble him.

A shorter, softer man in a brown septon’s robe sat at the end of the table, with a worn seven-pointed star on a cord and his brother’s upper lip. He was Septon Emmon, Lord Osmund’s younger brother, who kept the sept at Highgarden, and he had come, Orryn gathered, to swear the men to their word. Beside him stood a stooped old maester with a chain of mixed metals, who looked at the room like a man waiting for it to finish. Two Tyrell guards stood at the door.

Lord Alaric had taken a place on a side bench, among a few other lords and knights who had come to watch. Orryn stood behind him. Lord Alaric had said that a squire might as well see how the Reach settled its quarrels. He had not said what to expect.

The quarrel was about a well.

Ser Tom Costen was a stout, red-faced knight with the look of a man who had been out in the sun without a hat for several days, which Orryn thought was likely. He held a hill village called Cress Hill, with two hundred people in it, and there had been a spring on the hill that fed his village and, on the other side, the farm of a widow, Lady Ormesby, who held her land from Lord Rowan. The spring had shrunk to a seep. Ser Tom’s men had dug a trench by night and turned the seep to Cress Hill. The widow’s bailiff, a lean grey man named Maddock, had gone up the next morning with six men and a cudgel, and there had been blood.

Lord Osmund listened to all of it. He asked Ser Tom how many wells the village had. One, and it was dry. He asked Maddock how many mouths the widow fed, and Maddock said nine, and a dog. He asked who had dug the trench, and Ser Tom said his men had, on his order, and Septon Emmon rose, held out the star on its cord, and had both men lay a hand upon it and swear that what they had said was true. They swore. Orryn found he was holding his breath.

“The grant is plain,” said Lord Osmund. His voice was not loud, and it had no give in it at all. “The spring is the lady’s. Cress Hill is given the water of the brook, and the brook is dry. That is no fault of the lady’s.”

“There are two hundred souls on Cress Hill,” said Ser Tom.

“I know it.”

“They’ll be dead by the turn of the season!”

“Then it is for you to dig a deeper well, or to move them to water, and for your lord to help you,” said Lord Osmund. “It is not for you to take water from a house that has a right to it. The trench will be filled. You will pay the lady’s bailiff for his hurt, and a fine to Highgarden.”

Ser Tom did not answer. He stood with his big hands hanging open at his sides, and for a moment Orryn thought he was going to weep. Then something went across his face and was gone, and he was moving.

It was done before Orryn could understand what he was seeing. There was a knife that had been somewhere in the knight’s sleeve, and then it was in his hand, and he was across the floor with it, and Maddock threw up an arm. The blade went through the sleeve and the arm and came out red. A guard hit Ser Tom from behind with the flat of his halberd and another had him by the hair, and the stout man went down on the flags with a sound like a dropped sack. Someone shouted. Someone on the bench behind Orryn stood up so fast the wood cracked.

Lord Osmund had not moved.

“Put it down, Tom,” said Septon Emmon, and he had come around the end of the table and was standing between the guards and the man on the floor, with his star in one hand and the other held out flat. “Put it down. It is done.”

“It’s my village,” said Ser Tom, into the stone.

“I know it is.”

Maddock was holding his arm with his other hand and the blood was running between his fingers. He was grey in the face and perfectly steady. “My lady has a friend in Ser Moryn Tyrell,” he said, to the room, “and Ser Moryn will hear who drew steel in the Justiciar’s hall.”

Ser Tom, on the floor, with a guard’s knee between his shoulders, said it too, in a half-strangled voice. “Goldengrove will hear of this. Lord Rowan will hear it from me.”

Orryn looked at Lord Osmund. The Justiciar sat with his hands flat on the board and his face unchanged. Neither man had said his name. Neither man had looked at him. They had both looked past him, at the door and the great house beyond it, as though the person who had ruled on the well was merely the one standing in the way.

“Ser Tom will be held until he is calmer,” Lord Osmund said, evenly, “and the maester will see to the bailiff’s arm. This court is ended.”

He rose, and bowed to nobody, and went out through the small door behind the table.

Septon Emmon was already kneeling by Maddock, tearing a strip from the hem of his own robe. Orryn watched him bind the bailiff’s arm tight and quick. He spoke to him in a low voice, and Maddock answered in a lower one. Then the septon went over to the guards, and said something to them, and went on to Ser Tom. The guards let him kneel there.

Lord Alaric had not stirred from his bench.

“Mark who they named,” he said, quietly, to the air in front of him.

“Lord Rowan,” said Orryn. “And Ser Moryn.”

“And not Lord Leo. Not once.” He stood. “Come, Grandison. I want to see the grain.”

*

The carts were drawn up inside the east gate, in a line that ran the whole length of the wall. There were ten of them, with the long wicker sides that the carters used for sacks, and a boy was on each seat with a whip across his knees. Beside each cart stood a pair of Highgarden spearmen in Tyrell green, one at the head and one at the tail, with the bored look of men who had been told they would be needed and had not yet seen why.

Lord Osmund was waiting at the head of the line. He had a long grey cloak over his arm that he did not seem to have noticed, and beside him stood a younger man in a very clean doublet with a face that Orryn would have said was kind, if it had not been so still.

“Ser Lyman Beesbury,” said Lord Osmund. “Lord Treasurer of Highgarden. Lord Meadows, of Grassfield.”

Ser Lyman was thirty-five, fair and long-boned, and he stood with his weight placed carefully, like a man crossing a floor he did not entirely trust. Orryn had heard him named at supper. His mother was a Hightower and his father the lord of Honeyholt, which was sworn to Oldtown, and he served Lord Leo at Highgarden with a face that said he knew very well how that looked. He bowed to Lord Alaric, and his eye went to Orryn.

“And this is your squire. Grandison.”

“Orryn Grandison, ser.”

“Then you are kin to Parchments,” said Ser Lyman. “Ser Addam Penrose is my cousin, on my mother’s side, and he has married a Grandison.”

“My cousin Elenei, ser.”

“Then I am your cousin’s cousin, which is a very long way from anywhere.” He smiled slightly. It did not seem to take much of him. “I hope the Penroses were kind to you.”

“They were, ser.”

“They are, generally,” said Ser Lyman, “when it is useful.”

Lord Alaric said, “Walk with me, Lord Osmund. I came to ask a plain thing, and I would rather ask it plain.”

They went along the line of carts, and Orryn went three paces behind, and Ser Lyman a little behind him. A carter at the third cart touched his cap. The spearmen looked straight ahead.

“Who in the Reach would draw steel before the rain?”

“Not Ser Tom,” said Lord Osmund. “He drew it because he had nothing left. I mean the other sort.”

“I mean the other sort.”

“Peake and Tarly, if the king’s peace were a season shorter.” Lord Osmund did not hesitate. “Peake held the Marches for generations and lost them for backing the wrong claimant, and the Tarlys have been given them. They are polite to each other at feasts. Rowan and Redwyne, who have been at each other since before either of us was born, and have only been quiet because neither has had cause to be loud. And every lesser lord on the Mander who has a well and a neighbour. That is the whole Reach, Lord Alaric. It is a good country with a great many wells in it.”

“And Highgarden’s sons?”

“Ser Olyvar would ride to it,” said Lord Osmund. “Ser Moryn would talk it down, and be owed for the talking. Ser Garth would send grain. Of the three, I know which I should want in a dry year.”

“Lord Osmund,” said Ser Lyman, behind them, mildly.

“I said what I think, Lyman. I did not say what I should swear to.”

“Ser Moryn took my son,” said Alaric. “He feeds his friends.”

“He does.” Lord Osmund walked a few paces. “His friends have full barns and shut doors.”

“His friends,” said Ser Lyman carefully, “are good people to have as friends. They are not the Reach.”

They had come to the end of the line. Beyond the last cart the wall of the granary stood up, high and blind, and there was a door in it with two more guards at either side. A little way off along the wall, a crowd had gathered in the thin shade: women, mostly, and children, quiet, in a ragged line, with a cart at the head of it and a man in brown standing beside a great iron pot. Orryn could smell something that was not quite bread.

“Ser Olyvar’s riders ate Little Ford’s hay,” said Lord Alaric. “I was there.”

“Then you saw it,” said Lord Osmund. “A hundred riders eat what a village eats. A village in a dry year has nothing more to give, and if you quarter an army on it, it either starves or rises. Ser Olyvar is a fine man and a fine commander, and he has never had to feed an army in a drought.”

“And Ser Garth has?”

“Ser Garth has asked what the Reach can feed. He is the only one of them who has.” Lord Osmund turned and looked at him. “Look at the carts. Ten at a time, and two spears to a cart. No cart goes to a village that has not sent its reeve to ask for it. A man who rides in with his hat in his hand gets grain. A man who rides in with a sword does not. The grain is held here and goes out at a fixed share, and it goes in the open, so that every lord on the road can see it go.”

“It goes in the open,” said Alaric, “from Highgarden. And what the Reach cannot carry, the Arbor does.”

He said it lightly, but he had stopped walking. Ser Lyman’s face did not change, though it seemed to Orryn that something behind it moved a little.

“The Arbor has ships,” said Ser Lyman. “Ships are quicker than carts, when there is a river.”

“Will Oldtown let the Redwynes carry the grain of the Reach?”

It was a plain question, and it was a long time before anyone answered it. Ser Lyman looked at the ground. Then he lifted his head.

“Oldtown would not mind who carries it. It would mind who is seen to hold it.” He said it slowly, as a man lays a stone down. “My lord father owes his oath to Lord Jon. I owe Lord Leo my work. I have found that these are rarely asked of me on the same day.”

“And today?”

“Today I am asked about carts.”

Lord Alaric nodded, as though that were an answer, and Orryn thought that to him it was. “Lady Alys wants Blackbar’s girl for her son,” he said. “Ser Arron will want what his sister wants. You want her, I think, because she is Lord Jon’s granddaughter. Neither of you would say it is the same reason.”

“Ser Arron would say that the lady has excellent manners,” said Ser Lyman, dryly. “I should say that she is fifteen, and that she has not been asked.”

“It will be the heir’s father who decides,” said Lord Osmund.

“It will be the heir’s grandfather,” said Alaric, “and he has made up his mind to the other.”

Nobody contradicted him. Ser Lyman said, after a moment, “The king remembers the rebellion better than Lord Leo does. Oldtown has not been forgiven. Highgarden has, because Lord Leo is easy to forgive. Ser Garth will not be easy to forgive, because he does not ask.”

Orryn thought he had never heard anyone say so much in such a small voice.

“I have a daughter to marry,” said Alaric, “and a head full of what a wedding costs. And a brook that runs at night.”

“I have none to marry,” said Ser Lyman. “My wife and I have no children. I am told it is a freedom, by men who have five.”

It was gone before it had properly arrived. Orryn saw Lord Osmund glance at the younger man and look away, and he understood that it was not the first time it had been said and not the first time it had been left where it fell.

“Brother.”

It was the man in brown, come away from the iron pot with a wooden ladle still in one hand. He was shorter than Lord Osmund and softer, and the star at his neck swung when he walked, and his hands were cracked red from the pot.

“You said the well was the widow’s,” said Septon Emmon.

“It was the widow’s.”

“There are two hundred on Cress Hill and nine at the widow’s house.”

“And the grant says what it says, Emmon.”

“I have it in my mind that the grant was written when there was a brook.”

“A grant is not made less because the brook has failed.”

“Then who digs the well?” The septon’s voice stayed low. He did not look at Lord Alaric, or at anybody but his brother. “Ser Tom’s lord is at Goldengrove, Osmund, on the Mander, with a river at his door. He will not dig for a village on a hill. I know where they will go. They are in that line, and they come every morning.”

“Then Lord Rowan should be asked what he means to do for his own people,” said Lord Osmund. His hands had closed. “I will not rule that a house may be robbed of its water because the thief is thirsty.”

“I did not say you should.”

“You say it every time.”

“I say that you are right and that it is no use to anyone,” said Septon Emmon, and for a moment the two brothers looked at each other, with the same long upper lip, and then the septon turned back to his pot.

Lord Alaric had said nothing through all of it. He stood with his hands behind him and looked at the line of women in the shade.

“I hold a court,” he said, at last. “At Grassfield. There have been two such quarrels this summer. Two wells, two neighbours. I did not rule on either of them. I sent my own cart to the dry one, and told the other that I would remember it. I am not proud of that. The next man to come will want a cart too.”

“That,” said Ser Lyman, “is what Lord Leo does.”

“I know it.”

“Then you see the difficulty.”

“I see that I have a court and I will not give it to Highgarden,” said Alaric. “A lord’s court is the one thing he cannot hand over, and a lord who does is only a steward. But I will give you grain, Lord Osmund, when my brook fails, and I will send my men to guard your carts on my side of the road. And I will come to your hall when I ask for a judge, and I will mind what he says.” He paused. “I would sooner have a fair judge than a kind lord. I had not known I thought it until I watched you this morning.”

“That is a good deal more than most would give,” said Lord Osmund.

“Which side are you on, Lord Meadows?”

It was asked without heat, as a man might ask the hour. Orryn found he was waiting for the answer as he had waited for the knife.

“My own,” said Alaric. “It is a small side.”

“It has few friends.”

“It has Grassfield,” said Alaric. “That is a good many mouths.”

Lord Osmund did not smile. But he gave a short nod, as if he had put a mark against something that was satisfactory, and turned and walked on down the line of carts, with the grey cloak still on his arm.

*

Matthos was in Ser Moryn’s stable yard with a stirrup leather across his knees, picking at a stitch that had gone. He did not look up when Orryn came in, though Orryn knew he had seen him.

Orryn had been given a small leather purse by Lord Alaric, and a message, and told to find his brother. He had thought about what he would say all the way across the yard and had arrived with nothing.

“Lord Alaric sent this.”

“What is it?”

“Coin. For what a squire needs.”

“I’ve what a squire needs.” But Matthos took it, and weighed it in his palm, and put it in his belt. “He sent you to see I’m not crying.”

“He sent me to see you’re fed.”

“I’m fed.” He bent over the stirrup leather again. “I’m better fed than at Grassfield. Nobody watches what I eat.”

It was not true, Orryn thought, or not all of it; at Grassfield nobody had watched what Matthos ate either. But there was a way a boy said a thing like that, and it was not an invitation to argue. Matthos was thirteen, or just fourteen, and he sat very straight on the mounting block in Moryn’s green, and he stitched like a boy who had been shown how and was determined to be better at it than the man who had shown him.

“Hold this,” he said, and held out the leather.

Orryn held it. Matthos threaded the needle and drew it through, and for a time neither of them said anything. A groom crossed the yard with a bucket. Somewhere in the house a door slammed, and there was a woman’s laugh, and it stopped.

“Is the grey mare still lame?” said Matthos.

“Mostly. The groom says it’s the near fore.”

“It’s always the near fore.” He pulled the stitch tight and bit the thread. “You’ll tell my mother I’m well.”

“I’ll tell her.”

“Tell her I’m better than well.” He held out his hand for the leather, and Orryn gave it up. “And tell Elyce she’s not to ride Brindle. She can’t hold him, and she’ll say she can.”

“I’ll tell her.”

“Good,” said Matthos, and then, because there was nothing more to say and neither of them was able to say it, he stood up and put the leather over his shoulder and went across the yard. He did not look back. Orryn stood where he was with his hand still partly open, and after a while he put it in his belt, and went to find the gate.

*

They left at dawn, by the north road, with the escort strung out behind them and the dust already coming up from the hooves. Orryn had slept badly and woken sooner than he meant to, and he rode with his eyes half closed and his shoulders still stiff from the lance. Lord Alaric rode ahead, bareheaded, saying nothing, with one hand on his thigh.

The bridge was a quarter-day out of Highgarden, a low stone arch over a brook that had shrunk to a ribbon of green mud. A chain was drawn across the road at the near end. Beside it, on a stool, sat a thin knight in a patched surcoat with a bow across his knees and four men behind him, and on the road before the chain a cart had stopped.

It was a plain farm cart with a plain driver and four sacks on the bed, and beside it stood two of Highgarden’s spearmen in green, who had plainly been sent along as guards and now seemed to wish they had been sent elsewhere. The driver was a young man in a cap. He had a copper in his hand and he was holding it out as if it were a bird that might fly.

“A silver stag a cart,” said the thin knight. He did not get up. “It is the same for grain as for wine. I did not make the bridge or the price.”

“It’s for the village,” said the driver. “Reeve sent me to Highgarden for it. I’ve nothing but this.”

“Lord Leo’s grain pays no toll,” said one of the spearmen. His voice shook a little.

“Lord Leo is not at my bridge,” said the knight.

Orryn saw one of the bridge-men put a hand to his sword, and one of the spearmen move his weight. It was a very quiet sort of thing. Nobody shouted. Nobody drew. There was only the brook below them, the dust settling, and the two sets of men looking at one another across the chain, each waiting for the other to be the first to make it into something worse.

Lord Alaric rode forward at a walk. He did not hurry. He reined in beside the chain and looked down at the knight on his stool, and then at the driver, and the knight, seeing the quality of his horse and the cut of his coat, got up.

“It is a stag, my lord,” he said. “It is my right.”

“I know it is.” Lord Alaric put his hand in his belt, took out a silver stag, and held it out. “I will pay it.”

The knight took it. He looked at it, and at Lord Alaric, and at the cart and its four sacks. He seemed uncertain whether he had won or lost.

“Let it over,” said Alaric.

The chain was lowered. The driver slapped the reins, and the cart went forward over the stones with the two spearmen walking beside it. The young man looked back once, at the lord on the bay, and then at his hands, and did not seem to know what to do with either.

“I am Alaric Meadows of Grassfield,” said Lord Alaric, to the knight. “If the Lord Justiciar asks who paid the toll on a hungry man’s grain, tell him I did, and that I thought it a poor bargain. It is your right, ser. I would not take it from you. But I would not have you strand a village at a bridge for the price of a night’s ale.”

He did not wait for an answer. He put the bay forward and crossed, and Orryn went after him with his heart hammering, and found when he was on the far side that he had been holding the reins so tightly that his knuckles were white.

“Why did you pay?” he said, after a while.

“Because it was his right.” Alaric did not turn around. “And because there were ten men with their hands on their swords, and I did not wish to make it worse. And because it cost a stag.” He rode on a little. “It’s what Lord Leo would have done, Grandison. I do not like that I thought of it so quickly.”

Orryn waited, but that was all. They rode on in the thin early light, with the dust going up behind them and the road running pale and straight between the dry fields. Far back, across the brook, he could hear the cart’s wheels on the stone. Then he could not hear them, and after a while there was only the sound of the horses.
