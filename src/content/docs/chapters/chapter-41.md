By the second hour after the dawn drill every mattress in Grassfield Keep had been carried out into the sun.

They lay along the south wall of the lower yard in a long humped row, straw ticks in blue and brown and every shade of old, and Orryn counted thirty-one of them and Perkin Fenwick counted thirty-three. They argued about it for a quarter of an hour, with their sleeves rolled and sweat running into their eyes, until Baldric Haye walked down the row touching each one with the toe of his boot and it came to thirty-one. Perkin said Baldric had skipped two. Baldric said that if Perkin could find them he was welcome to them. The ticks smelled of mice and of last winter, and when the squires beat them with ash-rods the dust came up in slow brown clouds and settled on every wet neck in the yard.

Ser Tobin Whitlow had been put in charge of the beating. Orryn could only suppose that Lord Alaric had chosen him because he would stand still for it. Ser Tobin was thirty, a household knight, second son of a landed family in the Reach, and Perkin's master. He had golden-brown curls and easy lines at the corners of his eyes, a handsome face that always looked a little slept in, and very fine boots, which he had crossed at the ankle on top of an upturned barrel in the one strip of shade along the wall. He held a cup of small beer on his knee. He had not yet used his voice for anything except advice.

"Fenwick. You are beating that one as if it owed you money."

"It does owe me money, ser. It's the one I slept on my first night here."

"Then it has paid you in fleas. Harder, boy. Think of your lady."

"I haven't got a lady."

"Think of one anyway. It helps the wrist."

Matthos Meadows was beating the next tick along, with his jaw set and a dead look about the eyes. He was thirteen, his father's heir, tall for his age, with light brown hair stuck flat to his forehead and a long scratch down one forearm that he had earned, so the yard said, from his own dog. His mother had sent him down to the yard that morning with a note, and the note had said that he was to work with the squires until noon. Piers Fallow was working beside him. They had not spoken since the first tick, and they beat in a kind of savage unison, each flinching a little whenever the other's rod came near.

It was the smell that did it. Orryn had put his face too close to the tick he was turning, and the hot straw came up at him, sour and sweet, and for a moment he was eight years old in a doorway at Grandview with the sun coming through the shutters and his grandmother's voice going over his head to somebody else. No chamber stands unaired more than a week. She had been looking at a bed with the covers drawn up flat and white, and the look on her face had made the maid go red to the ears. A house that cannot air a bed cannot keep a promise. He had understood perhaps half of it. He had kept all of it, the way a boy keeps a stone.

"My grandmother used to say that a house which can't air a bed can't keep a promise," he said. He had said it to Piers, who was nearest, and Piers gave him a look that took in the whole of his dusty face and said nothing. But Marya Caswell had come out of the buttery door with a basket of folded linen on her hip, and she had stopped.

"Say that again, Master Grandison."

"It's only something she said. Lady Ellyn. My grandmother. That no room should stand a week unaired."

"Lord Alaric's aunt."

"Yes."

Marya looked at him for longer than he liked. She was thirty, narrow-faced, in a plain cap, with a way of standing very still while she listened that made people say more than they had meant to. She was Lady Jeyne's cousin, poor and Caswell-born, and Lady Jeyne's companion, and Orryn had noticed that whatever was said in front of her reached the lady's ear within the hour. Often it was repeated better than it had been said.

"It is a very good rule," said Marya, and went on across the yard.

Orryn went back to the tick. He had the feeling of having dropped something small into a deep well and heard nothing.

Lyman Verrall came down the yard a little before noon with a ledger under one arm and a face already shiny from the stair. He stopped at the end of the row of mattresses and looked at them with real pleasure.

"Thirty-one," he said. "Good. I had thirty-three in the book, but the book is often wrong." He caught Orryn's eye. "Master Orryn. I am afraid the chest must keep. Lady Jeyne has taken me for the whole day, and the better part of tomorrow, to count candles, Seven help me. Nobody has ever counted candles in this keep, and she wishes to know what a guest costs by the hour."

"Yes, Master Verrall."

"After noon, the day after. Or the day after that. It has waited ten years; it will not be offended."

He went away up the yard. Orryn found that he was relieved, and was ashamed of it a moment afterward, and then found that Baldric had stopped beating, three ticks along, and was looking at him with the rod resting across his shoulder.

"Tomorrow," said Baldric. He did not make it a question.

"Or the day after."

"Yes," Baldric said, and began to beat again, steadily, with his long face turned down to the straw.

*

The summons came at noon, when the first bread and cheese had been carried out to the wall, and it came from Marya, who stood at the end of the row with her hands folded and said that Lady Jeyne would have the squires to carry. She said which of them. It was Orryn, Alyn, Perkin, Piers and Baldric. Osric, she said, was to be spared, and it was said as one speaks of an invalid. Matthos was not mentioned at all, and he put down his rod with the air of a boy who has been given something for nothing, and went off toward the stables. No one called him back.

The best guest chamber lay above the great hall, and the stair to it was narrow and steep and turned twice, and Orryn learned in the first minute that a bed is a stupid thing to carry. This one was the great bed that Lady Odessa would sleep in, a four-posted oak frame that had stood in pieces in the loft since the winter, and every piece of it was heavier than a man and more awkward than a horse. The posts were carved with pears, climbing and leafing round the shafts to a finial at the top, and he was holding one of them against his chest with both arms and thinking, with a clarity born of heat, that the pears were doing it on purpose. Alyn had the other end. He walked backward up the stair in front of him, grinning, with his hair in his eyes.

"Left," said Alyn. "Your left. No, your other left. That's my foot."

"Then take it away."

"It's attached to me. I'm very fond of it."

The chamber, when they reached it, had been stripped. The rushes were gone and the floor had been scrubbed with sand and vinegar until the boards were grey, and all three windows stood open on the yard with their shutters thrown back. A hearth took up one wall. It had a clean-swept look, and a stack of dry herbs in the basket beside it, lavender and southernwood, ready to be burned when the time came. Orryn put his end of the post down with a sound of relief and found that Lady Jeyne Meadows was in the room.

She stood in the middle of the floor, a slender woman in a pale green gown, a veil of gauze over her hair and a gold chain across her breast. She was Lord Alaric's wife, a Caswell of Bitterbridge, and she was twenty-eight, and she looked it only around the eyes. It was said of her that she had never been heard to raise her voice. Orryn had the idea that this was because she had never needed to. Septa Lenora stood a little behind her with her hands folded in her sleeves, and Elyce sat in the window seat with a hound puppy asleep in her lap, which was the thing Matthos had said had eaten her ribbon. It had the look of an animal that had eaten a great deal more than that and was now being rewarded for it.

"The foot to the wall," said Lady Jeyne. "No, that wall. Thank you, Master Merryweather."

They put it up. There was a long time of kneeling and swearing in undertones and a hammer, and Baldric, who was handy, went round the joints with a mallet while Alyn held the headboard and Perkin was sent for the ropes. Piers carried the bolsters one at a time up the stair and did not once look at any of the others. Lady Jeyne watched without interference, and told them where the ropes should go, and moved the bed a hand's breadth when it was done, and had them move it back. It was only afterward, when the first great mattress had been brought up and laid across the ropes and she had run her hand along it, that Leonette came in at the door.

She came in quick and a little too bright. Her ribbon today was yellow. She had a book under one arm and a sharp look at the bed.

"Is that for Lady Odessa?"

"It is," said her mother.

"And where is Wylla to sleep?"

There was a small silence. Septa Lenora's gaze went to the ceiling. Marya, in the doorway behind Leonette with her linen, did not move at all. Orryn picked up the mallet that Baldric had put down, for something to do with his hands.

"With you, sweetling," said Lady Jeyne. "In your chamber. I had meant to tell you tonight."

"In my bed?"

"In the small bed. Marya will have it moved in from the west wardrobe. You will have the large one, since you are the daughter of the house."

"But I don't want her in my chamber."

"I did not suppose that you did."

"She'll go through my things. She did it the last time she came. She went through the chest while I was at the sept."

"Leonette."

"She did! And she will tell her mother everything I have, and her mother will tell all of Longtable, and then they'll tell the Fossoways—"

"That is enough."

It was not said loudly. Lady Jeyne had not moved her hand from the mattress. But Leonette stopped as though it had been, and the whole room went with her, the hammer and the shuffle of feet and even the puppy, which woke up and looked about it.

"A young lady shares what she is asked to share," said Septa Lenora, from behind. It was dry and quite without heat.

"I'm not asking her to love her, Leonette," said Lady Jeyne. She said it more gently, and it was nearly worse. "I am asking you to give her the small bed for a fortnight. There is nothing else to be done. Her mother will have the chamber and Ser Androw Ashford has the gatehouse, and I will not put a girl of fifteen in with the maids."

"Put her in with Elyce."

"I'll sleep with Wylla!" said Elyce, brightly, from the window.

"You will not," said Lady Jeyne, without looking round. "You are twelve."

Leonette's face had gone a hot, dark red that reached to the edge of her hair. She said, quite quietly, "Yes, Mother," and went out, and her slippers could be heard going away down the stair at a pace she would not have been allowed in the hall. The silence she left behind her was thin and uncomfortable. Elyce hid her face in the puppy's neck, but not before Orryn had seen her mouth, which was not sorry at all.

"Master Grandison."

He turned too quickly, and the mallet went with him. "My lady."

"Marya tells me that your grandmother had a rule about airing rooms."

He felt the others look at him, Alyn with his eyebrows up, Perkin over the rope, even Baldric. "Yes, my lady. Lady Ellyn. She said that no chamber should stand a week unaired."

"Lord Alaric's aunt. I never met her. My husband says she frightened him until he was twenty." Lady Jeyne's expression did not change. It remained, as far as Orryn could judge, perfectly pleasant. "It is a remarkable thing that his own wife should have it from his own squire, and in this house. Say it as she said it, Master Grandison, if you can. I should like to have it exact."

He said it. He heard his own voice go flat and careful, as it did when he recited. A house that cannot air a bed cannot keep a promise. It seemed a very small thing in that room, with the sun on the bare boards.

"Yes," said Lady Jeyne. "I think I shall have it written over the buttery door. Lady Ellyn's rule. We have been much too careless in this house, and I cannot think why none of us had it before." She turned her face to the window. "The shutters stay open from this hour until the Merryweathers come. All the chambers in the east range, Marya, beginning tomorrow. The sun in every room."

"Yes, my lady."

That was all. They were dismissed. Orryn went down the stair behind Alyn with a feeling in his chest that he could not name, in which pleasure and an obscure unease were so closely knotted that he could not have told which was which. At the bottom Alyn stopped on the last step and let the others go by.

"Careful," he said, low.

"Of what?"

"Her. She's kind. She's been kind to me for a year and a half, and I've never once come away from it without feeling I owed something." Alyn scratched his neck. "She never takes a gift she can't spend later. That's all. I'd not say anything more to Marya, if I were you."

"I only said what my grandmother said."

"I know you did." Alyn clapped him on the arm, quite lightly, and went down. "That's what I mean."

*

Leonette was sitting on the window-seat at the head of the east passage with her knees drawn up and her face turned to the glass, and she did not turn when Orryn came by with an armful of folded sheets. She had her slippers off. Her book lay face down beside her, and she was pulling a thread from the hem of her sleeve, not quickly.

He would have gone past. He had no wish to be spoken to, and she was Lord Alaric's daughter, and sulking, and fourteen. But she said, "Grandison," without turning her head, and he stopped.

"My lady."

"Did you ever see her? Lady Wylla."

"No, my lady. Not yet."

"She told the whole table at the last feast that in the Stormlands they eat bread with their fingers." The thread came loose in her fingers. "That was to Ser Garlan's mother. At the lord's own table. And then she said that she had meant it kindly, and that she was told the honey was very good."

He did not know what to say to that. It was the first time anyone at Grassfield had said it to him as if it were a thing a person could take offence at, and he found that the idea sat oddly. He had eaten bread with his fingers all his life, as had everyone he knew. Still, he supposed that was different.

"And Mother knows. She was sitting beside her." Leonette turned her head at last, and her eyes were bright and a little red. "She smiled. She said that Lady Wylla was very observant."

"That was courteous," said Orryn, uncertainly.

"Courteous!" She almost laughed. "Yes. That is exactly what it was."

He was aware that he had not been told all of it. A thing told so exactly, with the Fossoway mother in the right place and the honey in the right place, has usually been told before, and polished. He remembered that Alyn had said Wylla was all right once you knew her, and that he had said it in the voice of someone who has said it before and is no longer sure. Leonette watched him decide to believe her, and he had the uncomfortable idea that she knew the moment it happened.

"Is somebody being a scold to a lady?"

Alyn had come up the passage behind him with a wooden bed-rail on his shoulder. He set the end of it down on the floor, which was a mistake, because it rolled, and Orryn caught it. Leonette's face did something quick and complicated. It put itself in order before he could say what.

"I am not being scolded. I am being wronged. It's different."

"I beg your pardon. A lady being wronged in a passage. I'll fetch a knight."

"You'd be no use."

"I should be a very great use. I'd stand beside the knight and look sympathetic." He sat on the end of the window-seat, a careful yard from her, and Orryn saw him glance once up the passage and once down it. "Your mother has put my sister in your chamber."

"Your sister takes the whole bed."

"My sister takes the whole of anything she is in. It's the way she was made." He seemed about to say something more and then did not. "It's a fortnight. Marya will keep her on her side with a bolster."

"I won't speak to her."

"You will, because you'll be dying to know what she thinks of your gown."

Leonette drew a sharp breath, and Alyn put up a hand.

"Which is a very good gown. Green. As discussed."

She looked at him a moment longer, and then she turned her face back to the glass, but it was a different turning. "You must dance the first one with me."

"The first is Ser Androw's and Wylla's, by every rule there is."

"Then the second."

"The second is Ser Garlan's, if he comes."

"He won't come for moons yet."

"Then the second is yours." Alyn said it easily, but he was looking at the carved rail in his hand and not at her. "The third I've promised Elyce, and it's a thing I have to do, or she will cry on your mother's skirts for a week and you'll be blamed for it."

"She will cry anyway."

"Then I'll have been warned." He got up, the rail on his shoulder again. "Grandison here is my witness. Aren't you?"

Orryn had been standing with his arms full of sheets, trying to be a post. He said, "Yes," and heard how it sounded.

"There. He's a very reliable witness. He counts." Alyn raised the rail in a kind of salute, to nobody in particular. "One dance, Lady Leonette, at the feast, the second. Do not tell your mother or she'll have it written over a door."

She laughed then, short and reluctant, with her chin on her knees. When Alyn had gone on down the passage whistling something Orryn did not know, she took up her book again without opening it, and said to the window, "You may go too, Grandison."

He went. At the turn of the stair he looked back once, and she was not reading. She was sitting with the page flat under her hand, looking at the place where Alyn had been. Orryn knew that look. He did not care to wonder how. Marya Caswell, who had come up the other stair with her basket, stood halfway along the passage in the plain light of the window and was looking at her.

*

He had never been in the linen room. It was down three steps from the east passage, under the eaves, and he found it by the sound of a sharp, dry voice saying, "Fold it in thirds. Thirds, Osric. You are folding it as though it were a map."

It was a long low attic with a single window, and it smelled of cedar and of lavender gone to dust. The shelves ran from floor to rafter on both walls, stacked with sheets and bolsters and towels, and on a wide table in the middle of it Septa Lenora and her nephew stood either side of a heap of pale linen, folding. The septa wore her grey veil drawn close and her crystal on her breast. She had a way of folding that was almost a rite, edge to edge, flat to the table, and every sheet she finished sat as square as a stone.

Osric's sat a little less so. He had taken off his doublet, and his shirt-sleeves were rolled to the elbow on arms that looked too thin for him, and his fair hair had gone limp with sweat. But he had colour in his face for the first time in days. He was folding the way he did everything, with great exactness and no ease.

"Master Grandison," said the septa, not looking up. "Marya sent you?"

"She said you wanted the sheets, Septa."

"Put them there. No. There. We have not room for another thing on the left."

He put them down. Osric glanced at him, nodded once, and bent to his work again. For a time there was only the whisper of cloth. The heat in the attic was a thick, dry, patient thing, like the heat inside a baker's oven after the loaves are out, and Orryn found that he did not want to leave it.

"You are well?" he said.

"I am quite well."

"Maester Perrin says he may walk to the yard on his own," said the septa, "and sit in the shade. He may not run, or carry, or be shouted at. That is the maester's word, not mine. I should say he might do all three, and be the better for it, but I am not a maester." She squared a corner with the flat of her hand. "He has been eating. Do you hear me, Osric? I said you were eating."

"I heard, Aunt."

"Then say so."

"I am eating."

It was nothing, an old woman nagging a boy. But Orryn had seen her at the dancing with the child Elyce, and again in the yard with the ribbon, and he had never once heard her voice go quite so low. She picked a sheet from the heap and shook it out and held it up to the light of the window, looking for holes, and as she did it she put her free hand for a moment against the back of Osric's head. He did not seem to notice. She took it away.

"I must see the girls at their lesson," she said. "Fold the rest as I showed you. Master Grandison, you may stay if you wish to be of use. Otherwise, go and be of use elsewhere." She went out with her sleeves tucked into her belt and her back as straight as a plank.

Osric folded two more sheets before he spoke. "Has he said anything?"

Orryn did not need to be told who. "No. Nothing. I'd have told you."

"You said you would."

"I did say. I will."

"It has been nine days." Osric put the folded sheet on the pile and squared it. His face did not change. "Ser Lucan has not spoken of it. He told me to rest. One cannot ask a knight whether he has said anything to the lord, so I have not asked." He held the corner of the next sheet very still. "I was ill, and I fell. It was the sun. That is all it was."

"Lord Alaric spoke to me, you know. In the cold cellar. He did not speak of it. He asked whether I had been given enough water."

"Had you?"

"That is not the point." But the corner of his mouth moved, a very little. "I said I had. He said that was a lie, and that he would have the cellar boy whipped if it was not true by supper. I said it was true." He folded the sheet in half, in half again. "It was not entirely."

Orryn laughed before he could help it, and Osric looked at him, offended and not offended, in a manner that was oddly like his aunt's. Orryn took a sheet from the heap and began to fold it. It was not easy. His thirds came out in halves, and his halves in a lump, and after a time Osric put out a hand, without saying anything, and turned his corners for him.

They folded for most of an hour. They did not say much. At the end of it the heap had become a tidy white wall, and Orryn's hands smelled of lavender and old cloth and the sweat of the work, and when he went down the three steps into the glare of the east passage he found that his shoulders had come down from round his ears for the first time since the morning.

*

Lord Alaric came into the great chamber in the late afternoon, in his riding boots, with dust to the knees and a long green stain down one side of his shirt. Orryn, who had been sent up with the last of the bolsters and had been standing in the corner trying to find a place to put them, saw him stop in the doorway and look the room over, once, from the scrubbed boards to the ropes and the mattress, the way he had looked along the line of the yard on the day Orryn first came.

"It's a bed," he said.

"It is the best bed in the keep," said Lady Jeyne, from the window. She was standing with Marya, going down a list, and she did not turn. "It will have sheets on it presently."

"It will have a very great many." He crossed the floor in four strides, sat on the edge of the mattress with a creak of the ropes, and then, before anyone could stop him, lay straight back on it with his boots on and his arms thrown out. "Seven hells, that's good. Alyn, I forgave you for the pears."

"Alaric," said his wife.

"I am testing it. If Lady Odessa goes through the ropes I shall have to hear of it for a moon." He lay and looked at the beams. "It's cool. How is it cool?"

"Because it faces the north-east, and the shutters have been open since noon. And because your aunt had a rule about airing rooms." Lady Jeyne turned at last, and her face was entirely pleasant. "Master Grandison had it from her. Did you know she had it?"

"Aunt Ellyn?" Alaric lifted his head and found Orryn in the corner. "She had a rule about everything. She had the whole west range aired one winter, every shutter open, with snow on the sills." He put his head back down. "I have never been so cold in my life. She said it was good for me. What was the rule, Orryn?"

He said it. It was the third time, and it came more easily, and it sounded shorter.

"That's the one," said Alaric. "That's the very one. She said it to everyone." He closed his eyes. "It's a good rule. Put it over a door, if you like. I should be happier with some peace in this house than a rule over a door, but there it is." He opened one eye. "Is anyone going to want me for anything? I've three miles of fence to look at in the morning and Hob Marsh's boy has found a dead ewe in the brook."

"No one wants you for anything," said Lady Jeyne. "It's the thing that distinguishes you."

"Good." He did not move. "Wake me for supper."

She went on with her list. Marya, at her elbow, did not look up either, but as Orryn laid the bolsters on the end of the bed and slipped out of the door he thought he saw the lady's mouth turn, only a very little, at one corner, and then smooth itself out. It was a moment so quick and so private that he was not certain he had seen it, and when he looked again she was frowning at the number of candles.

*

They carried the mattresses in at dusk. By then the straw had baked to the temperature of a loaf, and every tick was stiff and light and smelled of nothing but the sun. Perkin carried two at once, one on each shoulder, and said between heaves that Ser Tobin was in a rare good humour and had lost only twice at dice the night before. Orryn said nothing. As far as the yard knew, Perkin was still the boy who had ruined Ser Tobin's boots. Orryn was walking behind him, looking at the back of his neck, which was freckled and peeling and very young, and he felt the old weight of it settle in his belly and sit there. Alyn passed the other way with a rolled tick on his head, whistling, and did not look at either of them.

The sun was down behind the western range. The air in the yard was still hot, and gold, and full of dust that did not settle. Up the slope, in the dark of the lower fields, someone was calling to a dog. He heard Hob Marsh's boy shouting, a long way off, somewhere up along the brook where he had found his ewe, and then the crickets began in the hedge below the wall, as they did every dusk, and went on until the whole yard seemed to hum with them.

He took his tick up to the squires' hall and laid it on the frame by the wall. It was beside Alyn's. It smelled of the sun and of nothing else, no person, not even himself, and he lay down on it in his shirt before the others had come, with the shutters open on the yard, and listened to the crickets. He had not known that a bed could smell of nothing. He thought he would remember it, and he was asleep before he could decide why.
