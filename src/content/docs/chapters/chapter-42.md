Lord Alaric was in the saddle before the shadows of the keep had finished crossing the yard. He had Hob Marsh's youngest, Jory, beside him on a borrowed pony, to show the place where the ewe had been found, and he took the gate at a trot with his sleeves already dark from the sweat of the first hour. Orryn, holding the stirrup of the horse he was not to ride, was told in passing that his lordship would be back by supper or by the time the fence had been walked, whichever came later, and that Lady Jeyne had asked for him.

"For me, my lord?"

"For a squire. I said you would do." Alaric gathered the reins. "She is counting chairs. It is a thing a person can be killed by, in a house like this one. Go and see that it isn't you." He rode out. Alyn, who had not been asked for, sat his own horse beside the gate with an expression of deep and cheerful surprise at his good fortune, and followed.

It was cooler in the great hall than anywhere else in Grassfield, and Orryn had come to think that this was because the hall had been built by men who understood that a lord's guests will forgive a poor roof and a thin supper, but not a sweating dais. It was a long room, high-beamed, with the stone floor swept bare of rushes and the windows on the yard side flung open. Down the middle the trestles had been stacked against the wall like the ribs of some great beast. At the upper end the dais stood three steps above the floor, and on it, in a bar of white light from the south window, Marya Caswell was kneeling with a knotted cord in her hands.

"Master Grandison. You are tall enough for the far end. Take the cord and stand where I show you."

He took it and stood. Marya measured from his boots to the edge of the board, wrote something on the tablet at her knee, and sent him four paces to the left to be measured again. It went on for some time. He had not been told what the numbers were for, and he had learned in the east passage that Marya Caswell did not volunteer such things. She asked a question, wrote down the answer, and asked another. He found himself enjoying it. A measured thing could be got right.

The ladies came in together a little before the second hour. Lady Jeyne walked ahead with a roll of paper in her hand, and behind her came Septa Lenora in her plain grey veil with the crystal swinging against her breast, and behind the septa came Maester Perrin, who was Grassfield's maester and the keeper of its books. He was a thin, stooped man of fifty or so, with a long nose and a grey beard trimmed to a point and a chain of many metals that rattled a little when he moved. His fingers were stained to the second knuckle with ink. It was Perrin who had taught Orryn the arms of the Reach two days gone, shield by painted shield, for the better part of a morning, and who had not once raised his voice.

"Nine at the high board," said Lady Jeyne. She did not look at the dais as she said it. She looked at the paper. "It seats nine, and I should like it to seat nine and no more, because when it seats ten the tenth sits with his elbow in the sauce. Lord Alaric in the middle. Lady Odessa on his right hand."

"Naturally," said Septa Lenora.

"And I on his left, which leaves six."

"Lady Wylla," said the septa. "Then Ser Androw Ashford, her promised husband, and the septon, and Leonette, for she is the daughter of the house and may not be seated with the children. That is four, and two chairs over."

"Two chairs," said Lady Jeyne, "for Ser Lucan, Ser Bertram, Ser Wyman, Ser Tobin, Ser Everard, the maester, and my son." She said it without any particular emphasis. "The septon sits by Lady Odessa."

"The septon sits at the right hand of the Seven, my lady, wherever he is put. It is Ser Androw Ashford who must be put somewhere." Septa Lenora had a way of speaking that made every remark sound like a verse from a book of hours. "He is an adult knight and the heir of a lordly house, not merely a visiting household knight. His father's sister married Ser Moryn Tyrell. That will matter to him, whether we wish it to or not."

"And he comes as Lady Wylla's promised husband," said Perrin, mildly. He had taken a small painted sheet from inside his sleeve and was turning it over. It showed a white sun above a chevron on a field of orange, and Orryn knew it before it had finished turning, for there are two orange fields in the Reach that a boy may meet on a road, and this was the one with the sun. "At twenty-nine, already knighted, he cannot be treated as a boy of the squires' board. Put him beside Lord Alaric and he has a fitting place for his station, but the two noble ladies may be parted from their daughter and guest. Put him farther down and you must account for it to Ashford. There is no rule that seats nine people without anyone's pride taking up more room than his chair." He glanced at the paper. "I could find you three examples of either arrangement, my lady, and each of them would have ended in a quarrel."

"I do not want an example. I want a chair." Lady Jeyne turned the paper in her hand, and then, as though it had occurred to her only that moment, she looked up and found Orryn on the dais with the cord in his fist. "Master Grandison. How many sat at the high board at your cousin's wedding?"

He had not expected to be addressed at all. "At Grandview, my lady?"

"At Grandview. At the feast. Marya tells me you were put in charge of the lodging."

"Only of the lodging." He saw Marya's pen stop and go on. "Nine, my lady. It was the same board as this one, or near it. My uncle sat in the middle, with Lady Alysanne Penrose at his right hand and Lady Alys Musgood at his left. Lady Alysanne was a Hightower by birth, and she had come the farthest. Then my aunt Lynesse, and my cousin Elenei, who was the bride, and Ser Addam beside her, and—" He stopped. He could see the board quite clearly, the white cloth and the spilled salt and Ser Addam saying nothing. The number was nine, but the faces would not come in order. "I counted, my lady. I do not remember where everyone was."

"But you remember that it was nine."

"Yes."

"And Lady Alysanne was content."

It was Lady Jeyne's tone that stopped him. It was exactly as pleasant as before. He thought of Lady Alysanne in the passage with the torches behind her, saying You counted well, Master Grandison in a voice as cold as a well-bucket. He had told no one at Grassfield about that, and he did not think Alyn had either.

"She was content with the table, my lady," he said. "It was the room she minded."

"Ah. Yes. That I had heard." Lady Jeyne lowered her eyes to her paper. "Thank you."

It was the moment, he understood later, at which he should have been quiet. He was not. He had a thought, and it had the clean, solid shape of an answer to a sum, and he wished to put it down on the table where she could see it.

"My lady, there is also the cupbearing. I mean for the dais. Alyn and I pour for Lord Alaric every night. If Lady Odessa comes, then she will have her own son at her chair, and I think that must please her. And if we serve together as we always do, nobody could say that it was a slight to anyone."

There was a short pause. Septa Lenora's eyes moved from him to Lady Jeyne and back, and Maester Perrin cleared his throat and began to examine the end of his sleeve.

"Lady Odessa keeps a list," said the septa, as though it were a fact about the weather. "Everyone knows that. She has kept it since before her wedding. It is not written down. It is in her head, and it is long, and it is correct. She will look for her son's place before she looks for her own." She said it quite without malice. "Whether it is a slight to put her son behind a chair with a jug, Master Grandison, is not a matter of what he is used to."

"I only meant—"

"I am sure that you did," said Lady Jeyne. She had not raised her eyes from the paper. "It is a kind thought. I shall bear it in mind." And then, to Marya, with no change in her voice at all, "Write that down."

Marya wrote. Orryn stood on the dais with the cord in his hand. He did not understand what he had done, only that he had done something, and that somewhere in the last minute a door had opened a little way and been closed again very quietly. He looked at the pen. It went across the tablet in short neat strokes, as if it wrote a list of candles.

"Ser Lucan," said Lady Jeyne, "will have the squires at noon to teach them the service. Septa, if you would see that Elyce is not under the board. Master Grandison." She smiled at him, not unkindly. "You may put down the cord."

*

Ser Lucan Marwick came into the hall at noon with the exactness of a man who has been told the hour and distrusts the sun.

He was thirty-eight. He was the eldest son of a small landed house in the Reach, and the master of Osric Dunmere, and the only household knight at Grassfield who wore his sword to the hall as though it were a vestment. He was tall and square-jawed, with cropped dark hair that left his ears bare, and he stood with his chest very slightly forward, like a man who has been told once in boyhood how a knight stands and has never found cause to alter it. His armour was kept in perfect order. His boots were not fine. They were only clean, and Orryn found that he preferred them to his face.

The squires stood before the dais in a line, from the eldest to the youngest, as he had ordered. That put Alyn at the left, who had come back from the fence scorched across the nose, and Osric next, a little unsteady, in a clean shirt with his sleeves buttoned to the wrist in spite of the heat. Then Baldric, Perkin, Piers and Orryn, and at the end of the line a hand's breadth outside it, Matthos Meadows, who had been sent by his mother and was holding himself as stiff as a pillar. A table had been set at the foot of the dais with a white cloth, six empty cups, an ewer, a loaf, a dish of salt, and a joint of cold mutton on a board. It was all for show. Nothing in the hall smelled of food.

"You will serve at the high board when the guests are here," said Ser Lucan. "There are nine seats and three servers. Lord Alaric has told me that he does not require ceremony. That is his lordship's right. It is not the guests'." His voice was quite level. It was neither loud nor unkind, and it carried to the back of the hall without any appearance of effort. "Lady Odessa Merryweather has been served in her own hall by men who were taught to serve. She will see what is done here. It will be done correctly."

He demonstrated. It took the better part of an hour. A cup was not carried but offered, in both hands, the rim turned away from the guest and the foot toward him. The ewer was held low, so that the lip did not show above a lord's shoulder. A squire stood behind the chair, not beside it, with his hands where they could be seen, and his eyes on the wall, and did not speak except to say my lord or my lady or the name of the dish. He did not laugh. If a guest spoke to him, he answered, and if the guest said something clever, he did not appear to have heard.

"Grandison."

Orryn stepped out of the line. The ewer weighed more than he had thought, even empty, and the handle was slick. He took the cup from the table in his right hand and held out the ewer, and he felt, with a sinking certainty, that the whole motion looked exactly as it was: the motion of a boy carrying a bucket across a yard.

"You hold it as though you meant to drown a cat in it."

"Yes, ser."

"It is a Stormlands habit. I do not say it against you." Ser Lucan said it in the tone of a man reading out a measurement. "They pour at Storm's End as they ride, I am told, which is to say quickly, and with one hand. It does very well for a hall of men who are drunk. Do it again."

He did it again. He did it five times. By the fifth his palms were wet and the back of his neck was hot, and Ser Lucan had stopped saying anything, which was worse. From the left of the line he heard Alyn make a small noise in his throat that might have been a cough.

"Merryweather."

"Ser."

"You will not jest in a hall with a cup in your hand."

"I did not jest, ser. I coughed."

"Then you will cough in the yard." Ser Lucan did not smile. He did not appear to have heard the thing as a joke, and Orryn, glancing sidelong, saw that Alyn's face was perfectly grave. "You will pour for Lady Odessa when she comes. As you pour for your lord every day. You will not be excused it."

The grave look changed a little. It did not break. But something went across Alyn's face and was gone, and Orryn remembered the dais that morning and the pen going across the tablet, and his own voice saying if we serve together as we always do. It had been a clean thought. It had come out clean. He had not once considered who would be standing behind the chair with the jug.

"My mother will want to know whether I am to sit," said Alyn. He said it lightly, to the roof beams.

"That is for Lady Jeyne to say."

"Of course, ser."

"Now. Salt and bread." Ser Lucan turned his head. "These are carried to the lower tables, and to the dais, by those who are not set to the cup. They are not a lesser office. Fallow."

Piers had been standing at the end of the line with his hands behind him, and his eyes, which were quick and brown, were on the loaf. His doublet was of fine green wool, better than any other boy's in the hall, and it had drawn a remark every day for a moon. He stepped forward and picked up the dish of salt.

"You will serve the salt and the bread to the lower tables," said Ser Lucan. "The cup is for those who serve a lord. The trencher is for those who serve a knight. I say it in no spite. Every man has his place at a feast, and it is not an insult to be put where one's blood puts one."

"Yes, ser."

"Salt is a good thing to carry," said Matthos, from the end of the line. He was not loud. He did not need to be. "Fallow is good at salt. His father counts it by the grain."

Perkin laughed. It was only a short laugh, and it stopped when he saw Ser Lucan's face. Orryn looked at Piers, who stood with the salt dish between his two hands and did not move at all. A dull flush had come up from his collar to his hair, and his jaw was working a little, as if he were counting.

"Master Matthos."

"Ser?"

"You will not mock any man for the place that he has been assigned in my hearing. It is not that the thing is false. It is that it is not yours to say. You are the heir of this house and you will be served by every man in this hall. You will learn to be served without remarking on it." Ser Lucan said it exactly as he had said everything else. There was no heat in it, only an almost mechanical fairness, like a scale being set level. "That is all. Fallow, you will hold the dish lower."

Matthos's jaw was set, and he looked as though he would speak, and did not. Orryn found, to his surprise, that he did not hate Ser Lucan for it. He found that he was a little afraid of him. Whatever the man was, he did not seem to be able to do a thing by halves. He would say that a miller's son should carry salt and that an heir should not remark on it, in the same breath, with the same expression, and believe both to be a single truth.

"A squire who serves a hall well is spoken of," Ser Lucan said, to the line, to nobody. "Some are spoken of to the lord, at the proper time, by their masters. It is the way a man's name goes up. It is not the only way, but it is a way, and it is honest." He did not look at Osric. Osric did not look at him. He was standing very still with his right hand against the seven-pointed star at his throat, and Orryn saw his fingers move on it once, as a boy tests a tooth.

He had not been asked again. Not since the linen room. It had been nine days, and ten now, and in all that time the lord had said nothing, and Orryn had kept his promise, and it had grown no lighter. He had said that he would tell. He had not said what he would do if there were nothing to tell.

"We shall do it again at the second hour tomorrow," said Ser Lucan. "Dismissed."

He went out first, straight-backed, into the white glare of the yard, and the squires broke up around the table with the loud, slightly guilty noise of boys let out of a sept. Baldric took the loaf and tore it. Perkin said that he had never seen a man so thorough about a jug. Alyn said nothing, and wandered up to the dais and sat down on the edge of it with his boots hanging, and looked at the place at the high board where, on the night, his mother would sit. Orryn went over to the table to put away the ewer. He found that Osric had come to stand beside him, quite close, with his face turned to the cups.

"Ser Lucan is right about the hand," said Osric, in a low voice. "It is not a bucket. You hold the weight in the wrist."

"Thank you."

"I did not say it to be thanked." But he showed him, quickly, with his own fingers, and the thing was easier to do than it had been all morning. Then he went away, and Orryn stood with the ewer in his hands and the sense of having been given something on a condition that nobody had named.

*

It was nearly dusk when he went to the buttery for the trenchers. They were wanted at the squires' hall, where the knights had been promised cold meat, and he went down the stair in the half-dark with his shirt stuck to his back and the day's weariness gathering at the backs of his knees. The buttery smelled of beer and old oak and the damp cool smell of cellar stone. A lamp burned on the shelf. Piers Fallow was sitting under it on an upturned cask with a tally-board on his knee, counting cups.

He did not look up. "Eighteen," he said. "Lady Jeyne has had them washed twice. There are eight of the good ones, which are silver at the rim and have the heron on the foot, and ten of the plain. If it is nine at the high board, and the guests are given the silver, and Lord Alaric and my lady the silver, that is five silver for the dais and three over. I should put the three over at the septon's end. It is not an insult. It is only silver." He moved a counter along the board. "But I do not know that Lady Jeyne will see it so."

"I did not know you were counting."

"Somebody must. Verrall has been all day with the candles." Piers glanced up then. His face was blotched and tired and entirely composed. "You did that this morning. At the hall. I was told you measured the dais."

"Marya measured. I held the cord."

"Yes. That is what I was told."

He went back to the cups. Orryn took down a stack of trenchers from the shelf, flat rounds of last week's bread gone hard as boards, and stood with them against his chest. He had meant to leave. He had a thing to say, and he could not find the beginning of it. The salt dish had been put back on its shelf by the door with a cloth over it.

"Ser Lucan was wrong about the salt," he said at last.

"Was he?" Piers did not stop counting. "He said it would be a good thing to carry. It is. If you are carrying the bread you know how much is left." He set down a counter. "I do not mind it. I should pour badly. I have watched Alyn do it all summer, and it is a thing you do with your whole body, and I should spill."

It was said very plainly. It might have been true. It might have been exactly what a boy would say who had stood in front of the hall that noon and had decided on the walk down the stair that nobody would see him mind. Orryn could not tell. He had known Piers Fallow for a moon and a half and had heard him speak, in all that time, perhaps two hundred words that were not figures.

"My father will ask me what was said at the table," Piers said, "when the Merryweathers come. He asks after every feast. I have never told him anything he could not have counted for himself." He picked up a cup, turned it, and put it down with the heron to the wall. "I thought you might like to know that."

"Thank you," said Orryn.

"It is not a thing to thank me for."

That was the end of it. Piers went back to the cups, and Orryn took the trenchers and went out with his arms full of bread. He did not know whether he had been told a confidence or a warning, or only a plain fact that a boy had said aloud to a lamp. He decided to think about it later.

Over the buttery door, as he came out under it, hung a new board of pale deal that had not been there that morning. It had been whitewashed and lettered in black, in a hand he knew for Marya's, with a line of words about beds and promises and the keeping of both. He stopped under it with the trenchers against his chest and read it through, as he had read the toll-book at Sedgeford, to the end of the sentence. It was his grandmother's rule, set down exactly as he had said it, with her name beneath. The lamp behind him threw his shadow across the board, and he shifted, and read it again.

He could not have said what troubled him. The words were hers. The hand was a good hand. They had been put over a door where every servant who passed would see them, and where they would be seen, he thought, by Lady Jeyne herself on her way down to count her candles. It was a fine thing to have said. He was not at all sure he ought to have said it.
