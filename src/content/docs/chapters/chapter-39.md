Lord Alaric was halfway across the yard when his youngest daughter came flying out of the passage beneath the gallery.

“Father!”

She was barefoot, which Orryn noticed before anything else. Her hair had come loose down one shoulder, and there was something pink in her fist. She ran straight toward the lord, with a maid following at a much less hopeful pace.

“Father, Matthos has taken it.”

Lord Alaric stopped.

“What has he taken?”

“My ribbon.”

“Then tell him to give it back.”

“He says he hasn't got it.”

“Has he?”

“I don't know. That's why I came to you.”

This seemed to Orryn a very good answer, though the lord did not appear to think so. Lady Elyce Meadows stood before her father with her chin raised, twelve years old and still small enough to make a business of being the youngest. Her brown hair had gone wild in the heat, and a ribbon, blue rather than pink, hung from one of her sleeves.

“Where is your mother?”

“In her chamber.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Because she said she had heard quite enough of it.”

Lord Alaric looked up toward the gallery.

“Jeyne!”

A window opened above them.

Lady Jeyne Meadows appeared between the shutters, dark chestnut hair pinned neatly beneath a gauze veil. She had not dressed for the yard. Her gown was pale green, with narrow sleeves, and a gold chain lay across her breast.

“Alaric, do not shout beneath the windows. You'll wake the whole west range.”

“Your daughter says Matthos has stolen her ribbon.”

“He has not stolen it. She gave it to him yesterday.”

“I lent it!” Elyce cried.

“You tied it round his dog's neck, sweetling. Now go and put shoes on.”

“It was my best one.”

“Then I hope the dog appreciated it.”

From the gallery came a noise that might have been laughter. Orryn looked up and saw Lady Leonette withdrawing from the window, too late to hide the smile on her face.

Lord Alaric turned back toward the stables. Elyce caught his sleeve.

“But Father—”

“Your mother has answered you.”

“She hasn't.”

“She has answered me, which is all I require.”

He freed his sleeve without roughness and went on. The stable door opened to admit him, and the yard swallowed him among the horses and the men preparing them.

Orryn had not spoken.

He still had the slate beneath his shirt. Its hard edge pressed against his ribs, and the words he had written during the night were there in chalk, unless the sweat had rubbed them out.

Across the yard Baldric Haye was wiping his practice sword with a rag. He looked once toward the stables. Orryn could not tell whether he had seen what happened at the gallery, or only that Lord Alaric had gone inside.

Elyce remained in the middle of the yard, looking at the stable door as if it had shut upon something valuable.

“You ought to have asked him before breakfast,” Orryn said.

She turned.

“Asked him what?”

“For the ribbon.”

“Matthos has it.”

“Then you ought to ask Matthos.”

“I did ask Matthos.” Her voice was becoming sharp. “He said he'd put it on the hound, and then he said the hound had eaten it, and then he said it was never mine.”

“Dogs don't eat silk.”

“Our dogs do.”

She looked at his sword, his damp shirt, the dirt on his knees. “You're the boy from the Stormlands.”

“Orryn Grandison, my lady.”

“I know that.” She seemed annoyed that he had thought otherwise. “Matthos calls you Goose.”

“He calls me several things.”

“Which one's your favourite?”

Orryn was spared the trouble of answering by the appearance of Septa Lenora.

The septa came down from the gallery with her grey veil drawn close about her face and a little crystal of the Seven swinging against her chest. She had the thin, upright look of a woman who had never hurried in her life and expected everybody else to do so.

“Lady Elyce. Your shoes.”

Elyce's expression altered at once.

“I was coming, Septa.”

“You were told to put them on before leaving the chamber.”

“I couldn't find them.”

“They are beneath your stool. I put them there myself.”

Elyce's eyes filled.

Septa Lenora looked unmoved. “I should hope they hurt no one, my lady. They are very small tears.”

The child went past her, her bare feet slapping the stones. She did not run until she had reached the arch, where she broke into a sprint and disappeared.

The septa watched her go. Then she turned toward Orryn.

“Master Grandison. You are wanted in the upper hall after the noon meal. See that you come washed.”

“By whom, Septa?”

“By Lady Jeyne.”

“What for?”

“To attend her daughters.”

Orryn waited for an explanation, but none came. The septa's gaze had gone past him, to the line of squires by the trough.

“Master Dunmere is permitted upstairs this morning, if he wishes. You may tell him so.”

She went away before he could ask another question.

Perkin Fenwick had heard the whole thing.

“To attend her daughters,” he repeated, turning the words over in an unpleasant imitation of the septa's voice. “You'll be putting ribbons in their hair, Goose.”

“Perhaps he'll get Elyce's back,” Piers Fallow said.

Alyn Merryweather came up behind them with his tunic over one shoulder and the damp hair at his neck curling in the heat.

“What's this? Are we to have a wedding?”

“Grandview's been summoned to the ladies,” said Perkin.

“Lucky Grandview.”

“It's Lady Jeyne,” Orryn said. “I don't know what she wants.”

“Oh, I do.” Alyn looked up toward the gallery, where the shutters were closing. “I had it from Marya last night. Music. Dancing. Leonette has decided we are all country oxen and means to have us taught to walk properly.”

“Leonette?”

“Her lady mother, more likely. Leonette only wants an audience.”

“Are we all going?” Piers asked.

“Every fool that can stand upright, I expect. And some that can't. Perhaps they'll let Osric dance from a chair.”

Perkin laughed. Baldric did not. He had finished cleaning his sword and was looking at the rag as though it required further attention.

“Did the lord speak to you?” he asked Orryn.

“No.”

Baldric nodded once.

Alyn threw his tunic over Orryn's head.

“Then you can tell us at dinner why you're frightened of a dance.”

The cloth smelled of sweat and horse. Orryn pulled it off and flung it back, catching Alyn across the face. Alyn made a great show of falling against Perkin, who staggered into the trough and got wet to the knees.

For a few moments there was only laughter and splashing and Perkin threatening to drown them all.

Baldric went in ahead of the others.

*

Orryn found Osric Dunmere in the little chamber beyond the infirmary, sitting on a stool with a cup of broth between his hands.

The shutters were closed against the sun, and the room smelled of vinegar, drying herbs and boiled linen. There was a pallet against one wall and a chest with a chipped blue bowl upon it. Osric had been lying on that pallet when Orryn last saw him. Now he was dressed, although his clothes hung badly on him, and his fair hair had been combed so straight across his forehead that he looked younger than seventeen.

“Septa Lenora says you may go upstairs,” Orryn told him.

“I know.”

“Have you eaten?”

“I am eating.”

It was not a promising beginning.

Orryn stood near the door. There was a chair beneath the window, but Osric had not invited him to sit. A fly was walking round the lip of the blue bowl.

“Does it still hurt?”

“What?”

“Your head.”

“No.”

“You struck the tree.”

“I remember.”

Orryn wished he had not come. He had thought Osric would be glad of company after two days away from the other squires, but the boy's narrow face gave no encouragement.

At last Osric put down the broth.

“Ser Lucan came yesterday.”

“Oh.”

“He brought my sword.”

“Was he angry?”

“Why should he be angry?”

Orryn did not answer. He had seen Alaric order the knight to stand before him at dawn after Osric's collapse, and had heard what was said in the yard. Ser Lucan Marwick had made his squire run in full harness through the midday heat until the boy fell. The lord's rebuke had been public.

“He said I was to rest until Maester Perrin released me,” Osric continued. “He did not say when I should return to his service.”

“He'll need you back.”

“Yes.”

“Has the maester said?”

“No.”

Another silence.

Osric lifted the cup and took a careful swallow. He had the seven-pointed star in his other hand, rubbing its silver edge between finger and thumb.

“Perkin has been to see me,” he said.

“Has he?”

“He brought a pear.”

“That was good of him.”

“It was rotten inside.”

Orryn laughed before he could stop himself.

Osric looked up.

“He said he had not known. He ate the other half to prove it, and then he was sick in the passage.”

Orryn laughed again. This time Osric's mouth moved a little too, although he kept his eyes on the cup.

“He is a fool,” Osric said.

“He is.”

“He offered to bring me another.”

“Don't let him.”

“I told him to bring an apple.”

Outside, a bell sounded from the little sept.

Osric set the broth down with a sudden impatience.

“I should be in the yard.”

“You'll be there tomorrow, perhaps.”

“You don't know that.”

“No.”

“Then don't say it.”

Orryn felt his face grow warm.

Osric looked at him, and then away. “I am tired of being told I'll be well. I am well. I can walk from here to the sept without falling over, and Maester Perrin says there is nothing wrong with my heart.”

“He didn't say there was.”

“I know what he said.”

Orryn did not. He had not been present.

He looked at the sword belt hanging from the bedpost. The leather was newly cleaned. Somebody had rubbed oil into the buckle until it shone.

“Did Ser Lucan bring that as well?”

“Yes.”

“He cleaned it?”

“His page did.” Osric's thin mouth tightened. “I am not a child who needs his sword polished for him.”

Orryn thought of his own father's habit of sending back boots that had been polished badly, and of the way a servant's care could become an insult in the wrong hands.

He did not say so.

“There's to be dancing upstairs,” he said instead. “Lady Jeyne sent for the squires.”

Osric looked almost alarmed.

“Dancing?”

“After the noon meal.”

“With whom?”

“Leonette, I suppose. And Elyce.”

“Elyce is twelve.”

“So is my cousin Merel. She danced at Dunmere.”

“She danced very badly.”

“You watched her?”

“She stepped on Cedric's foot. Twice.”

Orryn sat on the chair beneath the window. He had not meant to, but Osric said nothing.

For a little while they talked about Dunmere Hall, about Merel's questions and Lady Edda's honey cakes, and about how Piers had snored in the armoury loft. Osric corrected Orryn twice on matters that did not seem worth correcting. Orryn let him.

When he rose to leave, Osric called him back.

“Grandison.”

“Yes?”

“Is it true that Lord Alaric went himself into the millpond?”

“Yes.”

“Up to his waist?”

“Near enough.”

“With his boots on?”

“No. He took those off.”

Osric considered this.

“Perkin said he went in naked.”

“He did not.”

“I thought not.”

He picked up his broth again.

“Go on, then. You'll be late for your dancing.”

*

By noon the sun had made the yard almost impossible to cross.

Orryn came up the gallery stair with his hair still damp from washing and found Perkin waiting outside the upper hall, pulling at the collar of a tunic so fine it could not possibly be his.

“Whose is that?”

“Mine.”

“It isn't.”

“It is now. Ser Tobin gave it me.”

“Why?”

“He says I look like a beggar when I'm clean.”

The tunic was dark blue with a narrow band of gold stitching at the throat. It was too broad at the shoulders, and Perkin had stuffed the sleeves into his belt to keep them from covering his hands.

Alyn arrived behind Orryn carrying a pair of shoes.

“You've got it backwards, Fenwick.”

Perkin looked down.

“The stitching's on the outside.”

“That's because it is meant to be.”

“The ties are behind your neck.”

Perkin reached back and felt them. “Gods curse it.”

“Let me.” Alyn set his shoes down and pulled the tunic straight while Perkin struggled inside it. “You've dressed yourself like a sack of flour.”

“Better than being one.”

“You were one last night. Ser Tobin said so.”

“He says a great many things.”

The door opened before Alyn could answer.

Marya Caswell stood in the doorway, a narrow-faced woman of thirty in a dark gown that had been remade more than once. She was Lady Jeyne's cousin and companion, although Orryn had already learned that calling her a servant was a good way to receive a correction.

“Master Merryweather. Master Grandison. Master Fenwick.” Her eyes passed over Perkin's borrowed tunic without a change of expression. “Lady Jeyne is waiting.”

“Is she cross?” Perkin asked.

“She is becoming so.”

Alyn took up his shoes.

“You'll save us a place?”

“Inside, Master Merryweather.”

He bowed to her with a flourish and went in barefoot.

The upper hall was unlike the great hall below. It was long and narrow, with plastered walls painted in faded flowers, and it had windows on three sides. In a cooler season Orryn supposed it would be pleasant. Today the shutters had been thrown wide, and the warm air came through without much strength. Two servants were setting bowls of water along the walls, and a third was bringing up a tray of sliced fruit.

At the far end stood Lady Jeyne, speaking to a musician. He was an elderly man with a narrow face and a fiddle tucked beneath one arm. A boy beside him carried a little drum and looked miserable in a red cap.

Lady Leonette sat in the largest chair with her feet upon a stool.

She had been dressed in pale yellow, with a green ribbon at her waist and another threaded through her chestnut hair. Her slippers had been set beside her, and a serving girl was kneeling to fasten something at the hem of her gown. She was talking to the girl while she worked.

“If it catches again, I shall take it off. I mean it. You cannot dance with half your skirt under your heel.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And do not pull so hard. It isn't a horse.”

The serving girl flushed.

At the window, Matthos Meadows was standing with both hands on the sill, looking down into the yard. He had made no attempt to dress for the occasion. His hair was damp and untidy, and there was dried mud on one knee.

Elyce sat beside Septa Lenora, now wearing shoes. The pink ribbon had returned to her hair.

Orryn wondered whether Matthos had given it back or whether someone had found the hound.

Lady Jeyne turned as the squires entered.

“Good. All except Osric.”

“Still with the maester, my lady,” Orryn said.

“I know. I asked after him this morning. I am glad to see you have found something clean to wear, Master Fenwick.”

Perkin bowed.

Lady Jeyne looked toward Alyn, who was trying to put on his shoes while standing on one foot.

“And you, Master Merryweather, might at least have waited until you came through the door.”

“My lady, I wished to give the hall the pleasure of seeing me properly shod.”

“Then you have disappointed it.”

She smiled, and Alyn grinned back without seeming in the least ashamed.

“Where is Master Haye?”

“Coming, my lady,” Orryn said. “He was with Ser Bertram.”

“And Fallow?”

There was a little silence.

“Piers is here, my lady,” said Piers Fallow, from the doorway.

Orryn had not heard him arrive. He wore a dark green doublet trimmed in black, with polished shoes and his curls combed flat. Of all the boys, he looked most like someone who had prepared carefully for the afternoon.

Lady Jeyne regarded him for a moment.

“Very well. Come in.”

She turned back to the musician.

“The first figure, then. And slowly. My daughters know the steps, though they do not always remember that other people must be able to follow them.”

“I remember them,” Leonette said.

“Then you may demonstrate.”

Leonette rose from her chair and held out her hand to Alyn.

“Master Merryweather.”

He took it and bowed.

“Lady Leonette.”

His bow was beautiful. Orryn hated that he noticed how beautiful it was. Alyn could come down a stair half asleep, with his laces undone and yesterday's wine on his breath, and still bow as if he had been born doing it.

Leonette made a graceful little courtesy.

The musician put the fiddle beneath his chin.

It was a lively tune, four measures repeated twice, and Leonette stepped forward as Alyn stepped back. They crossed, turned, joined hands and came together again, the girl moving with a brightness that made her yellow skirts flare at every turn. Alyn followed without difficulty. When he went wrong, which he did twice, he laughed, and Leonette laughed with him.

After the third figure Lady Jeyne clapped her hands.

“Enough. You know it. The others must learn.”

Matthos groaned.

His mother turned.

“Matthos.”

“I know how.”

“Then come down from the window.”

“I have done it a hundred times.”

“You shall find the hundred and first no great hardship.”

“I would sooner be riding.”

“Your horse would sooner be standing in the shade.”

“That isn't true.”

“Ask him.”

Matthos looked briefly at Orryn, as though hoping for support, and then came away from the window with a scowl.

Lady Jeyne arranged them in two lines.

Leonette took Alyn again. Elyce claimed Perkin, who bowed so low that the borrowed tunic fell forward over his head and made her laugh. Matthos found himself opposite Piers.

“I am not dancing with Fallow.”

“Then you needn't,” said Piers.

Lady Jeyne raised her eyebrows.

“You will practise with him, Matthos. There are not enough ladies present, and he is as capable of remembering the steps as anyone else.”

“Father says his father bought him his place here.”

“Your father says a great many things when he is tired.”

Matthos went red.

For a moment Orryn expected him to strike Piers or throw something. He did neither. He took his place with his arms stiff at his sides.

Baldric arrived just then, having changed into the patched but carefully brushed tunic he wore at supper. Marya put him opposite Orryn and took the last place herself.

“Begin,” said Lady Jeyne.

The musician played.

Orryn stepped forward when everyone else stepped back.

“Other foot,” Marya told him.

He tried the other foot and collided with Baldric.

“Not both at once,” said Baldric quietly.

“I know.”

“You've got it wrong again.”

“I know that too.”

“Then stop doing it.”

The music continued. Leonette was laughing at something Alyn had said. Elyce was trying to lead Perkin by the fingers and had begun to pull him entirely out of place. Matthos danced with all the grace of a man carrying a dead pig, but Piers moved easily, turning on the beat without once looking down.

Orryn noticed.

So did Lady Jeyne.

“Master Fallow.”

Piers stopped.

“Who taught you?”

“My mother, my lady.”

“Your mother dances?”

“At feast days. And she showed me the steps when I was small.”

“She has taught you well.”

Piers bowed his head. “Thank you, my lady.”

Matthos made a noise.

Lady Jeyne heard it.

“You may learn from him, Matthos.”

“I'm not a miller's son.”

“No,” said his mother. “You are my son, which is why I expect better manners.”

There was nothing particularly loud about it, but Matthos went silent.

Orryn looked at Piers. He had gone very still, and for once did not appear to know what to do with his hands.

When the music began again, Matthos followed the steps perfectly.

He did not look at Piers.

*

By the second hour they had learnt three figures, or something sufficiently resembling them that Lady Jeyne declared herself satisfied.

The musician was given wine and bread. His boy ate four slices of pear without removing his red cap, until Marya noticed and told him to take it off.

Perkin lay full length beneath a window with his arms spread.

“I would sooner run the yard in mail.”

“You did run the yard in mail,” Orryn told him.

“That was different. Nobody made me turn about and kiss hands.”

“You have not kissed anybody's hands,” Baldric said.

“I would have done, if asked.”

“Then you have been fortunate.”

Leonette was sitting beside Alyn, who had taken possession of the fruit tray and was eating from it without any ceremony. She had untied her hair and was having the servant fasten it again.

“Ser Garlan dances like a man praying,” she said.

Her betrothed was Ser Garlan Fossoway of Cider Hall, a knight eleven years her elder and, so far as Orryn had heard, a decent man. He had visited Grassfield before, although not since Orryn's arrival. Leonette appeared to find him both admirable and very dull.

“He does everything like a man praying,” she continued. “He says the nicest things. I never know what to say back.”

“Say thank you,” Alyn suggested.

“I do say thank you.”

“Then you're doing splendidly.”

“You don't understand. He asks whether I am well. Then he asks after Mother. Then he asks whether the pear harvest is good. After that he has run out of things.”

“Ask him about his horse.”

“I have. He told me everything about it.”

“That was a mistake.”

“I know.”

Alyn laughed, and Leonette leaned toward him.

Orryn was drinking water from a cup that had gone warm in his hand. He had no wish to listen, but their voices carried across the hall.

“Will your sister come this year?” Leonette asked.

“Before the next moon is out, if Mother has her way.”

“With Ser Androw?”

“I expect so. They cannot go anywhere separately now. Half the Reach must be shown what a fine match they've made.”

Leonette's expression changed.

“Wylla said she would send me the pattern of her new gown.”

“Then she'll send it.”

“She promised it before the feast at Longtable.”

“Then she has forgotten.”

“She has not forgotten.”

Alyn reached for another piece of pear. “Then she has decided against it.”

Leonette was silent for a moment.

Orryn knew Wylla Merryweather by reputation, largely from what Alyn had said. She was fifteen, the sister of the senior squire, and promised to Ser Androw Ashford. Her family held Longtable, a lordly seat farther west in the Reach. Ser Androw was the heir to House Ashford, whose connection to Highgarden came through his aunt's marriage to Ser Moryn Tyrell, a son of Lord Leo.

Orryn had not yet met either of them.

He had been looking forward to their visit. At Grandview the arrival of guests had made the whole castle seem larger. Here the days had become so much alike that he sometimes could not remember whether he had been in the yard yesterday or the day before.

Leonette caught him watching.

“Do you dance at Grandview, Master Orryn?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Do you dance well?”

“No.”

“Good. Then you're honest, at least.”

“I've seen him dance,” Perkin said from the floor. “It is like watching a goose put on boots.”

“Perkin,” said Alyn, “you are still wearing yours backwards.”

He was not, but Perkin looked down anyway.

Leonette laughed.

Then she turned to Orryn again.

“You were at your cousin Elenei's wedding, weren't you? The one to the Penrose knight?”

“Yes.”

“Was the feast grand?”

“Very, my lady.”

“Did they dance?”

“They did.”

“Was there a bedding?”

Orryn almost choked on his water.

Leonette's smile widened.

“Of course there was. I asked whether you saw it.”

“Leonette,” said Lady Jeyne.

The lady had been speaking quietly with Marya at the far window. She had not seemed to be listening.

“I am only asking about the wedding, Mother.”

“Then ask about the bride.”

“I did ask about the bride.”

“No, you asked about the bedding. You know the difference.”

Leonette lowered her eyes, though the smile remained.

Lady Jeyne came over and set a hand on her daughter's shoulder.

“You will have enough time to hear such stories from married women. You needn't ask them of boys.”

“I should rather hear them from boys,” Leonette muttered.

Alyn covered his mouth with his cup.

His laughter escaped anyway.

Lady Jeyne looked at him.

“Master Merryweather, if you have something to contribute, do so.”

“No, my lady.”

“I thought not.”

She squeezed Leonette's shoulder and moved away again.

The conversation changed to horses, and then to a hunting dog Matthos wanted to acquire from Ser Wyman, and then to whether it was possible to train a hawk to strike a goose.

Perkin said it could be done.

Orryn hoped very much that he was wrong.

*

The afternoon might have ended pleasantly had Elyce not begun to cry.

She had spent most of the last hour trying to persuade Alyn to dance another figure with her. He had promised he would after finishing his wine, but then Matthos called him over to the window, where they began arguing about a horse, and the promise was forgotten.

Elyce stood waiting in the middle of the floor.

Orryn watched her for a while. She had the recovered pink ribbon in her hair, and she kept touching it to be sure it was still there.

At last she went to Lady Jeyne.

“Alyn said he would dance with me.”

“Then ask him, sweetling.”

“I did.”

“Ask again.”

“He won't.”

Lady Jeyne was speaking to Marya about a letter from Bitterbridge. She held out a hand without looking, and Elyce took it.

“Wait a moment.”

Elyce waited.

Then she began to cry.

It was very quiet at first, and Orryn might not have noticed if Perkin had not stopped talking. The music had ended, and the little drummer was asleep with his head against the wall.

Lady Jeyne looked down.

“What is it?”

“He promised.”

“Who?”

“Alyn.”

Alyn turned from the window.

“Did I?”

“You did.”

He looked puzzled, and then remembered.

“Seven save me, so I did. Come here, little lady.”

But Elyce had begun crying in earnest.

“I don't want to now.”

“Then don't.”

“That isn't fair!”

Alyn looked helplessly toward Lady Jeyne.

“I meant no harm, my lady.”

“No,” she said. “I don't suppose you did.”

She drew Elyce against her skirts and stroked her hair.

“Come, sweetling. It is nothing worth tears.”

“He said he'd dance.”

“I know.”

“And Matthos took my ribbon.”

“I know that too.”

“And Leonette had Alyn all morning.”

“Elyce,” Leonette said, outraged.

Lady Jeyne looked at her eldest daughter.

“You have had your share of the afternoon.”

“I danced with everyone!”

“You danced with whom you pleased, which is not the same thing.”

Leonette opened her mouth and closed it again.

It was the first time Orryn had seen her fail to find an answer.

Marya came forward with a handkerchief for Elyce, but the child pushed it away. Septa Lenora stood beside the window with her hands folded, observing the whole affair with a look Orryn recognised from Osric.

“Lady Elyce,” the septa said, “a young lady does not make herself ridiculous because a gentleman forgets a promise.”

Elyce's face crumpled.

Lady Jeyne looked up sharply.

“That is enough, Lenora.”

The septa's face did not change.

“I meant only—”

“I know what you meant.”

There was a silence.

Orryn had never heard Lady Jeyne speak to the septa in quite that voice. Septa Lenora had followed her from Bitterbridge when she married Alaric, and the servants said there was no one in the keep who could tell the lady what to do except her old governess.

The septa bowed her head.

“As my lady wishes.”

Lady Jeyne turned back to Elyce.

“Come. We'll find something cooler to drink.”

The girl allowed herself to be led away, though she continued sniffling until they reached the door.

Leonette watched them go.

“She always does that.”

“Does what?” Marya asked.

“Cries until Mother takes her away.”

“Then you might try it yourself,” Matthos said.

Leonette threw a cushion at him.

He caught it and hurled it back. It struck Perkin, who was still lying beneath the window, full in the face.

“Seven hells!”

That sent Alyn into another fit of laughter.

Even Marya smiled, though she made Matthos put the cushions back before anyone could throw another.

*

Lord Alaric came into the hall as the servants were carrying away the last of the fruit.

He had changed his shirt, but there was dust on his boots and something dark on his right sleeve. He looked at the scattered chairs, the sleeping drummer and the heap of cushions on the floor.

“What happened here?”

“We danced,” said Leonette.

“Did you?”

“Yes, Father.”

“Who won?”

“There's no winning a dance.”

“Then what was the use of it?”

Alyn laughed.

Lady Jeyne came back through the door behind him, Elyce now composed and carrying a little cup of watered wine.

“Do not encourage him, Alaric. He has been insufferable all afternoon.”

“Matthos?”

“Every one of them.”

“That is what comes of teaching boys to dance when they ought to be riding.”

“I asked them here because they ought to know how to behave when we have company.”

“Then I should have been invited. I am no better at it than they are.”

“You were invited.”

“I was not.”

“I sent Marya.”

“Then Marya neglected me.”

Marya inclined her head.

“I told your steward, my lord.”

“Then Verrall neglected me, which is much more likely.”

For a moment Lady Jeyne's expression softened. She took the cup from Elyce and handed it to a servant.

“You have been out in this heat again.”

“I have.”

“Did you eat?”

“Some bread.”

“Alaric.”

“Some very good bread.”

She shook her head.

It seemed to Orryn that they were having a conversation they had had a hundred times, and that neither expected the other to change.

Lady Jeyne drew him toward the window, away from the children, and began speaking quietly. Orryn could hear the names Caswell and Fossoway, but not what was being said. Alaric listened with his head a little bent, his hands behind his back. Once he interrupted. Lady Jeyne answered at length, and he let her finish.

Leonette went over to them and slipped her arm through her father's.

“Father, will Ser Garlan come before the year is out?”

“I expect so.”

“Can I write to him?”

“Of course.”

“Will you send it?”

“If you give it to Verrall.”

“He always takes so long.”

“Then write it early.”

She made a face.

Alaric looked down at her, amused.

“You are very like your mother.”

“I should hope so,” said Lady Jeyne.

“And I should hope not,” said Alaric.

Leonette laughed and kissed his cheek.

Matthos, who had been listening from the window, made a disgusted noise.

“Girls.”

Lady Jeyne turned.

“Matthos, I have had enough of you for one afternoon.”

“I haven't done anything.”

“You have done a great many things. None of them useful.”

“I could have been riding.”

“You could have been polite.”

“I was polite!”

“Then you may try it again tomorrow.”

Matthos scowled and went out.

No one called him back.

Lady Jeyne watched the door close, and for a moment her expression became difficult to read. Then Elyce took her hand and asked whether she might have the pink ribbon tied properly, and the lady bent to see to it.

Orryn was still standing near the window when Alyn came up beside him.

“Well, Goose. You survived.”

“Barely.”

“You'll learn.”

“I don't think I shall.”

“You will. It isn't difficult. You've only got to remember where your feet are.”

“That seems to be the difficult part.”

Alyn laughed and put an arm briefly around his shoulders.

“Come on. Perkin's promised to show us how they dance at Fenwick. He says there is a step where everybody falls over.”

“He would know.”

“Wouldn't he?”

They went down the stair together.

Behind them the fiddle began again, faintly. Lady Leonette had persuaded the musician to play one more figure, and somewhere above the sound of the strings Orryn could hear Elyce calling that she wanted to dance too.

In the passage below the gallery, Baldric was waiting with his cloak folded over one arm.

He looked at Orryn, then at Alyn, and said nothing.

Alyn went ahead toward supper, singing badly.

Orryn followed him, with Baldric coming after, and the sound of music drifting down through the warm stone stair.
