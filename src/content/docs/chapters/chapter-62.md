Grassfield Keep, Grassy Vale, 208 AC

They came home on the second afternoon with the dust of two roads in their teeth and the cart empty behind them, rattling like a drum.

Orryn had stopped being able to feel his thighs somewhere past Hedgefort. Baldric rode beside him and said nothing at all. He had not said much since the loft, and whether that was the stag, or the other talk, or only that he was sixteen and tired, Orryn could not tell, and had decided not to ask.

The gate stood open. A groom took Rook’s head before Orryn was fairly out of the saddle, and Ser Wyman Latham looked the dun over from nose to hock, as he looked over every horse that came back to him, and grunted, and said nothing to the boy who had ridden it. Orryn had learned that this was praise.

“Grandison.”

Lord Alaric was crossing the yard with a roll of paper under one arm and his shirtsleeves pushed to the elbow, as he did on any day that was not a feast. He had a way of covering ground that made everyone near him look as though they had been standing still too long. He did not break stride to ask.

“Did it go in the open?”

“Yes, my lord. At Hob’s Cross, before a Highgarden knight and seven carts. Ser Robyn Rhysling. He made me say it twice.”

“Rhysling.” Alaric’s mouth moved. “What did he make you say?”

Orryn told him straight: the four words, and what he had said about Ser Garth and Ser Moryn, and what Ser Robyn had said about a gift that is given against no one. Alaric laughed, once, without much amusement in it.

“A quarrel is not grain. He is right, curse him. Anything else?”

Orryn had not meant to say it first, and found that he had no choice. “We paid a stag at Hedgefort, my lord. Ser Dudley’s bridge. It wasn’t owed.”

“Dudley took toll of my own wax?”

“He said the grain was bound for Highgarden, so it wasn’t on your errand. Baldric argued the custom. I told Cobb to pay.”

“Did you.” Alaric looked at him for the length of a breath. “Why?”

It was a real question, and Orryn answered it as one. “It was one stag, my lord. And we were to sleep in his hall.”

“And Haye said it would be every cart after.”

Orryn’s face went hot. He had not said that. “Yes, my lord.”

“He is right too.” Alaric tucked the paper higher under his arm. “It would have been every cart after, if I had ever intended to send more than the one. Dudley would charge the king for riding by.” He began to walk again, and over his shoulder said, “I will speak to Dudley. Go and wash. Lady Elyce has been asking for you since morning, and I would sooner she asked you than me.”

He was gone before Orryn could say that he did not know why.

*

He found her in the long stable, where he should have looked first. Elyce Meadows stood on an upturned pail beside the big bay colt that had been defeating her for a moon, with a bridle over her arm and her chin on the colt’s neck, and she did not look round when he came in.

She was twelve, Lord Alaric’s youngest daughter, small for her age, with soft brown hair that never stayed where she put it and wide hazel eyes and freckles all across her nose. Everyone at Grassfield said she was the sweetest child in the household. Orryn had been there long enough to hear the cooks say, under their breath, how many servants she had got into trouble with a single tear.

“You went away,” she said.

“I came back.”

“Everyone goes away. Leonette went. Matthos went. Alyn went. Nobody tells me anything.” She still had not turned. “Did you know?”

Orryn kept his hands where they were. “Know what, my lady?”

“Don’t.” She did turn then, and her face was perfectly dry, and he saw that she had been practising it. “Mother told me yesterday, with the door shut. Septa Lenora sat in the corner with her sewing and did not look at me. Verrall knew before I did. Marya knew. Cook knew. The girl who empties the pots knew. I was the last one in the whole keep.” She took a breath. “Were you in the room?”

He thought of lying, and could see exactly where it would take him. “Yes.”

“So you knew before me.”

“Only that morning.”

“That is before me.” She looked at the colt’s ear. “I am to be Lady of Old Oak.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Leonette got a Footly, who is nobody, and married in pale blue and had everyone cry. I get a lord.” She said the word as though she were tasting it. “I would be higher than Leonette. In any hall in the Reach.”

Orryn did not say that her mother had said it first, in the solar, and had not sounded pleased. “It’s a very old house.”

“I know it is. Verrall told me.” A little crease appeared between her brows. “And the boy is a rebel’s son, and everyone looked at me as though I should be sorry. Why should I be sorry? I didn’t rise for anyone. Nobody asked me.” It was nearly true, and her mouth began to tremble in the way that Orryn had seen make grown men put down their cups. “They all decided. They always decide. And then they look at me, and they’re so kind.”

“Elyce.”

It was the stableman’s voice behind him, mild, and in the same instant her face crumpled like a sheet being folded, and she turned it into the colt’s neck. Orryn looked round. Ser Wyman stood in the door with a halter in his hand. The tears were real enough, or as real as such things could be. He did not know. He knew only that they had come at exactly the moment that someone could see them.

“Master Grandison,” said Ser Wyman, gently, with a look that said he would be remembering this. “Her ladyship’s colt wants walking.”

Orryn took the bridle from her arm, and she let him, and did not look at him. As he led the colt out into the white light of the yard he heard her say to Ser Wyman, in a small clear voice, “Orryn was in the room.”

*

Ser Cleyton Caswell rode in at noon the next day on a horse that had been ridden too hard, in a cloak that had been very blue.

He was Lord Armond’s nephew, and so Lady Jeyne’s cousin, a knight of Bitterbridge, twenty years old and the best-looking man Orryn had ever seen who was not a Tyrell. He had fair hair that curled at the neck, a straight nose, and a trick of carrying his head as though somebody were drawing him. At Bitterbridge he had left the hall at the first hard word of the Rowan quarrel, with his cloak over his arm. Orryn had not forgotten it, and he thought that Ser Cleyton had not forgotten it either, because he came across the yard with his chin up and his eyes everywhere but on the faces of the men who had been at that table.

“Lady Jeyne!” He swept her a bow that would have done credit to a court. “Cousin. Lord Alaric. I bring my uncle’s love, and a letter, and—” he dropped his voice, because there were grooms about, and the whole yard was listening — “some news.”

They took him into the hall. Orryn followed Alaric, because that was what a squire did, and stood behind his chair. Lady Jeyne sat, with Marya at her elbow and Verrall on the other side with his smile ready and a roll of paper ready beside it. A servant brought wine, and Ser Cleyton drank it all in one swallow and sat very straight, like a man who has been told he will be asked questions.

Lady Jeyne broke the seal herself. She read the letter, which was short. She read it again, and handed it to Alaric without a word.

Orryn could see it over his shoulder. It was in Lord Armond’s big uneven hand, and it said that he had come safe to Goldengrove; that Lord Rowan had received him civilly and at length; that he had seen the boy, and would tell his daughter what he had seen when he saw her face to face and not before; that Lord Rowan would answer Lord Alaric in his own hand, and that Lord Alaric should not expect it soon. It said nothing at all about the boy. It said, at the bottom, that Cleyton would tell them the rest, and that Jeyne should not believe the half of it.

“He says not to believe the half,” said Lady Jeyne.

“My uncle jokes.” Ser Cleyton coloured. “I saw it all, cousin. I was at his shoulder.”

“Then tell us.”

He told it well. He told it the way a boy tells a tale he has been rehearsing for three days on a horse. The ride to Goldengrove; the great castle on its hill, whose walls were gold in the evening and whose gate was hung with a golden tree; Lord Rowan, grey and broad, who had stood in his hall with his arms folded and listened to Lord Armond without moving his face. “White about the mouth,” said Ser Cleyton, with relish. “I have never seen a lord so angry who was so courteous. He said, ‘Lord Leo has a High Marshal, and a justiciar, and a seneschal, and it seems a lord of the Reach may not marry a girl to a ward without one of them to see to it. I wonder that nobody thought to ask the man who has the boy.’ He said it very quietly.”

“And Lord Armond?” said Alaric.

“My lord uncle said that it was not Lord Leo’s doing, but Lady Arlanna’s. And Lord Rowan said, ‘Is it a better thing when a woman does it?’” Ser Cleyton shook his head. “It was horrible.”

“And the boy,” said Lady Jeyne.

It was her first question, and she asked it very softly. Ser Cleyton turned to her with a look of enormous relief, as though he had been waiting for the part he could do.

“Oh, he is a lord, cousin. He is a lord from his boots up. Thirteen, and as thin as a lath, and so stiff you would think he had a poker down his shirt. He looked at me, when we were named, as if I were something he had found on his shoe. ‘And whose man are you?’ he said. ‘And what does your house pay you?’” Ser Cleyton laughed, and it did not quite come off. “I said that I was Lord Armond’s nephew. He said that was not an answer. I said, ‘No, my lord.’”

“You said, ‘No, my lord,’ to a boy of thirteen,” said Alaric.

“He is Lord of Old Oak!”

“He is thirteen.”

Lady Jeyne had not taken her eyes from her cousin. “What did he say of his mother?”

“That she wrote to him. That he was too busy to answer.” Ser Cleyton lifted a hand. “But he spoke of his father. All the time. ‘My father held the south road for the king’s peace,’ he said. ‘My father would never have borne it.’ Of what, he did not say. He has never seen him. He was a babe in arms.” He shook his head again. “I liked him, cousin. I think. He has a great deal of spirit. He is exactly the kind of lord one would not wish to cross.”

“Is he,” said Lady Jeyne, and nothing else.

Orryn had been watching the back of Alaric’s head. It was perfectly still. He thought: this is what my lord looks like when he is counting. He did not know what Alaric was counting. He knew only that Lady Jeyne had asked a thing that Ser Cleyton had not answered, and that she knew it, and that she had decided not to ask again.

“And Rowan’s household?” said Alaric, in an ordinary voice. “Ser Alyn Merryweather was with them.”

“Alyn?” Ser Cleyton brightened. “Aye, in the tilting-yard. He was knocking a Rowan cousin out of the saddle when we rode in. They tell me he has done it every morning.” He glanced round, for the first time in an hour a little at ease. “He asked after you, Master Grandison. He said, ‘Is Grandview still in the dust?’”

Orryn said that he was. He said it in an even voice. He did not know what his face did.

“Ser Cleyton,” said Lord Alaric. “You rode three days. That is not what you came to say.”

The fair head came up. It was plain to Orryn that this was so, and that Ser Cleyton had been wishing to say it since the gate and had been trying, with considerable skill, to put it off.

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

He set down his cup.

“There was a rider at Goldengrove, the second night. A Tarly man, from Horn Hill, with his horse dead under him two leagues short, and he came in on his own feet and fell down in the hall. He said that the Marches were burning. That there had been Dornishmen at a steading below the Red Mountains—a hundred, he said, perhaps more, he could not see in the dark—and they had taken the cattle and fired the barns, and there was a woman dead, and a boy missing, and that Lord Tarly was calling for every spear the Reach could spare.”

Nobody said anything. In the passage, a dog scratched.

“A hundred,” said Alaric.

“That is what he said.”

“A hundred Dornishmen. He saw them.”

“In the dark, my lord.”

“Did you see him? The rider.”

“I—yes. I saw him fall.”

“And did you speak to him?”

“Lord Rowan did.” Ser Cleyton’s voice dropped. “Lord Rowan gave him water, and a bed, and a message for Lord Tarly. He said that he was grieved. He said that when Highgarden remembered whose men the lords of the Reach were, Highgarden would find them where it had left them. And he went out of the hall, and the Tarly man was still on the floor.”

Orryn thought of Denys Tarly at Bitterbridge, saying the Marches were as dry as the Mander. He thought: so it was not a story. He thought: or it was, and a man with a dead horse has told it.

“That,” said Lord Alaric, “is the first thing you have said in an hour that I can use.” He did not say it unkindly. “How many did the rider see?”

“A hundred.”

“Dornish. With banners?”

“No, my lord. I think not.”

“Then he saw riders in the dark and a barn on fire and a wife with her throat cut, and he counted his own fear as a hundred. I do not say he lied.” Alaric got up. “I say that I have been a lord long enough to know what a hundred is.” He looked at his wife. “Jeyne. A cup for Ser Cleyton, and a bed. And Verrall.”

“My lord.”

“How many horses can this keep feed?”

Verrall’s smile went out for the second time in two days.

The grey sergeant came the following morning, and he came from the south on a mule.

Orryn knew him at once. He had been the stocky man at Ser Denys Tarly’s back in the small hall at Bitterbridge, grey and heavy-handed, with two fingers gone from his left hand, who had walked a great many young knights into a great many halls and looked as though he expected to walk this one out again. He was alone. His mule had a Tarly saddle-cloth, green gone brown with dust, and the huntsman on it was drawing his bow at nothing.

He gave his name as Hollis, sergeant to Lord Tarly of Horn Hill, and said that he would give his message to the lord and no one else. Lord Alaric came down to the yard to take it. He stood with his feet apart and his hands behind him, looking at the mule, and Hollis stood with his hands in front of him, looking at the ground, and neither of them seemed in a hurry.

“Lord Tarly asks every lord of the Reach who can spare them for ten spears before the year turns,” said Hollis. He said it as a man says a lesson. “And hay for them, or silver to buy it. He sends me because Ser Denys has had enough of being told no, and because I am no good to anyone at Horn Hill till the rain comes.”

“Ser Denys said no to a lord?” said Alaric.

“Ser Denys was told no by a lord.” Hollis’s eyes lifted. They were small and flat and pale, and he did not seem to find the yard of Grassfield much of a prospect. “At Bitterbridge. He said nothing back. It was the best thing he did on the road.”

Alaric’s mouth moved. “Come into the hall. You will eat.”

They ate in the small hall, at the high table, with the shutters open on the dust. There were more at it than there had been at breakfast. Ser Bertram Corliss sat at Alaric’s left, lean and grizzled, with his battered gorget bright and his eyes on Hollis as though he were a horse he might be asked to buy. Ser Lucan Marwick, tall and square, with cropped dark hair and a face like a shut door, sat across from him and had so far said nothing to the Tarly man except the one word, “Sergeant.” Ser Tobin Whitlow had come late, with his golden curls on end and a smear of something on his very fine boots, and sat at the bottom of the table beside the salt. Lady Jeyne sat at her husband’s right. She had not been asked, and had not been told to go.

Orryn stood behind his chair and was not asked to leave either.

Hollis told it plainly, as he had told his message. It was a steading at the foot of the hills, two days’ ride south of Horn Hill, in a dry gully where the stream had failed: a farmer and his wife and his brother, a score of cattle, a barn. The riders had come at night. Hollis had not seen them; he had come to it the morning after, with a dozen men, from the next hold. The barn was a black frame. The woman was dead, and the brother, and a boy of ten was missing, and there were hoofprints in the ash going south into the sand, a good many, and most of them shod.

“How many?”

“Forty horses. Maybe fifty.” Hollis spooned a little of his porridge. “Not a hundred.”

“Ser Cleyton said a hundred.”

“Ser Cleyton was not there, my lord, nor was the rider who told him. I have heard it was thirty at Starpike, and sixty at Horn Hill, and a hundred by the time it reached Rowan. It will be five hundred by the time it reaches Highgarden.” His mouth did something short that was not a smile. “It was forty horses. I counted the prints twice.”

“Dornish?”

“I don’t know, my lord.”

“Lord Tarly believes it.”

“Lord Tarly believes it very strongly.” Hollis put down the spoon. “I will tell you what I think, my lord, and then you may do as you like with it. I have been a sergeant of the Marches for thirty years. Dornishmen who mean to be Dornishmen, in a dry year, ride at dawn and take the horses. They do not burn a barn. These burned a barn. They took the cattle and a boy. They did not take the horses, which a Dornishman would sooner have than his own mother. I think they were hungry, and a long way from home, and had no banner because they had none to fly.” He shrugged. “But Lord Tarly is a marcher born, and he has not slept right since his steading went up. I would not say it to him.”

“You say it to me.”

“You asked.”

Ser Bertram made a sound. It was not quite a laugh, and Orryn was surprised to hear it. “Ten spears,” he said. “Ten spears, and hay. Where is the hay?”

“There is none,” said Lady Jeyne.

Everyone looked at her. She had not raised her voice. She sat quite straight, with her hands folded on the swell of her belly, and the pinned look of courtesy she wore for the Caswells had gone, and what was under it was something much plainer.

“A levy eats,” she said. “My father said so at his own table. Ten men on horses, going to the Marches and back, will eat what ten of our households would eat in a month, and drink what ten wells would give. We sent Lord Osmund Leygood eighteen sacks to be let out in the open. I do not say that was wrong. I say that we have not got it twice.”

“Lord Tarly does not ask you for twice,” said Hollis, politely. “He asks you for ten spears.”

“He asks Lord Leo’s vassal for Lord Leo’s men,” said Ser Lucan Marwick.

He said it flatly. Orryn had learned that when Ser Lucan spoke in that voice the room went still, because he was the only man in Grassfield who thought rules were a kind of weather. He did not look at the Tarly man. He looked at Alaric.

“If Lord Leo calls the Reach, I will ride, my lord, and so will every man of this house. That is the law. But Lord Tarly is not Lord Leo. He is a marshal. And a marshal who asks a lord’s men from his lord’s own mouth is a marshal who has not been given them.” He folded his hands. “It is the second time this moon that a Reach lord has sent to Meadows for what Highgarden should send for. I do not think it is a thing to encourage.”

“A Stormlander would send the spears,” he added, “and ask afterward.” He did not look at Orryn, who was the only Stormlander in the room, and Orryn felt his face go hot and kept his eyes on the back of Alaric’s chair.

Alaric had been listening with his chin on his fist. He did not seem angry. He seemed, instead, to be enjoying something, and Orryn thought it was the first time in a fortnight.

“Ser Lucan is right,” he said. “Which is a thing I say seldom, so you may all enjoy it.” Ser Bertram’s mouth twitched. “I will not send spears to Lord Tarly. Not because I doubt him, Sergeant. Because I owe him nothing and I owe Lord Leo a great deal, and a lord who sends his men to the nearest voice has no right to complain when the next voice is louder.” He glanced at Hollis. “That is not a refusal. It is an order of precedence.”

“It sounds like a refusal, my lord.”

“It sounds like one. I will write to Highgarden today. I will ask Lord Leo, by his own hand, whether he calls the Reach, and if so, what he wants of Meadows. When he answers I shall send Ser Bertram, and a dozen men and the horses to carry them, and what hay we can find, and I shall send it as Lord Leo’s man and not as Lord Tarly’s.” He turned his head. “Until then Ser Bertram will have six riders in the yard, saddled and ready, and the stable will find the oats. If Lord Leo does not call, I shall know it by the end of the moon, and Lord Tarly may do what he likes with a Highgarden that will not.”

“And if the next steading burns before the end of the moon?” said Hollis.

“Then it burns.” Alaric’s voice did not change. “And I shall have to live with having waited for a letter. That is a lord’s work. It is not a pleasant one.”

Ser Bertram had not moved. He was not a man who smiled, and he did not smile now; but something went across his scarred face that Orryn had not seen before, and when he spoke it was in a voice that was almost mild.

“Six riders, my lord. I will have them by dusk.”

“Good.” Alaric looked at the bottom of the table. “Ser Tobin.”

“My lord?”

“You will go with him.”

Ser Tobin opened his mouth. He looked at his boots. He closed it again.

“Gladly, my lord,” he said, in the voice of a man who has been sentenced.

Lady Jeyne had not taken her eyes from her husband. She said nothing; but when the meeting broke she rose with her hand on the table, and looked at Hollis, and said, quite gently, “Sergeant. You will want a bed for your mule.” It was the first thing she had said to him that was not a question, and for the first time he looked at her.

“Thank you, my lady.”

“And the boy,” she said. “The one who was missing.”

“Aye, my lady?”

“Did they find him?”

“I don’t know, my lady.” For a moment his flat pale face was something else. “I did not ask. I had a message.”

*

There was a Stormlands knight at the gate by sundown, and he asked for Orryn by name.

He had come three days up from the south on a bay mare, and he had the look of a man who has not been out of a saddle for a week. He was forty, brown and weathered, clean-shaven but for a week of stubble, in a plain coat with the arms of House Penrose of Parchments on the breast, which Orryn knew from his cousin’s wedding. He gave his name as Ser Anselm Fell, a household knight of Lord Ronnel Penrose, and held out a bundle tied with a thong.

“From Lady Elenei,” he said. “She says she is owed a letter.”

Orryn’s face grew hot. She was owed three.

“And I am to tell you,” Ser Anselm went on, “that the dun you ride is a Parchments horse, and that Lord Ronnel’s master of horse wishes to know if he is fed.” He glanced at Rook, standing with his head over the stable door. A faint smile crossed the brown face. “I see he is.”

“He is well, ser.”

“Good.” Ser Anselm swung down. He moved stiffly. “And there is something else you may carry to Lord Alaric, if he will hear it, since I have a letter for him that he will not like to read at supper. Lord Dondarrion has ridden out of Blackhaven with sixty horse. Lord Caron has sent forty from Nightsong. They are going south to the Red Mountains, by the Boneway, and they do not say why.” He took off his gauntlet. “I do not think they have asked anyone’s leave.”

Orryn stared at him.

“Forty,” he said.

“And sixty. It is not a great force. But it is two lords of the Stormlands, who would not usually walk the same road for a bag of gold.” Ser Anselm looked at him, with the first real attention he had given him. “Lord Ronnel said, ‘The Reach has argued for a moon whether Dorne has raided it. Blackhaven has decided to go and see.’”

That night Orryn sat on the edge of his bed with the candle and the bundle, and read Elenei’s letter three times.

It was short. She said that Parchments was drier than she had expected, which was the only unkind thing in it. She said that Ser Addam was kind and slow and loved her, and that his mother, Lady Alysanne, was a more tolerable mistress than she had feared. She said that she had heard Orryn had been to Highgarden, and wished to know whether he had been rude to anyone important, and whether the Tyrells were as splendid as they were said to be. She signed it with only her name, and under it, in a smaller hand, as though she had added it after thinking better of it: Do write. Alyssa says you will not. Prove her wrong.

He did not know why he wanted to cry. He folded the letter very small and put it in his boot.

*

“A hundred Dornishmen,” said Perkin Fenwick, the next morning, at the washstand. “With banners, and burning barns, and the whole Marches in flames.”

“Forty,” said Orryn.

“A hundred.”

“Forty. Sergeant Hollis counted the hoofprints.”

“Hollis. He has two fingers short, and he is sixty. He can’t count to ten.” Perkin spat into the basin and grinned at himself in the bit of mirror. He was fifteen, wiry and freckled, with a gap in his front teeth, and he had been at it since waking. “I heard Ser Tobin in the passage. ‘Gladly, my lord.’ Like a man being given a wife.”

“You were not in that room.”

“Ser Tobin has a mouth.” He splashed his face. “It’s true, isn’t it? Six riders. Bertram’s taking them.”

Orryn knew exactly what was wrong with Perkin, which was that he could not keep a secret for an hour, and that if Orryn so much as nodded the whole keep would have it by the midday meal. He said, “Ask Ser Bertram,” and Perkin laughed, because nobody asked Ser Bertram anything.

“I would,” said Baldric Haye, from his bed, in the voice of a boy who has been thinking about it for some time. “I would go with him.”

Everyone looked at him. He sat up, with his flat brown hair on end and his square pale face quite serious, and began to lace his boots.

“Six riders,” he said. “Bertram takes six. A squire is a seventh. I have been Bertram’s squire for two years. If there is a levy, and there will be, and I am left at Grassfield counting hay, I shall never have my spurs. A man is not made a knight at a trough.”

“You’re sixteen,” said Osric Dunmere.

“I am sixteen and I can sit a horse and hold a spear.” He got to his feet. “I mean to ask him.”

“And if he says no?”

“Then I’ll ask again.” He did not look at Orryn. “It’s a matter of asking twice.”

Perkin whooped. Piers Fallow, who had been lying face down on his bed with a blanket over his head, put out a hand and groaned.

“If there’s a levy,” he said, into the pillow, “the price of grain will go up. Father’s been saying so since the wedding. Ten spears for a month. A thousand loaves.” He turned over. His broad face was perfectly serious. “He has a barn full of it. I think he is going to be very glad.”

Nobody said anything.

“What?” said Piers.

“Nothing,” said Baldric. He was looking at Orryn, now. He did it without any particular expression, as he always did, adding it up. “Only that a man who buys cheap and sells dear in a dry year must be very pleased with the Dornish.”

“It isn’t the Dornish,” said Piers. “Hollis said so.”

“And the Alder Mill is Haye’s, and your father holds it on lease. I know.” Baldric picked up his belt. “I only say that when the price of grain goes up, so does a miller’s toll, and a sixteenth of more is more than a sixteenth of less. And then somebody will want to know who pays for the men of Eaglegrave to grind free.”

“Don’t start,” said Perkin. “It’s still dark.”

“It is not dark,” said Osric.

“It is dark to me.”

Orryn pulled on his boots. The letter was in the left one, folded very small. He thought: Baldric is going to ask me, and I am going to say nothing, and he knows it. He found that he was angrier at Baldric for knowing it than for asking.

He went out into the yard. The sun was not up. On the far side, under the beech, Ser Bertram Corliss stood with six men and six horses, saddled, in the grey half-light, with his arms folded and his scarred cheek turned to the east. He did not look round, and the men did not speak. Ser Tobin’s grey stood at the end of the line with nobody yet on it, and Orryn told himself that it did not matter at all.

In the stable, at the end of the long row, Elyce’s big bay colt put its head over the door and blew at him, and past it, in the shadows, he saw a small figure sitting on an upturned pail with a bridle across her knees. She did not look up. He thought she had been there since before the dawn. He thought of going to her, and of what he would say, and of what Ser Wyman would see.

He went on to the yard, where Ser Everard was waiting with the practice swords, and the day began.
