A rider left for Longtable at first light. Orryn saw him go from the well, where he had been sent for the squires' water and had stopped, as everyone stopped now, to look into the bucket before he lifted it. The man wore the gold horn on his breast and carried a leather letter-case slung across his back, and the gate-ward told him the road was dry as far as Hob's Cross and not much better after. Lord Alaric came across the yard a moment later with his doublet half buttoned and his hair on end, and stood watching the rider go out under the arch. He said nothing. When the dust had settled he turned and looked at Orryn and the bucket as though neither of them had any business being in his yard at that hour.

"Saddle Rook," he said. "We're taking Ser Fenwick his tally. Bring it. And bring bread, because the cook's boy isn't up and I'm not waiting for him."

They rode out by the brook lane with the sun not yet over the orchard wall. Alaric took the pace he always took when he had something to think about, which was faster than the horses liked, and for the first mile he said nothing at all. Orryn kept Rook a length behind and watched the dun's hooves on the stones. They had split a little more at the heel since the dry began, and Piers had shown him how to pare them back and had said it would not help for long.

"Lady Odessa sent that rider to Lord Merryweather," Alaric said at last, over his shoulder, as if they had been speaking all along. "About Elyce. She'll have been writing most of the night, which tells you what she thinks of it, because Odessa sleeps. She doesn't write at night for a thing she means to refuse."

"Yes, my lord."

"Don't say yes, my lord. I'm not asking you to agree." He pulled up where the lane widened beside a dry ditch and let the gelding blow, and tore a piece of bread from the loaf Orryn handed across without looking at it. "Do you know why I'm riding to Fenwick with a hay bill that Verrall could have sent by a boy?"

Orryn thought it was the sort of question to which any answer would be wrong. "Because Ser Fenwick wants it signed?"

"Ser Fenwick wants it signed and has wanted it signed for two days, and I'll let him have it. That isn't why." Alaric chewed. "Fenwick was with Daemon. Not in the field. He went to Daemon's banner at the start, with his own men and his own money, and he was home again before the end, and he came to Lord Leo and knelt and paid what he was told to pay. That's all it was. It was eleven years ago, and Lord Leo forgave a good many men who came to him with their hands full. He forgave them over dinner. He didn't have it written."

"Ser Garth would?"

"Ser Garth has it written already. He's a careful man. Do you know what a roll of service is, when it's done properly? It's a book with a column for every house and a line for every knight, and beside the line what he owes and what he gave, and where he stood." He broke another piece of bread. "A clerk who has read that book can do things with it. He can say that a man's service was late. He can say there's a debt left over. He can say that a fine paid in silver was never matched by a muster of men. He doesn't have to say it to the man. He says it to whoever is lord of Highgarden when the time comes, and the man finds he has been moved down a page."

They rode on. The sun was up now and flat across the stubble, and Orryn felt it on the left side of his face.

"Moryn doesn't care about Fenwick," Alaric said. "You understand that. He wouldn't know him in a hall. Moryn wants forty knights who like him better than they like a clerk, and he'll be kind to every one of them for as long as it costs him nothing. That's what Lady Ashford was telling me, in a great many words, and every word of it was true. It doesn't help me. Whichever of them sits in that chair, I hold from him. If it's Garth I'll answer to his clerks. If it's Moryn I'll answer to his friends." He tore the heel from the loaf and gave it back. "And I'd like to know, if you've got an opinion, what you made of Ser Androw."

It was said so lightly that Orryn almost answered it before he understood that he had been asked. He thought of the yard and the long arms, and Ser Androw saying that it taught patience if it taught nothing else.

"I liked him, my lord."

"So did I," said Alaric. "That's the trouble with him." He picked up his reins. "When the Ashfords ride out, you and Verrall go down to the chest. The day after, not the day they leave, because Odessa will find a reason to stay an extra night. I want to know what it says before any clerk writes to ask me. Don't make a ceremony of it, and don't tell Dunstan."

"Yes, my lord."

*

Ser Fenwick's hold stood a little off the road behind a hedge of thorn that had once been clipped and was now left to go as it liked. It was a long low hall of timber on a stone footing, with a stable block across a yard of beaten earth, and Orryn had seen it only from the road until the day before yesterday, when he had come with Verrall's note. The thatch on one stable roof was still new and pale. The patched manger had been dragged into the sun to dry, and a woman in a stained apron was scrubbing the inside of it with sand.

The Ashford and Merryweather horses stood in six of the stalls, lean, glossy, and entirely at home. A groom in orange was rubbing down the bay with the white mark between its eyes, and he straightened and touched his cap to Lord Alaric and went back to his work. Two Merryweather grooms sat on an upturned trough in the shade of the wall, throwing dice for what looked like buttons.

Ser Fenwick had been warned of the visit by someone, because he came out of the hall before they had dismounted, in a doublet that had been brushed that morning and a belt with the buckle polished. He was Perkin's father, a stooped, weathered man of fifty or so, with sandy hair gone mostly grey and Perkin's freckles faded to a brown mottling on the backs of his hands. He had a way of keeping his weight on one leg. Orryn had not noticed that on the day of the oats, when the man had stood with his thumbs in his belt and talked of stalls. He saw now that it had an old cause, a knee that had been badly set, and he saw that Ser Fenwick knew he was looking.

"My lord. I did not expect you. I would have had the hall swept."

"The hall's fine, Fenwick. I've brought your tally."

"I had hoped you would send it by a boy." He said it too fast, and then, as though he had heard it, added, "That is, I'm honoured. I would not have put you to the road."

"It's on my way," said Alaric, which was not true. "Have you a table?"

The hall was dim and smelled of woodsmoke and wet dog. A long board stood under the one window, with a bench along each side and a single carved chair at the head, its arms worn pale by a good many elbows. Ser Fenwick sat on the bench, not in the chair, and Orryn understood that this was courtesy and also that the chair had been sold or broken or had never been comfortable. Alaric laid the tally flat and put a stone on each corner.

"Six horses in your stalls, two pack animals at the tenant's farm, the oats and hay by the day, to be paid from Grassfield. Verrall's hand, and the figures are right. I checked them."

"I do not doubt it."

"Then put your mark to it."

Ser Fenwick took a pen from the shelf, and put it back, and sent a girl who was standing in the doorway to fetch his seal. He read the tally slowly, from the top, moving his lips on the numbers. Orryn stood against the wall and looked at the floor, where a lurcher with a grey muzzle lay with its chin on a threadbare rush mat and looked back at him.

"There is nothing in it about the roof," said Ser Fenwick.

"Because the roof's yours."

"The thatch was done on account of the horses. I did not think the Ashford knight's horses should stand under a leak."

"Then put that in and I'll pay it," said Alaric. "How much?"

Ser Fenwick named a sum. It was small. Orryn thought that it was smaller than the cost of the thatch, and so, he saw, did Alaric, who looked at the man for a moment and then took the pen and wrote it in himself at the foot of the tally in his quick bad hand.

The seal came. It was a ring, and Ser Fenwick pressed it into the wax with the flat of his thumb, hard, as if the pressing mattered more than the picture. The beast on it had been worn so smooth that it might have been a fox or a dog. He wiped the ring on his sleeve.

"There is something else I want to ask you," said Alaric.

"My lord."

"Suppose a letter came from Highgarden. Suppose an officer there wrote to every knight sworn to Meadows and asked what he could send if the lord paramount called, how many men, how armed, and what he'd owed in the past. What would you answer?"

For a long moment Ser Fenwick did not answer. The lurcher's tail thumped once on the mat. When the knight spoke his voice had gone flat in the way voices do when a man has decided to be careful.

"I should send what I could, my lord. As I have always done."

"I'm not asking what you'd say. I'm asking what you'd send."

"Four men, besides myself." He looked at the table. "Four who could stand in a line with a spear and not shame the house, and two lads, one of them Perkin's age and one younger. I have the old harness, which I can't put on by myself. The horses are not mine, this week." It came out of him a little at a time, in short pieces, each one set down separately. "They have my name already at Highgarden, my lord. It's in a book beside the fine. I paid the fine in four years, and I've not been late with a thing since. If they want to know what I owed before that, they have only to read."

"And if they read it and write you down for more than four?"

"Then I should have to find them," said Ser Fenwick.

It was said so plainly that Orryn looked up. He had not heard that voice from the man before, in the yard or at the oats. It was the voice of someone who knew exactly what the sum was.

"I'll have Verrall write a roll for Meadows," said Alaric. "Before anyone asks us for one. Every man down for what he can truly send, and where there's an old debt, down for that too, with what he paid against it. Short, and true, and in our own hand. If Highgarden writes, I'll send them that and they can find the rest of the lie themselves."

"A roll is a roll, my lord. Once it's written, it's owed."

"It's owed now. It just isn't written, and I'd sooner be the one holding the pen." Alaric picked up the tally and blew on the wax. "I'll send Verrall to you before the Ashfords ride. Tell him the truth, and the knee as well."

Ser Fenwick said nothing. Then he said, "Thank you, my lord," and, "I did not think that you—" and stopped, and did not finish it. Orryn found that he was looking very hard at the dog.

*

The girl who had brought the seal was waiting in the yard when they came out, with her arms folded and the lurcher at her heel. She was fourteen or thereabouts, with sandy hair braided tight and coming out of the braid, and a freckled face with a straight direct stare that Orryn recognised, though it took him a moment to place it. It was Perkin's, without the gap in the teeth. Her boots were too large and had been her brother's.

"You're the Grandview boy," she said. "I'm Bethany Fenwick. Perkin writes about you."

"Does he?"

"He writes about everyone. He can't help it. Is he eating?"

"He eats a great deal," said Orryn. "He ate a pear that was rotten."

"That sounds like him." She considered Orryn with her head a little on one side. Behind her in the stable the Ashford groom was singing under his breath. "He wrote that Ser Tobin boxed his ears for ruining his boots, and he never touched them. Did he touch them?"

"No."

"Then who did?"

Orryn opened his mouth. He did not know, afterward, what he would have said. From the hall door Ser Fenwick called, "Bethany," in the tone of long habit, and she rolled her eyes at the sky and went. At the door she looked back once over her shoulder. It was the look of a girl who has been left out of a decision and means to find out later what it was.

Lord Alaric was already mounted. On the road home he rode without speaking, and Orryn, who had expected to be asked something, found that he was not.

Lady Jeyne's solar was on the south side of the keep, over the buttery, and in the heat of the day it was the coolest room in Grassfield after the great hall. It had a south window with its shutters half closed against the glare, and a floor of scrubbed boards, and a long chest under the window where Marya Caswell kept the household's linen lists. When Orryn was sent for, in the middle of the afternoon, he found Lady Jeyne seated by the window with her silks in her lap, and Lady Ashford on the stool across from her with her little book closed on one knee.

"Master Grandison," said Lady Jeyne. "You may sit by the window and hold the yarn."

It was not a request. He sat on the low stool by the window and held out his two hands, and Marya Caswell, who had been waiting for this, put a skein of grey wool over them and began to wind. It was a small, warm, slightly ridiculous task, and he understood within a few turns of the ball that he was not there for the yarn. Lady Jeyne had seen him in the yard with a horse on either side of him. He thought that a squire who had overheard was better kept in a room where she could watch him than left free to carry it to the squires' board, or to Alyn, or to anyone, and that she had arranged it so, with his hands occupied. It was done so civilly that he could not have said it was a punishment, or been certain that it was meant as one.

"You were speaking of Ashford," said Lady Jeyne, to her guest.

"I was about to." Lady Ashford turned her book over. "It will be quicker said plainly, so I shall say it plainly. My lord husband owes money in three places. Ser Garth's clerks hold the names of all three, because Ser Garth's clerks know every debt in the Reach that touches a toll or a bridge. The reason Ashford is fond of Ser Moryn, whose wife is my husband's sister, is not the love of the thing. It is that Ser Moryn does not send clerks."

"That is the whole of the reason?"

"It is the first of them. You will have wondered about the others." She said it without any offence. "I have been fortunate in my husband. He is a generous man and a hospitable one and a bad one to leave alone with a steward. I have been fortunate in my elder son, who counts. I have a younger son who is brave, and a daughter, who is eleven and talks. And I should like to know which of Lord Leo's sons I am feeding in ten years. That is the other reason. It is not a deep one."

"No," said Lady Jeyne. "It is not."

The silks moved in her lap. She was working a small green leaf, with a very fine needle, and she did not look up from it.

"My stepmother is Lady Brenna," she said. "She writes to me twice a year about her son's teeth and the fashions at Highgarden. Last year she wrote that Ser Moryn had a new falcon. I have never had a line from her about anything that he said at his brother's table. You'll understand that I should not show it to a guest if I had."

"I should think the less of you if you did."

"Then we agree about that." Jeyne bit off a thread. "I'll tell you what I think of the other matter, since you've been good enough to be plain. My husband will not be bought with a squire's place. He'll take it as an insult, and he'll turn it over for a month, and then he will do what he thinks best, which may be to say yes and may be to say no and will certainly be something he explains to me afterward. You may have your answer when he is ready or you may not have it at all."

"Ser Moryn can wait a month. He cannot wait a year. Ser Garth has begun to ask for the rolls."

"Alaric knows that. He rode to Fenwick this morning about it." Orryn felt the wool go tight across his wrists and made himself keep his hands still. "He didn't tell me. Marya had it from the groom."

Marya Caswell wound a turn of wool without looking up.

"I would take Matthos," said Lady Jeyne.

She said it in exactly the same voice as the rest, and for a moment nobody in the room moved. Lady Ashford's hand lay flat on her book. Orryn kept his eyes on the skein.

"I would take it, if it were left to me. My son is thirteen and will be lord of Grassfield, which holds from Highgarden, and he cannot learn it in his father's yard for ever. He is to be given to somebody, and I would sooner it were a house that holds him to what he is. I would not give him to the Redwynes' friends and I would not give him to Longtable, which has made itself plain." She turned the leaf over and examined the back. "It is Elyce I will not have bargained. Lord Alaric says what he likes under a willow, and I will say what I like to him in the yard. But that is a separate matter and I should be obliged if it stayed one."

"It shall," said Lady Ashford.

"And I should like it understood that I am not Ser Moryn's. I am Lord Alaric's wife, and I will do what is best for my son."

"I did not suppose that you were anyone's but your son's, Lady Jeyne."

Jeyne looked up at that. She looked at Lady Ashford for a time with the needle held still, and then, for the first time that afternoon, she smiled, and it was a small, tired, completely real smile.

"Then you're the first woman who has said it to my face," she said. "Marya, the yarn."

Marya took the skein from Orryn's hands. He had not noticed that it was finished. His wrists ached with the stillness of it, and he sat for a moment with his empty hands open on his knees, looking at the grey fibres caught in the creases of his palms. Nothing had been decided. Two women had said what they wanted in a room over the buttery, and Lady Jeyne wanted what Lord Alaric did not.

"You may go, Master Grandison," said Lady Jeyne. "Thank you for your help."

*

He was sent to the mews at dusk. Ser Wyman wanted the hoods back on the pegs before the evening feeding and had given the errand to Piers, who had given it to Orryn, because Piers was counting lamp oil for the guests with Verrall and had said so with great dignity. The mews stood against the east wall, a long narrow room with a single window high in the end and a row of perches down the middle. The birds were hooded and still. It smelled of straw and leather and the sharp clean smell of mutes.

He had the door half open when he heard Androw's voice.

"It is not a promise, Alyn. I would not put it as one. It is a place."

"I've been offered places all week."

"Then you know their price." Ser Androw's voice was even and unhurried. "A place in Ser Moryn's household is a knight's place. It is a good one. It is food and a room and a horse and a man to teach you the things your brother would not, and it is Highgarden, which is more than your father's table at Longtable will give a second son. I do not ask for an answer. Your mother has not answered, and I do not wish you to be quicker than she is. I only wish you to know that I said it to your face and not to the back of your head."

"That is more than most people do," said Alyn.

"It is the least a man can do." The two figures at the end of the room did not move. "I will say the same to Lord Alaric's face when it is time. Good evening, Master Grandison. Mind the door, the birds dislike a draught."

He came down the room and went out past Orryn with a nod, quite unhurried, and the door swung to behind him on its leather hinge. Orryn stood with the hoods in his arms and did not know where to look. Alyn was leaning against the end perch with his arms folded and his hair in his eyes, looking at the floor.

"You heard that," he said.

"I heard some of it."

"Of course you did. You hear everything, you and Perkin, only Perkin tells it." But there was no sting in it, and he straightened and held out a hand for a hood. "Give me a few, I'll hang them. Wyman will skin you if the jesses are wrong."

They hung them together, in the half-dark. Tansy shifted once on her perch as they passed, and the small bells chimed.

"My mother told me at supper last night," Alyn said, to the peg. "About Elyce. She said Lord Alaric had spoken, with his hawk-bearer standing behind him, so I suppose that was you. She said it as she says everything, as though she were telling me what the cook had found in the market. She says he said Elyce will be fourteen in two years, and that he'd sooner she married where he could see it. Is that how he put it?"

"Nearly the same."

"That sounds like him." He hung a hood and tugged at it to be sure. "Elyce is twelve. She has a puppy that bit a septon. She told Lady Jeyne that I had been unkind to her when I forgot a dance the other week, and I hadn't, I'd been in the cellar with Perkin. Lady Jeyne said she would not hear a word against me." He laughed, a short breath, and it was not a good laugh. "Everyone is very anxious to dispose of me this week. It's flattering. I'd like to know what I did."

"Nobody has disposed of you."

"Not yet." He took the last hood from Orryn's arm. "Highgarden. Ser Androw is a fair man, I think. He says it to my face. That's more than my brother has ever done, or my father, and it's the first honest offer anyone has made me since I came here, and I'd be a fool to throw it down because of the sort of man who might be standing behind it." He hung the hood and stood looking at it. "I haven't said yes. Don't carry that to the board."

"I won't."

"No. You won't." Alyn glanced at him then, quickly, and away. "That is a great comfort, Grandview, and I'm not sure you know it."

Orryn did not know what to say. He did not know, either, what had happened in his own chest in the last minute, or whether it was only the stillness of the mews and the smell of the birds. He was standing quite close to Alyn. He could see the line of a freckle along his jaw, and the place where his collar had been mended, and the wet hair at his temple that he had pushed back with the heel of his hand. He thought of the dry stream and the stars over the willows, and of an empty bed on a night he had not asked about, and he said nothing.

"Come on," said Alyn. "Verrall will have set the candles wrong again, and Lady Odessa will say something polite about it."

They went out into the dusk together. Across the yard the lamps had been lit in the gatehouse window, in the only glazed window in Grassfield, which was Lady Ashford's. Orryn stood a moment in the mews door and looked at it before he pulled the door shut behind him against the draught.
