The hall of Grassfield announced itself before he reached it: tallow and woodsmoke, wet dog, mutton and barley, and under all of it a thick sweet cidery smell that came from the pears. They lay in baskets along the passage behind the screens, the last windfalls waiting for the press, and every servant who went by with a dish carried a little of the smell on his sleeve.

Orryn had come down the stair at the second bell, or near enough, having stood a while on the landing with his hand on the wall so as to be neither eager nor a fool. The brindled hound there had lifted its grey muzzle at him, considered, and let it sink again. He took that for a hopeful sign. He tugged at the sleeves of the clean shirt, found that one roll had come undone, did it again, and went in.

It was a long room with a roof of black beams, and less than half full. A few household knights and men-at-arms sat at the middle board with their knives out, loud and unbothered, and servants went up and down with trenchers. At the far end the dais stood bare. Two high chairs had been set there with their cushions squared, and before them lay a white cloth and a salt and nothing else. He wondered whether the lord ate alone tonight, over his letters, and found that he rather hoped so.

By the screens, where the door let in the draught, stood a short table, and behind its benches five boys were standing shoulder to shoulder, very quiet, in the manner of boys who have been told to wait. They turned all together as he came in, like cattle at a gate. He understood that they had been waiting for him, and that these were the five he would share a long room with, and a table, and the winter.

"Grandview," said one of them. The long lazy vowels of the Reach made the word seem to take the whole afternoon to arrive. "There he is. Come in, come in, you let the cold in, and we've been made to stand about like septons."

He was tall, eighteen or near it, and handsome in a rumpled way, as if he had been made with great care and then slept in: unruly chestnut hair, a wide mouth, green eyes that went over Orryn from boots to crown and seemed content with the whole of it. His blue tunic was good wool, with the lacing half undone at the neck.

"Alyn," said Osric Dunmere, the senior squire, from the head of the table. He stood with his hands folded and the little star at his throat, and he did not raise his voice. "The grace."

"Osric says it," the tall one told Orryn cheerfully, as a man explains the weather. "He does it better than the septon, and it takes longer."

It did take longer. Osric named the Seven one by one, and what was asked of each, in a clear flat voice, like a man reading out a tally, and the other boys bowed their heads and shifted their feet. Orryn's stomach, which had had nothing since a heel of bread at the lord's desk, spoke up in the middle of the Smith. The wiry boy beside him made a noise into his sleeve that might have been a cough.

Then they sat, and Orryn found that the foot of the table was the place left for him, on the bench nearest the door, with the draught at his neck and a trencher already waiting. Mutton and barley, black bread with a good crust, a crock of butter, a dish of pears stewed in cider, which he suspected would be the pudding of every night at Grassfield. The smell nearly undid him. He took his bread, and made himself tear it, and not cram it.

"I'm Perkin," said the wiry boy, as if they had met before and he were reminding him. He was fifteen, short and quick, with sandy hair that stood up at the crown whatever had been done to it and freckles that ran together across his nose like spilled pepper. When he grinned there was a gap where a tooth had been. "Fenwick. My father's a knight up the Vale. He has a keep, but it's a small one, you could lose it in a good barn, and he does lose things in it, mostly pigs. That's Lem's shirt."

Orryn looked down at himself. "It is?"

"It was. Lem got tall and went home to be a knight. Mostly he got tall." Perkin lowered his voice, which made no difference at all; the whole table could hear him. "Don't tell Osric I said that. He'll tell the septon, and the septon will tell Ser Everard, and Ser Everard, who is master-at-arms, will put me on the quintain till I'm dead."

"I hear what is said at table," said Osric, not looking up from his bread. "It is a good deal more than I should wish."

Alyn laughed, and Perkin laughed after him, and Orryn, who had been about to, was left with his laugh unspent.

"Merryweather," the tall one said to him across the board, and lifted a flask from under the bench, red, stoppered with a rag. "Alyn. Second son of Longtable, which is a table, and long, and that's all anyone needs to know of it. My brother Ser Lymond has the rest and my sister Wylla has the temper. She comes in autumn. You'll want to be standing near a door." He poured, without asking, into the cup at Orryn's elbow, and into his own, and into the cups of those about him as they passed it down. It was a Reach wine, thin and warm and sweet with cloves. Orryn had been given wine at feasts, a splash in a great deal of water. This was not that.

At Alyn's left sat a stocky dark boy who had filled his cup before Alyn's hand was out for it. "Baldric Haye," Alyn said, with a wave of the flask. "Keeps us in order, and tells us what we owe. A poor knight's son from the Reach, and the only one of us with any sense." Baldric nodded to Orryn, and his face did not change at all, which Orryn thought was its own kind of answer. He was sixteen, with watchful brown eyes and a tunic that was patched at both elbows and brushed so clean that the patches looked like a choice.

"You're his lordship's cousin," Baldric said. His voice was polite and quiet, and he said it as a man sets down a weight to see what it will balance. "I heard that in the yard. Is it close?"

Orryn told him: Lord Alaric's aunt Lady Ellyn had married his own lord grandfather, so that they were cousins by that road, and Lord Alaric was first cousin to his father, Ser Steffon. He had not meant to give the whole line, but Baldric's questions came one after another, mild and well-placed, and each one asked for the next. How far was Grandview? What did the lord uncle hold, and how many men could he call? And his father, the second son, did he have lands of his own? No. Orryn said it, and felt the thing happen: a lamp turned down a quarter-inch behind the brown eyes. Then Baldric said, "But kin to Grassfield, that's a better thing to be than most," and the lamp came up again, and Orryn thought that he had been weighed on a very fine scale and found, for the present, worth the carrying.

He drank to have something to do with his face. It was warm all the way down.

"That dun in the lower stable," said the boy across from him. "The one you rode in on. Whose?"

He was the fifth, and had not yet been named. Fifteen, stocky, with dark curls and quick brown eyes that had done a sum of Orryn's shirt, his boots and his scratched wrist before he sat. And his doublet was dark green wool slashed with grey silk, finer than anything else at that table, finer than the knights' at the middle board; Orryn had seen its like on Lord Alester only at weddings. "Piers Fallow," he said, before anyone else could say it for him. "My father is Dunstan Fallow, of the mill, and the market, and half the cheese in the Vale. Everyone will tell you about the mill. I'm saving them the labour."

"It is not mine," Orryn said. "It was lent."

"A dun gelding, seven, a good deep chest, sound in the wind." Piers named a figure in silver, to within a few stags, and watched Orryn's face to see if he knew it was right. Orryn did. He had priced horses in the yard at Grandview since he was nine. He kept his face still, and saw Piers see that he had.

"Piers can price anything," Alyn said, comfortably. "Give him a bride and he'll tell you the dowry by the way she curtsies." The table laughed. Piers laughed with it, a beat late, and a flush went up the side of his neck above the silk collar and stayed there.

Orryn ate. The mutton was fat and good, and he had not known how hungry he was until he was past the first mouthful. Around him the talk went on, and he found that he was not required to take part. Perkin told a long story about a ferret and the master-at-arms's boots, which went wrong in the middle and had to be begun again. Alyn put a hand under the table to scratch a hound. Baldric passed things to Alyn that Alyn had not yet asked for. Osric ate in small exact mouthfuls and said nothing at all, and every so often his pale eyes rested on one boy or another for the length of a breath, as if he were marking a line in a book he meant to quote.

"Osric says you told his lordship the whole thing," Perkin said suddenly, through bread. "To his face. What was the whole thing?"

The talk stopped, a little. It was the kind of question a boy asks because he has been wanting to for an hour.

"Leave him," said Alyn. "A boy's entitled to his first supper before he's put to the question." He leaned back, and stretched, and his green eyes came round to Orryn, warm and amused and perfectly steady. "That's what the cup is for."

"Oh," said Perkin, with deep and sincere joy. "He's going to do the cup."

From under the bench Alyn drew a wooden mazer, dark with years and banded in tin, its rim worn pale by a hundred mouths. He filled it from the flask and set it in front of Baldric, who passed it on without being told. It came down the table hand to hand, and Piers took it and passed it with his lips tight, and then Perkin, who put his thumb in it and licked the thumb. The cup reached Orryn warm from five hands, and the wine in it smelled of cloves.

"Custom of the board," said Alyn. "Every new boy gets it. You stand up on the bench, so the hall can see you, and you tell us three things. True ones. One you're proud of. One you're sorry for. And one you've never told a soul. We drink to each. Then you're one of the board, and nobody asks again."

"I do not sanction it," said Osric to the table at large, in the voice of a man making an entry. "I wish that to be remembered. I shall say so to Ser Everard, if I am asked."

"Osric disapproves, so it's a proper custom," said Alyn.

"Mine was the chicken," said Perkin, to Orryn, in confidence, at the top of his voice. "The third one. I told them about the chicken. It was a very sad chicken."

"I made mine up," said Piers, in a flat voice, to his trencher.

"Everyone knows you did," Alyn said. "That's why you pour, Fallow." He did not say it unkindly. He did not seem to say it for any reason at all.

At the middle board a knight laughed at something and thumped the table. The servants were already clearing. Nobody there had turned to look, and Orryn understood that nobody would; this was not the hall's business, only the squires'. The dais stood empty, with its two cushions square. And the wooden mazer was heavier than it looked, and the wine in it trembled very slightly from the shaking of his own hand.

They were all looking at him. Alyn with his wide, kind, waiting face, like a man holding open a door. Perkin eager, almost hopping. Baldric's eyes rested on Orryn's hands, and Piers watched him with his chin up, as at a rival's horse. Osric looked at no one, and touched the star at his throat, once, and was still.

Orryn could not tell whether the kindness was real. He thought, perhaps, that it might be.

What does Orryn do?

1. Stand on the bench and take the cup, and give them three true things, but small and carefully chosen ones, nothing that Perkin can carry to the kitchen or Osric to the septa.

2. Hold the cup and say that a guest's courtesy is for the host to show first: ask Alyn, who knows the custom best, to give his own three before Orryn gives his.

3. Set the cup down, politely, and refuse. Say that he is Lord Alaric's squire before he is anyone's cup-fellow, and that what he says on his first night is not his to give away; offer the board any forfeit it likes instead.

4. Something else of your own.
