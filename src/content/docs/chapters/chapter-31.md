The orchard was settled in eight days, which was quicker than anything at Grassfield had ever been settled, and Orryn did not trust it.

Lord Alaric had gone down to the Pykes' cottage alone on the evening after the lane. What was said there nobody told the squire. But on the third morning Master Verrall entered Col Pyke's name in the orchard book, under wages, and Hugh Tull was told that the old row would come down once the leaves were off, and that Col was to lead the grubbing and the grafting after. Col had nodded to Orryn once across the yard, and not since. Orryn had turned that nod over each night in the dark, the way a boy turns a coin from a country he does not know, and it had not grown any clearer.

On the ninth morning Lord Alaric sent for three of them at once, which was never good. Osric Dunmere came in straight-backed, and Piers Fallow came in after him with his dark curls still wet from the pump, and Orryn came last and stood where he was put, on the lord's left, below the window.

"It is the name-day of Lady Edda Dunmere," said Lord Alaric. He sat with a cup of small ale he had not touched, and the hazel eyes went from one of them to the next. "Ser Marlon has asked, very civilly, that his son be allowed to ride home for the feast, and I have said yes. Dunmere Hall is half a day's ride. Master Fallow's house stands upon the road, and Master Fallow has asked me three times since the harvest whether his son might be spared to him for an afternoon, and I have said yes to that also, because I am tired of being asked. You will all go. You will be back by tomorrow's noon bell."

"My lord is kind," said Osric, who always said the right thing and never seemed to enjoy it. "My mother will be gladdened."

Piers said nothing at all. A muscle moved in his jaw.

"And I, my lord?" said Orryn. "Why must I go?"

"Because they will ask you questions at both tables, and I would rather you learned to answer them where I can ask what you said." It was very nearly a smile. "Master Fallow will inquire after my health and my temper. I am well. I am always well. Say that, and eat what you are given. He lends kindly, Orryn, and he writes down names. You will find that he writes yours."

*

The road ran out of Grassy Vale along the river, past the last of the town's tanneries and over a plank bridge that boomed under the horses, and for the first hour nobody spoke. Osric rode ahead on his tall grey like a man with an errand. Piers rode at the rear, looking at his own saddlebow. Perkin Fenwick would have found a joke in it by now, but Perkin was at Grassfield, and the quiet rode with them like a fourth rider.

The Fallow house stood where the town gave way to water meadows, and it was the only house Orryn had seen in the Grassy Vale with glass in every window. Three storeys, painted shutters, and a stone step so white that someone must scrub it daily. A stout man came out of the door before they had dismounted, with his arms already wide.

"My boy! My Piers!" He had a loud, warm, wheezing voice, and he caught his son by both shoulders and shook him, and then looked past him with his small bright eyes and took in the two others and their horses and their cloaks, and Orryn watched him do it, quick as a clerk running a column. "And Ser Marlon's son, the young ser—"

"Not ser, goodman," said Osric. "Not yet."

"Not yet! Not yet, he says!" The stout man laughed, and the rings on his fingers flashed. There were six of them, Orryn counted, and his coat was a little too fine for a man in his own yard. He was Dunstan Fallow, Piers's father, miller and merchant, whom Lord Alaric did not ask to dinner and, Orryn had gathered, could not do without. He took Orryn's hand in both of his, which were warm and damp. "And this must be young Master Grandison. Lord Alaric's own squire! I've heard such things. Such things! Come in, come in, there is a fowl in the pot."

"Father," said Piers, low. "You are shouting."

"Am I? It is a happy day." But his eyes slid sideways at the street, to see who heard.

There was a silver jug on the board, a carpet on the floor, and three kinds of bread. Mistress Alis Fallow came in from the kitchen last, wiping her hands. She was Piers's mother and Dunstan's wife, a small broad woman with her son's dark curls gone grey at the edges and a plain wool dress under a finer apron, and she did not say much. She put her hand flat against Piers's cheek, quickly, as one tests a loaf, and he let her; it was the only time that day Orryn saw him stand easy in that house. "You are thin," she told him. Then, to Orryn, with no ceremony at all: "Eat, lad. You've the look of one who's been told not to."

Dunstan asked after Lord Alaric's health, and Orryn said that he was well. Dunstan asked, kindly, whether it was true that there had been some trouble in the east orchard, only he had heard—a word, no more—and Orryn felt the whole of the lane come up into his throat, the wasps and the dead tree and Maud Pyke's flat voice saying since spring. He had his mouth open. A boot caught him hard in the shin under the board.

"It's mended, goodman," he said. "Lord Alaric is well."

"Master Fallow, lad. But mended is good." The small eyes rested on him a moment longer. Then a calfskin book came out of the broad coat, and a short pencil, and Dunstan wrote something in it with great gentleness, and put it away again. Orryn did not look at Piers. He could feel the heat coming off him.

"Father," said Piers, "you said the mill."

"The mill is not a place for gentlemen—"

"It is where the money is made, Dunstan," said Mistress Alis, mildly, to the fowl.

*

The mill stood below the house on the brook, a tall wheel and a taller roof, dim and loud and sweet with the smell of flour, which lay on everything like a first snow. A huge man came out of the dust to meet them, grey to the eyelashes, and put a hand the size of a shovel on Piers's head. This was Hob Marsh, Mistress Alis's brother, who ran the stones, and he said "Aye" to Piers, and "Aye" to the lords' sons, and nothing more for a good while, but he took a sack from the hopper and weighed it in one hand and nodded, as if it had told him something. His two sons, Wat and Jory, tall youths with the same pale lashes, were on the grain-floor with a barrow.

Orryn had expected Piers to be ashamed here too. He was not. He went up the ladder two rungs at a time and slapped the hopper and rubbed meal between his fingers, and he took the lever from his uncle's hand without being asked and set the stones, a hair's breadth, with his tongue between his teeth. "Too fine and it heats," he said, over the noise. "Too coarse and the bakers curse you. My uncle can do it with his eyes shut. I can do it with mine open." He laughed, and it was the first real laugh Orryn had heard from him. "Father says a knight need never know how fine to set a stone."

"Do they?" said Orryn. "Need never know?"

"Knights eat bread," said Piers.

Osric stood in the doorway with flour on his boots and his hands behind his back. "It is honest work," he said, and meant it as courtesy, and Orryn saw Piers's face close, as if he had heard the other thing. Neither of them said so. The wheel went on turning. Orryn said nothing either, which seemed to be the day's chief skill.

*

They came to Dunmere Hall as the light went, along a causeway across a long dark lake that the family called the mere. The hall stood at the end of it, moated and compact, with a grey hound seated on dark green above the gate, and a silver seven-pointed star about its neck. A tall grey man stood in the gateway with his hands folded, and Osric swung down and bent his knee to him on the wet stones, and the man laid a hand on his head and said, "Son."

That was Ser Marlon Dunmere, fifty-four, lord of the Hall, Osric's father and Septa Lenora's elder brother: a long, stern, weathered face, a star at his throat like Osric's, and eyes that went over Orryn once and forgot nothing. Orryn gave him the parcel that the septa had pressed into his hands at the stable door, and the cask of Grassfield perry that Lord Alaric had sent for the name-day, and said what he had been told to say. Ser Marlon said a short thing about his lordship's courtesy. Then Lady Edda came out, a brisk plump woman of forty-nine in a blue gown, who kissed Osric on both cheeks and looked at Piers's silk and at Orryn's darned shoulder in the same glance and took them both in with a single word, "Hungry," and drove them like geese to the hall.

At the board Ser Marlon said the grace, and it was long. He thanked the Father for the harvest and the Mother for the household, and Orryn, who had been up since before light, studied the salt-cellar. There was Ser Cedric, the heir, who was twenty-four and broad and quick to laugh, and who had been married two years to Lady Willa, a slim quiet girl of twenty from Holyhall who watched everyone and said little. There was Merel, twelve, all elbows and braids, who had been waiting with visible effort through the whole of the grace.

"Why does your shirt have a darn in it?" she said.

"Merel," said Lady Edda.

"It's only that it's a lord's squire's shirt, and it's darned."

The table seemed to wait. Orryn heard his own voice come out smaller than he meant. "It was given to me, my lady. By a friend."

"A good shirt is a good shirt," said Ser Cedric cheerfully, and Lady Willa looked at Orryn and gave him a small private smile. It was the kindest thing that had happened since the Fallow house. He found that his hands had stopped shaking.

"My sister writes that there has been some trouble in the pear yard at Grassfield," said Ser Marlon, to his goblet.

Osric did not look up from his plate. Orryn thought of the septa at Grassfield with her pen, and then of Osric, and then, quickly, of nothing.

"It's mended, ser."

"Mended." Ser Marlon turned the word over in the way his sister did, and Orryn found that the resemblance was uncomfortable. "That is a good word to carry into a house. See you keep hold of it."

*

They slept in the loft over the armoury, three pallets in a row under the rafters, with the smell of oil and old leather and the mere lapping somewhere beyond the wall. Piers was asleep within a minute, flat on his back and snoring, like a boy who has been holding his breath for a day. Orryn lay awake and listened to the water.

"Orryn," said Osric, very quietly. "Are you awake?"

"Yes."

For a time nothing more came. Then, in the dark, in that precise voice that did not seem to know how to be anything else: "The boys say that whatever I know, my aunt knows. They are right. She asks me, and I answer her. It would be a lie to be silent. I have never told her a thing that was not so." A pause. "I would rather you heard that from me than from Alyn."

"I did hear it from Perkin," said Orryn. "He said you told the septon."

"I do not tell the septon." It sounded almost wounded. The rafters creaked. "I wish to ask you something, and I would not have you carry a tale to do it. My father hopes I shall be knighted. Lord Alaric does not say, and it is not done to ask. I am seventeen. If Lord Alaric speaks of it in your hearing, whether I am to have my spurs this year, or wait, or be sent elsewhere—" He stopped. When he went on his voice was even more careful than before. "I should like to know. Before my father does."

The mere lapped at the foundations. Beside Orryn, Piers turned over and muttered something about a hopper. Orryn lay with his eyes open on the dark, and found, a little to his surprise, that he was not afraid, only careful, as he had been in the lane, with a thing in his hands that would break.

"Orryn?" said Osric.

What does Orryn do?

1. Promise Osric that he will tell him anything he hears, as a friend.

2. Tell Osric plainly, and kindly, that he cannot carry the lord's words to him or to anyone, his aunt included.

3. Say nothing tonight, and think on it, and decide on the ride home.

4. Something else of your own.
