Maester Perrin's roll of arms was eleven feet long, and he would not unroll it on any table where food had ever been set down.

That left the long bench under the window of his chamber, which he wiped first with a wet cloth and then with a dry one while the six squires stood in the doorway in their own sweat, waiting. It was the coolest room Orryn had been in for a week. The walls were three feet thick, the shutters were closed on the sun, and a sheet hung wet across the window-slit and dripped into a pan, so that the whole chamber smelled of vinegar and old vellum and hot stone gone damp. Six boys in a room made for one thin man warmed it again soon enough. Perrin had given each of them a sliver of chalk and a shingle to draw on, and told them to copy what they saw. None of them had begun.

Orryn had been inside twice before, once with a cut hand and once to carry a message, and both times the maester had answered a question he had not asked. Perrin was a stooped, narrow man past fifty, with a long nose, a grey beard clipped to a point, and ink worked into his fingers to the second knuckle. Around his neck hung the chain every maester wore, a dozen metals linked together, one for each branch of learning he had mastered at the Citadel. He touched it when he was about to be correct. He did not hurry, ever. He drew the roll from its leather tube the way another man might have drawn a sword in a hall, and let the first yard of it fall open on the bench, and every shield on it was bright.

"Lady Jeyne has asked," he said, "that you should know the arms of those who will shortly be among us. A squire who cannot tell an Ashford from a Peake at a quarter-mile will pour for the wrong man, or hold the wrong stirrup, and the slight is remembered long after the squire is forgotten. You will attend."

It was Lady Jeyne's asking, but it was the rider from Longtable who had made it urgent. He had come in at the evening meal with dust to his eyebrows and two letters, one for Lady Jeyne and one for Lord Alaric's senior squire, and by dawn every servant in the keep had known that the Merryweathers were coming in a fortnight and not at summer's end. Alyn's mother, Lady Odessa, had no intention of waiting for the summer to end. The roads were dry as bone from Longtable to the Grassy Vale, and the Mander could be forded wherever a goat could stand, and she would never again have so easy a summer to bring her daughter across the country and show her to the neighbours. So the whole keep was to be scrubbed and aired and counted, and the squires, who could do none of those things well, were to be taught to tell one orange banner from another.

Alyn sat on the sill with his boots up and the letter open on his knee. It had been open there since the first drill. Osric stood against the wall with his back very straight. He had been let into the yard that morning, to the shade only, and he still had the look of something kept too long in a cellar. Perkin was picking at the peeling skin of his nose. Piers had edged up to the bench with his hands clasped behind him, as a merchant's son stands before a stranger's goods, and Baldric had put himself at the back, beside the door.

"The guests first," said Perrin. "Master Merryweather will forgive my beginning with his own."

"Master Merryweather forgives everything, Maester. It's the shield that might mind."

The maester let this go by. He laid a finger beside a field of white, bordered all round in gold, and on the white a great gold horn lay on its side with fruit pouring out of its mouth: apples and plums and carrots and onions and a bunch of leeks, and below them, as if at the end of a long fall, a single turnip.

"Merryweather of Longtable," said Perrin. "A horn of plenty, gold on white, and the words are Behold Our Bounty. I painted that border myself in my second year at Grassfield. The white has not yellowed. Lady Odessa wrote to ask which pigment I used, and I did not tell her."

"What's in the horn?" said Perkin.

"Produce."

"I mean what kind."

"My mother says it looks like a market stall someone has sat on," said Alyn from the window. "She's had it done in silk on three gowns and none of the leeks have come out the same."

"Orange," said the maester, ignoring them both, and unrolled another yard.

The sun was white on an orange field, with a chevron beneath it like the roof of a house, and underneath, in small black letters, Our Sun Shines Bright. It looked very sure of itself.

"Ashford," said Perrin. "Of Ashford, in the south-west, a week's ride from here. This is the one you must have by heart before the horses come into the yard, because Lord Ashford's eldest son and heir will carry it, and he will carry it when it has not been asked for. There are two orange fields in the Reach that you may meet on a road. This is one." He moved his hand down the roll to three black castles, small and square, set one above two. "This is the other. Peake of Starpike. Lord Peake is brother to the Lady of Highgarden, and the Ashfords are tied to the Tyrells by a marriage of their own, so you will hear the word rose in every sentence a visitor from either house speaks. But hear this. An orange pennon at a mile is Ashford or Peake. At half a mile, you must know which. One of them will be disappointed if you guess wrong."

"Which one?" said Perkin.

"Both," said Alyn.

Orryn had not met Ser Androw Ashford. He had a shape for him all the same, from three or four things Alyn had said, none of them quite a description. Alyn read the next line of his mother's letter under his breath, and then aloud. "You will have your hair cut, and your boots blacked, and you will stand where I can see you. You will not drink before the first course, nor speak of Lord Alaric as though he were a friend, nor bring any Stormland dog into the hall." He looked up. "I've never brought a dog into any hall. The dog came in on its own."

"Is Lord Ashford's heir like his banner?" Piers asked, careful as always.

"Androw? He's tall. He wears velvet in all weather, and I mean all, he'd wear it in the Dornish desert and complain of the chill." Alyn rolled the letter in one fist. "He's very good at bowing. He'll bow to you as if you were somebody, and you will walk away feeling you've been priced." He shrugged. "He's all right. He only does it to people who can't do him any good, and that's most of us."

Orryn thought about that for longer than Alyn had. A bow that priced you. He had not known a bow could be made to do that.

The maester, having been interrupted twice, took a long time over the next shield, and Orryn used it to look at the others. Osric's lips were moving. When Perrin came to a dark green field with a grey hound on it, sitting watchful, a silver seven-pointed star hung at its collar, Osric said, before he was asked, "Dunmere of Dunmere Hall, Maester," and the hand that had been at his pendant dropped to his side.

"That's a dog with a necklace," said Perkin, very quietly.

"It is a hound, and it is the hound that keeps watch. The star is the Seven's."

"Dunmere," said Perrin, with approval, and Osric's colour came back a little.

They went on. There was a golden rose on green, which everyone knew, and a yellow centaur drawing a bow on white, which Orryn knew better than the rest because it was on the sleeve of the lady whose roof he slept under, Caswell of Bitterbridge, and Perkin called it a horse with a man on it and was told, by three people, that it was the other way about. Then the maester unrolled a yard of green and white, cut into wedges like a pie, and across the wedges a two-headed eagle, its colours reversed in every wedge, with a gold beak and gold talons.

"Graves of Eaglegrave," said Perrin. "You will not need this for the guests."

He went on to something else at once. Orryn did not hear what it was. He had turned his head, he did not know why, toward the back of the room, and Baldric was standing quite still with his hands at his sides. He was looking at the eagle, and then he was not. His mouth had gone shut like a lid. Orryn felt the slate against his ribs, warm and hard and gritty with his own sweat, and thought, who pays, and found he could not have said for whom he was asking.

The door opened behind Baldric and he stepped aside for the girl who came through it.

"Maester," said Lady Leonette. "Mother says I am to learn them too. All of them. She says it will be a disgrace if I greet Lady Odessa with the wrong horn."

Marya Caswell came in behind her, a narrow woman of thirty in a gown that had been turned twice, and stood against the shutter with her hands folded. She was Lady Jeyne's cousin and her companion, and Orryn had learned that she could be called neither a servant nor a friend without being corrected, and that she heard everything. Leonette was fourteen, Lord Alaric's eldest, chestnut-haired and quick, with a green ribbon at her waist and, for once, shoes on her feet. Perrin made a bow he did not make to squires.

"My lady honours my poor roll."

"Show me Longtable, then. Wylla's."

The maester unrolled it back, with ceremony. Leonette bent over the horn of plenty with her hands behind her back, as though it might be hot.

"It's very yellow."

"It is gold, my lady."

"Is that a turnip?"

"It is a turnip," said Alyn, coming off the window-seat. "It's Longtable's finest turnip. They keep it for guests."

"What will she wear?"

"Wylla? Whatever Mother sends, and it will be white and gold, and she will look like a sweetmeat."

"Then I shall wear green."

"Green suits you."

"I know it does." But she had flushed a little, and she looked down at the horn to hide it, and he looked, for no longer than a breath, at Marya Caswell against the shutter, who had not moved. Alyn took his eyes back. "It's the lord's colour," he said. "In this house. Everyone knows that."

"It is nothing of the kind," said Leonette, delighted. "Father hasn't any colours. He wears whatever is clean."

"Then it's clean, and it suits you."

Orryn was holding a sliver of chalk, which he had been given to copy a shield with and had not used. It had broken in his hand. He put the two pieces in his pocket and began, very carefully, to count the shields on the roll, from the horn to the rim of the bench, and had counted eleven before he knew he was doing it.

Marya looked at the ceiling. "Lady Leonette, your lady mother is waiting."

"She is not. She has the seamstress."

"Then the seamstress is waiting."

Leonette went, with a backward look at the roll and none at anyone else. Marya stayed a moment longer, and her gaze moved across the six squires and rested, briefly, on Orryn.

"Master Grandison," she said. "Master Verrall wants you, when the maester has finished with you."

"Me? What for?"

"To count," said Marya, and was gone.

*

Lyman Verrall's chamber was above the buttery, and it was almost as hot as the yard. He was the steward of Grassfield, forty-four years old, medium in height and sleek about the head, with thin sandy hair combed carefully across his scalp, hazel eyes, a quick smile, and cuffs that were always well cut and always inked. He had been kind to Orryn from the first morning. He knew about the five inn nights that should have been one, and had kept it, and Orryn had not decided whether that made him a friend or a man who had been given something to keep.

"Master Orryn! Come and tell me I'm wrong." He did not get up. He had a ledger open under one hand and a sheet of foolscap under the other, and both were covered in the cramped brown scratches of a man who liked to see a problem fully drawn before he solved it. "You counted a wedding. Lord Alaric says so, in his way, which is to say he said nothing and looked at me while he said it. How many chambers does a lady of Longtable require?"

"One, my lord—Master Verrall. And her maid."

"I'm no one's lord. One, and what sort?"

"The best."

"And what is the best?"

"The one over the hall," said Orryn. He knew it from the squires' talk. It was the only guest chamber in the keep with both a hearth and a window that faced the shade, and anyone of rank who had ever stayed at Grassfield had been put in it. "Lady Odessa has the rank. She'd expect it."

"Good. And her daughter?"

"In with Lady Leonette. They're nearly of an age."

"Yes," said Verrall, and his smile went sideways. "Lady Jeyne has proposed that, and Lady Leonette has not yet been told. I should like to be in the next county when she is." He turned the foolscap to him. "Then there is the heir, and his men, and whoever rides with them, and I count four horses more than we have stalls for. Ser Androw Ashford. An adult knight, and next lord of Ashford. Where?"

It was a puzzle of the kind he liked, and Orryn came to it as a horse comes to a fence, with a lift. He could see the keep set out like a ledger. There were twenty beds in the squires' hall and loft, and eleven were in use. Ser Androw was twenty-nine, a knight and his father's heir, but the unoccupied beds presented themselves to Orryn before the man's rank did. "The loft," he said. "There's room. If we put Master Haye and Master Fallow together, the Ashford knight can have the end bed, and his man can sleep on the floor, and it saves the gatehouse chamber for the grooms or whoever—"

He stopped, because Verrall had put his pen down. At the far side of the room, in the window, Marya Caswell had been standing the whole while with a stack of linen over her arm. He had not seen her come in.

"Master Grandison," she said. "He is Lord Ashford's eldest son and heir, already a knight. His father's sister is married to a son of the Lord of Highgarden. If he sleeps in the squires' loft, it will be told at Highgarden before the harvest, and in six versions."

"It's only a bed," said Orryn, and heard how it sounded. "I mean, it's a good bed—"

"It is a bed in a loft with Perkin Fenwick snoring in it."

He thought of the garden chamber at Grandview, which he had found promised three ways and had settled by his sums, and of a lady standing in a doorway looking at a bed that someone had carried in. The memory came up into his face before he had called for it. He had been seen to be wrong about a chamber once, in front of someone who mattered, and she had not forgotten it, and neither had he.

"I didn't think who he was," he said. "I thought what he cost."

"Marya's right," said Verrall, cheerfully, and picked up his pen. "The gatehouse chamber, then. It has the only glazed window in the keep. He'll think it was a compliment, and it costs nothing I wasn't paying already. The grooms go in the long stable with the horses, and the carts in the lower yard, and we shall water the beasts from the pond and tell the Merryweathers we have a well." He wrote for a while. "Four horses over. I shall have to borrow stalls from the Fenwicks."

"Master Verrall." Orryn had not known he was going to speak. "Where is Lord Alaric?"

"Lord Alaric? At the forge, holding horses' feet. He'd be happier at my figures, but there it is." Verrall bent over the foolscap with his quick smile. "Don't tell him I said so."

*

The smithy stood at the bottom of the lower yard, a low black shed open on one side, with the forge at the back glowing in its own dark. After a moon without rain the ground rang like iron under the horses, and the hooves had begun to split. Alaric knelt in the dust in his shirt, with the bay's near hind foot across his thigh, and the smith, a short grey man with arms burned the colour of leather, was fitting a shoe a finger longer than any Orryn had seen. Ser Wyman Latham sat on an upturned bucket with his chin on his hands. He was Master of Horse and Hawks, a lean, quiet knight of forty-two who taught the ring, and who had put his own old ash lance into Orryn's hands and never mentioned it since.

"It's the wall of it that goes," Wyman was saying. "Not the sole. She'll stand all day on a stone and never feel it, but the wall splits in the dry."

"Then we leave the shoe longer behind and drive higher. Hold her."

"I'm holding her. You're the one who keeps moving."

"I'm thinking."

"It's a bad habit," said the smith, without lifting his head, and Alaric laughed, a short sound through his nose, and drove the nail.

Orryn stood at the edge of the shade with his heart going. He had it ready. He had had it ready since the stair of the maester's tower, and in the passage, and in the long hot walk down the yard, and now the whole of it had gone into something that was hardly words. He said, "My lord."

Alaric glanced up. "Did Perrin make you say the vegetables?"

"Yes, my lord."

"He makes everyone say the vegetables. It's the only thing in the roll he's certain of." He bent to the hoof. "Has the heat got into your head, or have you a question?"

"A question."

"Then ask it. I've a clinch to finish."

Ser Wyman had begun, very quietly, to stand. "I'll look at the grey," he said, and went out into the glare with his hands behind him, and Orryn understood that he had been left alone with the lord and a horse and the smith, who had not so much as turned his head.

He took the slate out of his shirt. It was warm as a loaf and the chalk had gone to a ghost of itself, grey streaks on grey, and he stood holding it with the writing toward his own chest. He did not need to read it. "Who was meant to pay for the Graves corn, my lord?"

The hammer went on for two more strokes. Then it stopped.

"Whose corn?"

"The Eaglegrave men's. At the Alder Mill. The lease says they grind free as of old, but it doesn't say who loses the toll while they do. Master Fallow says it's owing to him, and Haye's father says it's not, and I thought—" He had not meant to say I thought. "I've read it. Twice."

"Whose lease?"

"Master Fallow's. Four sheets."

"And who gave it you to read?"

It was the question he had been dreading. It had been the whole shape of his fear down the yard: that it would be asked, and he would have to answer it, and the answer would be a name. He had promised Baldric nothing. He had said I won't promise yet. He looked at the dust between his boots. "Baldric Haye showed it to me," he said. "He couldn't make out the middle of it. I read it with him. But I'm asking, my lord, because I'd ask it anyway now that I've read it. It's mine."

Alaric put the hoof down. He did it slowly, one hand under the fetlock, and the bay stood without being told. He sat back on his heels, in the dust, with a nail between his teeth, and looked not at Orryn but at the smoke going up out of the forge-hole.

"I've known since the dry began," he said, "that there was something in the Graves corn I'd sooner not look at."

He took the nail out. "Verrall brought it me with the toll-rolls. It said a hole, so much. I said leave it till harvest. If I'd read the lease, I'd have been put to deciding it, and I don't like deciding between the man who holds my mill and the knight who holds my oath, and I had two horses lame and a drought." He turned the nail over. "That is the truth of it, and you may think what you like."

Orryn did not know what to think. It was nothing he had been ready for. He had been ready for anger, or the cold voice, or a patient demonstration of how much he did not understand, and he had not been ready for the lord to speak as if to another man in the yard, a little ashamed, and not very.

"Did you seal the award?"

"I carried it out. I was twenty. Graves had been on the wrong side in the rebellion and came out of it with a pardon and no mill, and his men were to grind free for a term, to ease it. A term. Ten years, I think, but I wouldn't swear to it, because I was twenty and ten years was a very long time. Dunstan's clerk wrote the lease afterward, and he wrote it to be signed." He spat a little dust. "As of old. It sounds generous. Nobody argues with generous."

"Then it's only a term. It might be over soon."

"It might. It might not be. I don't remember the day." The smith had begun to hammer again, softly, and was plainly not listening. "I don't keep that sort of thing in my head, Orryn. That's what Verrall is for, and he keeps it very well, and I check him. The award itself is in the chest in the undercroft, the one with three locks. Verrall has one key, Maester Perrin another, and I have the third, which I lost, and then found, in a boot." He rubbed his thumb along the nail's edge. "Go down with Verrall and read what it said. Not the lease. The award. If it takes you more than a sentence to say what's in it, it's a quarrel, and I'll hear it when I have to."

"Today?"

"Tomorrow, after noon. Verrall won't go into the undercroft before noon. He says the cold is bad for his chest." The smith made a noise in his beard. "And don't make a ceremony of it. If you go to that chest as if it were a sept, Verrall will have told Dunstan before you've turned the key."

"Should I tell Baldric?"

"It's his father's mill. Tell him what you like." Alaric took the hoof up again and set it on his knee. "You said it was yours."

"Yes, my lord."

"It was Haye's boy's yesterday."

"It's mine today."

"Hm," said Alaric. It was not a yes. It did not seem to be a no either. He had already looked down at the hoof, and was frowning at the angle of the clinch. "Hold her head. She doesn't like the fire."

Orryn went round and took the bay by the cheek-strap and stood with his hand against the hot, wet neck. The mare shivered under it and was still. The hammer went on. In the white glare of the yard, some way off, Ser Wyman was running a hand down the grey's shoulder. He stayed until the shoe was on, because no one told him to go, and by then the last of the chalk on the slate had rubbed away entirely against his shirt.

*

He told Baldric after supper, at the trough in the lower yard, where the stone was still holding some of the day's heat and the water was a green skin over mud. Orryn had expected it to be difficult. He told it badly, in the wrong order, beginning with the maester's turnip, and Baldric listened with his cap turning in his hands and said nothing until Orryn had said all of it, even the part about the boot.

"He didn't say who pays."

"No."

"He said a term."

"He said ten years, he thought. He said he wouldn't swear."

Baldric looked at the water. "My father has a copy of the grant," he said, slowly, after a time. "A fair one. He's had it all my life. He's never asked to see the first." He turned the cap again. "It never came into his head that there might be a difference."

"I said you'd shown it to me."

"Did you?"

"He asked. I wasn't going to say I'd found it in the road."

Something happened in Baldric's long face. It was gone so quickly that Orryn could not afterwards have said what it was. "No," he said. "I suppose you couldn't." He put the cap on. "Noon. I'll ask Ser Bertram for the time."

"Lord Alaric said not to make a ceremony of it."

"Then I shan't wear my good tunic," said Baldric. It was nearly a joke. He went up the yard toward the hall with his arms stiff and his head down, and halfway there he turned, and Orryn thought he would say something more, and he only lifted one hand.

It was dark when Orryn went up. The squires' hall had been shuttered to keep the cool in, and had kept the heat instead. Perkin was snoring already, on his back with his mouth open. Piers lay with a wet cloth over his face. Somewhere behind him Osric was saying his prayers in an undertone to the Seven, very exactly, with a pause between each face. Orryn took off his boots and lay on the blanket in his shirt and stared up at the beams, and found that the roll had unrolled itself behind his eyes: the horn and the sun, the three black castles, the rose, the hound, the centaur with its bow, and the eagle, with two heads and the colours turned about in every wedge, looking both ways at once.

He had meant to count the things he had to do tomorrow. He fell asleep somewhere between the hound and the eagle, and when he woke once, in the small hours, to the sound of the heat ticking in the roof-timbers, the bed beside his was still made.
