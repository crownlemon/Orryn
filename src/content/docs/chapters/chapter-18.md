"My lord," Orryn said. "Forgive me."

His voice was small, and he was glad of it, since there was less of it to be taken amiss. He kept his eyes on the paper. The wet ink on the figure had begun to dull. "The old knight looked as though he had only just come in, when I saw him. His boots had the dust of the road on them, and his mule was still at the post. I think it may have been one night, not five. I may be wrong."

Nobody moved. Verrall, who had been so sure of himself a moment before, looked suddenly at a point on the wall about a foot above the lord's head, with the studied innocence of a man who has stepped into a room and found a bull in it.

Lord Alaric did not speak. A muscle stood out in his lean cheek and stayed there. His hazel eyes went from Orryn to the paper and back to Orryn, once, and then to the window, where the dark had fully come down on the orchards and nothing remained but the glass and a long thin reflection of the lamp.

It lasted so long that Orryn began to count his own heartbeats, and gave it up at forty.

Then the lord reached across him, took the pen from his fingers, and drew a single clean line through the sum. He wrote a new figure above it, small and precise: four. He blotted it. He set the pen in the stand, and the stand squared against the edge of the desk.

"One night," he said. "Four stags." His voice had returned to its dry, level self. "I took the figure from Wat's account of the day and I did not check it against the page. That is an error of method. It will not be repeated." He looked at no one. "Verrall, you will say nothing of this to anyone. Not to the maester. Not to my wife."

"My lord," said Verrall, with admirable gravity, "I did not hear a thing."

"Master Orryn." Now the eyes came down. They were not unkind, and they were not kind. They were the eyes of a man who had been handed a cup of cold water in a place where he had expected none, and who did not know whether to be grateful or insulted, and had decided, for the present, to be neither. "When you find me in error again, you will tell me as you have told me tonight. Quietly. And in a room with the door shut. Not at table, and not in the yard, and not in front of the other squires. Is that understood?"

"Yes, my lord."

"Good." He picked up the sheet, shook the sand from it, and handed it to Verrall without looking at it. "Have it fair-copied in the morning, and send it by a rider who does not stop to drink. The old man will have my terms and a bed. If he does not come, he does not come." A pause, very slight. "Wat's figures were exact. See that he knows it. I find I cannot say it to him myself."

It was, Orryn thought, probably the nearest thing to a thank-you that the household of Grassfield had seen in a year. He kept his face still.

"Now go and wash," said Lord Alaric. "You smell of horse and pears. There is a clean shirt laid out in the squires' chamber and supper in the hall at the second bell. Do not be late for it, and do not be early. A squire who is early to the table is eager. A squire who is late is a fool. I should prefer to find you somewhere in between."

Orryn bowed. He had been bowing to Alester all his life, a quick duck of the head at the end of the sentence, and he found now that his body did not remember how. It went down slowly and came up again as if it had been wound. Then he turned and went out, and the door closed behind him, and he was alone on the dark landing with his own pulse.

He stood there for some time with his hand on the wall. It was cold stone, damp, mended in two shades. Under his palm he could feel the faint, steady hum of the keep: someone hauling water below, a cart in the yard, a door, a voice. He had spent two days of his life going toward this place, and now he was in it, and it was only a stair.

"You'll be the new one."

He jumped. A young man had come up the stair behind him with a lantern, taking the steps two at a time and not making the least noise. He was seventeen, perhaps, tall and thin, with fair hair cut straight across the brow and pale eyes that took in the torn doublet, the stained shirt and the scratched wrist in a single level look. His tunic was good wool, brushed clean. A small seven-pointed star hung at his throat on a thong, and he touched it once, lightly, as the look finished, the way another boy might have touched a sore tooth. He held the lantern a little to one side, so that it should not shine in Orryn's face.

"Osric Dunmere," he said. "Senior squire. The septa is my aunt, so say nothing of her that you would not say to her. His lordship has sent word that you are to be shown where to wash and where to sleep, which means me. It is a duty like another." His thin mouth made something that was meant for a smile. "You are the cousin. Grandview. The one who came in on a borrowed horse and told him the whole of it to his face. It is being talked of."

Orryn flushed. "It was not— that is not how—"

"Oh, I do not mind. Nobody tells him the whole of anything. It is very refreshing." Osric turned and started up the stair again. "This way. Mind the fourth step, it is loose. I have reported it twice. And the dog sleeps on the landing and bites strangers. He will know you by tomorrow."

The squires' chamber was long and low and smelled of tallow, wet wool, and some sweetish thing that Orryn could not name, like old apples. Six pallets lay along the walls with chests at their feet, and a window at the far end that looked out over the orchards. A long table stood down the middle, scarred with knife-cuts. On the last bed a shirt had been laid out: folded, white, plain, too large. Someone had put a basin beside it, and a pitcher, and a bit of rough linen.

"There is water, but it is cold. The hot comes up after supper, if the cook is in a good humour, which she is not." Osric set the lantern on the table. "I shall leave you the light. Do not stare at the window. It is the quietest thing in the keep, and it will make you think too much." He paused at the door. "His lordship is a just man, you know. He is only exacting. I have never known him ask more of us than he asks of himself."

"Be where you are meant to be, when you are meant to be there," Osric said, not unkindly, and touched the star. "I shall know if you are not. I am bound to say so."

And he went down the stair, with even, unhurried steps, and the sound of them was lost.

Orryn stood alone in the long room. For a time he did nothing at all.

Then he took off his torn doublet and his stained shirt and dropped them on the floor, and bent over the basin, and poured the cold water over his head. It was shockingly cold. It went down his neck and over his shoulders and he gasped aloud, and then he laughed, with his hair dripping and his eyes shut, because it felt so exactly like waking up. He scrubbed his face with the rough linen. He scrubbed his hands, the nails black with road and pear and stable, and the long scratch on his left wrist where the dun's bridle had caught him. The water in the basin turned a muddy grey. He emptied it out of the window into the dark and filled it again, and washed once more, slowly this time, down to the elbows and up to the hairline.

When he was done he stood in front of the narrow window with the linen round his neck and looked at his own faint reflection in the glass. A boy of fourteen looked back: slight, freckled, with wet hair the colour of straw and a narrow chin and eyebrows that did not quite match. He did not look like anyone's squire. He did not look like a man who had sworn a thing and meant it, or ridden a stranger's horse across a border, or told the truth to a lord who did not like to be corrected and been thanked for it, in a manner of speaking. He looked like someone's youngest son.

But he thought of the road. The silent cart, and the rope, and the name that had weighed nothing. The far bank; the dun horse; the slip with a Penrose name on it. The stone with the stag and the rose. The old knight on the bench, with his one good eye. The blanket in the dark. The stool at the lord's desk. He had set out that morning as a boy who was being sent away. He had come in as a boy who had been sent for. He did not know yet whether these were the same thing, but he thought they were not.

Outside, in the dark, the orchards stood in their even rows. A single light burned in a window of the lower yard, and then it went out. Somewhere a bell began to ring, slowly, once, twice, the first of the two that would call him down to supper. He pulled the shirt over his head. It was too large, and smelled of lye and sun. He rolled the sleeves twice at the wrist, and went to see what Grassfield had for him.

End of the road.
