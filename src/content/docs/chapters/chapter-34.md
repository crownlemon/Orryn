Ser Wyman Latham had them out on the ring-field before the heat had properly begun. The grass lay pale and dry along the hedge, and in the lane the first hooves had already raised a fine dust that hung in the still air, and the squires stood about in their shirtsleeves, squinting against a sun that had been white since dawn, and waited to be told what they were.

At the far end of the railed lane stood two posts. From the first hung an iron ring no bigger than a child's fist, and a rider must carry a blunted lance at a canter and take it on the point. The second bore a quintain: a shield on one arm of a swinging beam, a sandbag on the other. Hit the shield squarely and the beam swung round, and the bag came after, and if one was slow one learned how much a bag of sand could weigh.

Ser Wyman was master of horse and hawks, a weathered, lean man of two-and-forty with a close grey-brown beard and hands that went very still whenever they were near an animal. He had taught Orryn to read weariness in a horse's ears. The squires adored him.

Others had come to watch. Ser Tobin Whitlow, thirty and beautifully booted, leaned on the rail with an apple in his hand and let Perkin Fenwick hold his gloves, his hat and presently his cup. Ser Lucan Marwick, tall and square and exact, stood at the far end looking at Osric Dunmere as a man looks at a page of rules. Ser Bertram Corliss had gone out at dawn with men to the east ford, and Baldric Haye, who was his squire, wore the face of a boy who was glad of it.

"Master Grandison," said Ser Wyman. "When you are ready."

He was not ready. Rook broke into a canter beneath him, and the point of the lance wandered about before him like a drunk man's finger. He missed the ring. He missed it again. On the fifth pass the iron touched the point, rang once, and was gone, and he did not know whether he had taken it or been spared. On the seventh he dropped the lance and had to ride back for it in the sight of the whole field. The quintain was worse. He struck the shield fairly, and was pleased for the length of a breath, and then the sandbag came round and took him in the ribs and put him on his back in the dust.

Somebody laughed on the rail. It was Ser Tobin, but gently, as one laughs at weather. Ser Wyman came down the lane on foot and gave him a hand up, and the hand was dry and steady and held a moment longer than it needed to.

"You will never be quick, Master Grandison," he said. "I tell you now because I should hate for you to learn it at a joust. But you stay on, and you are there at the end of the day when the quick ones have gone to their dinners. That is worth more than it sounds. It is not worth a ring." He looked at the heavy, ill-balanced practice lance in Orryn's other hand and shook his head. "Wait."

He came back from the rack with another, long and plain, of dark ash, the grip rewound in new leather over old. "Mine," he said. "From the year I had a farm to ride out of. It is lighter at the point. Do not hold it in your fist like a spoon." And he turned away before Orryn could thank him.

Orryn was still rubbing his side when the hooves came. Alyn Merryweather came down the lane at a hard gallop, the wrong way, laughing, and did not see him until he did, and then swerved. The horse's shoulder brushed Orryn's arm and the wind of it spun him half round. "Grandview!" Alyn cried over his shoulder, quite unrepentant. "Stand like a post and you'll be treated as one!" And at the end of the lane, without slowing, he took the ring clean on his point and held it up, spinning, for the rail to see.

After that Orryn watched, because there was nothing else to do. Piers Fallow rode one of Ser Wyman's greys like a man who had done his sums, exact and safe. Osric took the ring with perfect form nine times out of nine, and Ser Lucan said "Again" nine times, in the same voice, with no more anger than a man shows to a door, and Osric went back to the head of the lane and did it again. Orryn thought of the loft, and the careful voice in the dark saying I have a small purse, and looked away.

*

"Walk them," said Ser Wyman, when the heat stood too high for the horses, and gave Orryn's bridle and Baldric's to the two of them. "Up to the beech and back. Do not let them stand."

So they walked the horses up the hedge-side in what shade the hedge gave, Baldric on the near side and Orryn on the far, the sweat drying white on the flanks. Baldric was sixteen and stocky, in a patched tunic that was very clean, and he looked at things a long while before he spoke of them.

"Hold the lance against your side," he said at last. "Not out in front. Under your arm, and let the horse carry it. The point goes where the horse goes. Your arm only has to keep out of the way."

Orryn tried it, walking, and felt how much less it asked of him. "Thank you. Why do you serve Ser Bertram, and Piers Ser Wyman? Why aren't we all Lord Alaric's?"

It was an idle question, and Baldric did not answer it idly. "The whole hall is under his protection. That's the custom. But a knight takes a squire if he will, and the lord is glad to have it done. You and Alyn are his own. Osric is Ser Lucan's because Ser Lucan wants a boy who need not be told twice. Perkin is Ser Tobin's because Ser Tobin hates to do anything himself." The corner of his mouth moved. "And Ser Bertram took me because he expected his spurs from a better lord than he got, and he knows what it is to wait for what other men are given. He doesn't pity me for it. That is why I like him."

"And Piers?"

"Ser Wyman is paid for Piers." He said it as a plain fact, to the horse's neck. "Master Fallow pays him, each quarter, in coin. The Lathams sold the last of their land before Ser Wyman had his spurs, and he has been in debt since. Everyone in the keep knows it and nobody says it where he can hear. He is kind to Piers. He is kind to everyone. I think that is why it hurts him."

Orryn walked with that a while. He had liked Ser Wyman's hand on his arm, and did not like now to think what it was worth.

"My father has the Alder Mill," said Baldric suddenly. "On paper. The king's award gave it to Haye, and Lord Alaric put his seal to it. But the sluice needed mending and my father had nothing, and Master Fallow mended it, and works it now on a lease for more years than I shall live. We have the name and the paper. Fallow has the mill." He said it without bitterness, as he might have said the date. "I shall be knighted late, if at all. My father cannot buy a destrier. Ser Bertram says a man can be a good household knight on a hired horse. I think he has not asked what it costs to be right."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I only tell you because you count." Baldric looked at him directly, and his eyes were pale brown and entirely steady. "What do you pay for your place, Grandview?"

"A toll. Two stags and three pence, for the rope at Sedgeford. And the horse isn't mine."

"Then you owe the lord already. That is the cheapest creditor you could have, or the dearest. I haven't decided which." He nodded, as a man does at a sum that comes out. "I'll teach you the grip properly and I'll ask nothing today. But my father has a lease he can't read the end of, and neither can I. Some day I may ask you to look at it. You can say no."

"I might," said Orryn slowly, "say yes."

"Then I'll remember that you didn't say it at once." And he said nothing more until the stable yard.

*

It was dusk when Alyn found him, in the long stable, where the day's heat still lay under the thatch, with Rook's near fore in his hands and a rag, and Lady Edda's cloth of honey cakes on the shelf above the manger.

"Grandview." Alyn leaned on the stall-post, rumpled as ever, with straw in his chestnut hair and a flask he did not offer. "I owe you a horse's length of apology. I didn't see you till I did, and you looked so terribly like a post."

"You nearly killed me."

"Nearly is a very good number. Nearly is what keeps a man from the other thing." But it was said easily, and he came into the stall and sat on an upturned bucket as though he had nowhere else in the world to be, and took a honey cake from the cloth without asking. The lantern made his green eyes darker and his wide mouth quicker. Orryn found he was waiting for Alyn to laugh, and when Alyn did, something in his chest turned over, small and quick and unwelcome, like a fish in a shallow. He had felt it once before, on the road, with someone else, and had not been glad then. He looked hard at the hoof.

"My lady sister comes in a fortnight," said Alyn, "with her Ashford. And Lady Jeyne will put you to the names and the arms, and I cannot be trusted with either. So you shall have them from me."

"Your sister Wylla."

"My sister Wylla, aye." For a moment his face was not amused. "She's fifteen, and Ser Androw is twenty-nine and thinks my father a fool. He may be right. My father laughs at every joke a Tyrell makes before the joke is finished. Longtable is a very kind house, Grandview. Everyone in it is somebody's honoured guest."

"It's two days west, by the river."

"Where the Mander comes into the Blueburn, which is why my father is always on the water and never on his own business. The arms are a horn of plenty. Modest, he says, so as not to boast. My lady mother keeps a list of who may sit above whom. I am the second son, which means a good horse and a better name and nothing else."

"And Ashford?"

"Ah." Alyn grinned, and took another cake. "You'll want this, for they will ask. A week south-west. A small castle, three-cornered, a round tower at every corner, orange banners, and a whitewashed town below it all thatch, on a ford of the Cockleswent. The tourney meadow is across the river. Lord Ashford is fifty and florid, with a sun on his chest the size of a hand and a ring on every finger, and cannot refuse an expense. His feasts are the best in the Reach and his creditors are all well fed. The arms are a white sun on a chevron on orange, and the words are Our Sun Shines Bright, which my mother says is all they can afford to light."

Orryn laughed before he could stop.

"They'll tell you they have had a knight in every reign since the Conqueror, and that he came to dinner several times. Ser Androw is the heir, twenty-nine, who runs the whole place and is thanked by nobody. Don't joke with him. Ser Robert's the second, the jouster, whom every squire loves. Their aunt is wed to Ser Moryn Tyrell at Highgarden. That, and Androw being heir, is the whole of why my father said yes."

"And in the war?"

"The war." Alyn turned the flask in his hand. "No one can say where Ashford stood. Ask in the hall and every man will tell you they were with the king. Ask where, and he'll tell you about the feast." He was quiet a moment. "When Ser Androw comes he will say something about Stormlanders in front of you. Don't answer. I shall be there. I shan't do anything, but I shall be there."

"Why tell me this?"

"I'm leaving," said Alyn. It came out quite lightly, as he said everything. "Before another year is out, or perhaps the one after. A senior squire at eighteen is a joke, and I run a hall of boys because it is the one thing I'm good at, and I was the boy on the bench once, and nobody was kind. Ser Everard let them. The cup is a stupid thing. I know it." He looked at Orryn, and the green eyes were quite steady. "I called you Grandview to see if you would bite. You didn't. You said your three things, and the board went quiet, and I have never seen it do that. I couldn't have done it myself. I thought you ought to know that someone saw."

Orryn did not know what to say. He was aware of his own hands on the hoof, and of the warmth in his face that the lantern would not hide. "Thank you," he said.

"Don't. It's only true." Alyn stood and brushed straw from his breeches, and at the doorway looked back, as if he had only just thought of it. "I've eaten all of Lady Edda's cakes, by the by. I always do."

He was gone before Orryn could answer. The cloth on the shelf was empty. It was not hurt that Orryn felt, quite. But sitting in the smell of horse and lantern oil with an old ash lance across his knees, he could not find, anywhere in himself, any wish that Alyn had not come.

What does Orryn do?

1. Go to Ser Wyman, thank him for the lance, and say nothing of what he has heard about the debt.

2. Tell Baldric quietly that he will look at the lease, once he has asked Lord Alaric whether he may.

3. Seek out Osric and ask how Ser Lucan can be borne, to open the way back to what was said in the loft.

4. Something else of your own.
