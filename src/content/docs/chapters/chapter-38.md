Orryn pinched out the candle with wet fingers and stood in the dark, and then took the four sheets over to the window, because he had said he would. The moon had gone round the corner of the tower. It gave him the shape of the lines but not the words, and the clerk had written small. After a minute of squinting he went out to the passage, lit the candle-end again from the lantern that hung by the stair, and carried it back cupped in his hand like a thing that might escape.

"You'll set the floor on fire," said Baldric, to the wall.

"I thought you were asleep."

"I was." Baldric rolled over. In the candlelight his long face looked older than his years, and tired, and he made no move to take the papers back. "You said after the drill."

"I know what I said." Orryn set the candle in the sill, where the stone was cool. "I'd feel like a dog if I went to bed and it was lying there. Sit up. If it's your mill you can hold the light."

"It isn't my mill." But he sat, with the blanket round his shoulders in spite of the heat, and held the candle in its dish between his two big hands, and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

It was not a pleasant document. Orryn had read his father's rent-rolls at ten, because there had been nothing else in the solar on wet days, and he had thought that made him good at this. It did not. The clerk wrote every sentence as if he were paid by the clause. There was an Item for the repairs, an Item for the term, an Item for the toll, a second Item for the toll that began again halfway down with Provided always, and every one of them referred to some other. He read the first sheet twice and could not have said what it meant. On the second he found the term, and said with relief, "Twenty years."

"Twenty-five," said Baldric.

"It says twenty."

"It says xx. And then, three lines on, and five. He wrote it twice, in two places, the second time as if it was an afterthought." Baldric took the sheet, turned it to the light, and gave it back. "You didn't read to the end of the sentence."

Orryn felt his ears go hot, and bent over it again to cover them.

They found Baldric's chalk in the pocket of his breeches. It was the same stub that had marked the odds on the stable wall at noon. Orryn took it, and a square of slate off the sill, and began to copy: what Master Fallow paid, for how long, what he might take back, and from whom. It was slow, because he kept stopping to say aloud what the clerk could have meant, and Baldric kept answering, gravely and quite correctly, in a voice that surprised him with its patience. Between the two of them, over an hour, with the candle guttering and two moths dead in the dish, the lease came apart into three pieces, like a loaf.

The first: Master Fallow had paid for the mending of the Alder Mill, the wheel and the leat and the race. In return he held it for five-and-twenty years, rent free, and took the toll on all corn ground there, until his costs were made good. After that, the toll was to be split between him and the house of Haye, half and half, for the remaining term.

The second: the miller's toll was a sixteenth of the grain, and so was the toll at every mill in Grassy Vale. But there was a clause that said provided always that the men of Eaglegrave shall grind there free as of old, with no more said.

"That's the Graves exemption," said Baldric. He had begun to speak faster. "That's the thing that has my father sleepless. Before the war the mill was Graves's, and Graves's men ground there free. After the king's peace the mill was taken from Graves and awarded to Haye, and Lord Alaric carried out the award, and the clerk wrote the old custom in, because no one would sign without it. And then he never wrote who pays for it."

Orryn looked at the slate. "Pays for the corn the Graves men don't pay on."

"The lease says Master Fallow takes the toll until his costs are made good. If a third of the corn comes from Eaglegrave, and that third pays nothing, he is not made good in twenty-five years. In forty. So Fallow says that the lost toll is owing to him from Haye, a charge on the house, and my father says that's not what it says at all, and had it read to him by a septon, and was told it says what he says." He drew breath. "Fallow's clerk says it says what Fallow says. They've been at it since midsummer."

"And what does it say?"

Baldric looked at the slate for a long time. "I don't think it says either," he said. "I think the clerk forgot. And I think the first man to tell the other that he forgot is the one who loses the mill." He rubbed his face with a big hand. "My father knows it, in his heart, and Master Fallow knows it. Neither wants to say it first. And I'm the only one at home who'd bother reading it twice." He paused. "I'm not clever, Orryn. I'm careful. It's the one thing the poor ones are allowed to be."

It was not said bitterly. It was said as one says that a day is wet. Orryn thought of Ser Wyman's old ash lance, and the Lathams' land sold off acre by acre, and Dunstan Fallow's money paying for a boy's place at Ser Wyman's side, and the sixteenth part of a hundred bushels, and the way Baldric had stood in the door with his speech lost. "Are you to be knighted?" he said. It came out wrong and he knew it, and added quickly, "I mean, is that why—"

"If I'm useful." Baldric laughed once, without much in it. "If the lease comes out right for Haye, and my father's pleased, and Ser Bertram says a word, and I am not too old by then. It's all if. But I would sooner be useful than lucky. Useful, they can't take away."

"Lord Alaric sealed the award," said Orryn slowly. "He carried out the award in the first place. He'd know what was meant. If the clerk forgot, he could say."

"He might." Baldric did not look at him. "I wouldn't ask him. He doesn't like being asked a question he doesn't know the answer to."

"I've seen him like that." Orryn thought of the pond, and the peg that went with a crack, and the lord with a skinned thumb laughing in the mud. "I think he'd like it better than being told it afterwards."

"Then you ask him."

"Me?"

"He took you with him today," said Baldric. "Not me." It did not sound like envy. It was a fact, laid down on the table with the rest. "And if it was you who asked, my father could say that the question came from Lord Alaric's own squire, with no word from Haye. Nobody would have to say first that anyone forgot."

The candle was low. Orryn's eyes ached. Outside in the dark the water went past the bend, a long soft sound like a breath let out, and he found he was thinking of it, and of the arm across his chest in the pool, and he made himself stop. He rubbed the slate clean with his sleeve, and wrote on it, in his poor round hand, the words who pays for the Graves corn, and underlined them twice, and put the chalk in Baldric's palm.

"I'll think on it," he said. "I won't promise yet."

"No," said Baldric. "That's honest." He folded the four sheets small and pushed them under his pillow, and blew the candle out.

*

The dawn drill started in a grey light with the heat already standing in it like a wall. Ser Everard, grey in the face and newly shaved, had them run the ring in their shirts, and nobody complained, since Lord Alaric stood in the gallery door with a heel of bread in his hand and a cup of small beer, watching with the look of a man at a play he has seen. He did not speak to Orryn. He ate his bread. When the drill ended, and the squires went in a ragged line to the trough, he put the cup down on the sill and began to cross the yard toward the stables, alone.

Baldric, in the line, did not look round. The slate was in Orryn's shirt, against his ribs. Orryn's mouth was dry, and his legs felt as if they belonged to a heavier boy.

What does Orryn do?

1. Catch Lord Alaric before he reaches the stables and ask him plainly who was meant to pay for the Graves corn, saying that the question is his own.

2. Tell Baldric he cannot ask for him, and that if he wants an answer he must go to Lord Alaric himself.

3. Say nothing to Lord Alaric. Take the slate to Ser Everard, or Ser Wyman, and ask whether it is a question a squire ought to be asking at all.

4. Something else of your own.
